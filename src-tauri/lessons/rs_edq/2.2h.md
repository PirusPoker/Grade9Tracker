## What it is

This lesson covers **Christianity in Britain** and **the Church in the local community**. You need:

- the results of the **2011 census compared with the 2001 census**, which showed an increase in diverse religious and non-religious beliefs, but **over half** still calling themselves Christian;
- the **2021 census**, in which Christianity was still the **largest** religion but **fewer than half** called themselves Christian;
- how **UK laws, festivals and traditions** are rooted in the Christian tradition, while also celebrating the festivals, beliefs and cultures of **other religious and non-religious** traditions;
- the **role of the Church in the local community**: a **place of worship** and its **social and community functions**;
- the importance of **mission, evangelism and church growth**.

This is part of Question 2 (Practices) of Component 2. **Evangelism** is one of the eight (a) concepts. In June 2025 the 2(d) was "The main role of the church is to be used as a place of worship". The best answers weighed **worship** against **social and community** roles. Answers that just described types of worship scored poorly. Either meaning of "church" (the building or the community of believers) was accepted.

### The census: what it shows

The **census** is a survey of every household in England and Wales every ten years. The religion question is **voluntary**: "What is your religion?". The figures below are for **England and Wales** and are rounded.

| | 2001 | 2011 | 2021 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Christian** | about **72%** | about **59%** | about **46%** |
| **No religion** | about 15% | about 25% | about **37%** |
| **Muslim** | about 3% | about 5% | about **6.5%** |
| **Hindu** | about 1% | about 1.5% | about 1.7% |
| **Sikh, Jewish, Buddhist** | each under 1% | each under 1% | each under 1% |
| Not stated | about 8% | about 7% | about 6% |

**2001 to 2011**:

- The proportion calling themselves Christian fell sharply, but it was still **over half**.
- "**No religion**" rose a lot. Some people wrote in **Humanist** or **Atheist**.
- The other religions (**Islam, Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism, Judaism**) grew or held steady, so Britain became more **religiously diverse**.

**2021**:

- For the first time, **fewer than half** called themselves Christian.
- Christianity was still by far the **largest single religion**. "No religion" was the second-largest answer.
- **Islam** was the second-largest religion, and still growing.

**Reasons suggested for the change**: **secularisation** (religion playing a smaller part in public life); younger generations less likely to be brought up in a church; **immigration**, bringing Muslims, Hindus, Sikhs and also many Christians (e.g. from Poland, Nigeria, Romania); and people being more willing to say they have no religion.

**Limits of the census**: it measures what people **call** themselves, not belief or practice. Many who tick "Christian" never attend church, and some with "no religion" pray or believe in God.

**Exam tip**: say both halves. Christianity is **still the largest religion**, but it is **no longer a majority**.

### A Christian country? Laws, festivals and traditions

Many British institutions are **rooted in Christianity**:

- **The established Church**: the **Church of England** is the state church in England. The **monarch** is its **Supreme Governor**.
- **The coronation** is a Christian service in **Westminster Abbey**, led by the **Archbishop of Canterbury**, in which the monarch is anointed. In 2023, leaders of other faiths also took part.
- **Parliament**: **bishops** of the Church of England sit in the **House of Lords** (the Lords Spiritual), and each day's business in both Houses begins with **prayers**.
- **Law**: many laws reflect Christian (and Jewish) moral teaching, such as the Ten Commandments against **murder**, **theft** and false witness. Witnesses may swear an oath on the **Bible**. They may instead **affirm** without religion, or swear on another holy book.
- **Festivals and holidays**: **Christmas Day**, **Boxing Day**, **Good Friday** and **Easter Monday** are bank holidays; school holidays are built around Christmas and Easter; Sunday is traditionally a day of rest, and Sunday trading is still limited for large shops in England and Wales.
- **Education**: **religious education** is compulsory in state schools. Schools in England must hold daily **collective worship** that is mainly of a broadly Christian character. Many schools are **church schools**, mainly Church of England or Catholic.
- **Culture**: the national anthem ("God Save the King"); church weddings and funerals; a Christian shape to the calendar and to much of Britain's art, music and literature.

**Celebrating other traditions**:

- Britain now also marks the festivals of **other faiths**. **Diwali**, **Eid**, **Vaisakhi** and **Hanukkah** are celebrated publicly in many cities, by councils and in schools, and employers often give time off for religious festivals.
- The **Equality Act 2010** protects **religion or belief**, including **non-belief**, from discrimination. Schools teach about the main world religions and non-religious views such as **Humanism**.
- **Humanists** and **atheists** take part in public debate. Humanist naming ceremonies, weddings and funerals are popular, and faith and belief leaders join in national events such as **Remembrance**.
- **Diverse views**: some Christians want to keep Britain's Christian heritage, for example the established church and Christian collective worship. Others, including some Christians, think a diverse society should give no religion special status. Many argue Britain is now a **multi-faith and secular** society with Christian roots.

### The role of the Church in the local community

**"Church"** has two meanings: the **building**, and the **community of believers** (the body of Christ). Both are credited in the exam.

**1. A place of worship.** The church is first a place where Christians **gather to worship God**: Sunday services, the **Eucharist**, prayer and Bible study. Jesus promised to be present "where two or three gather in my name" (Matthew 18:20), and the first Christians met together for teaching, fellowship, breaking bread and prayer (Acts 2:42). It is also where the **rites of passage** happen: **baptisms**, **confirmations**, **weddings** and **funerals**. Many churches are open in the day for quiet prayer.

**2. Social and community functions.** Christians believe faith must be shown in **action**:

- the Sheep and the Goats: whatever you did for the least, "you did for me" (Matthew 25:40);
- "love your neighbour as yourself" (Mark 12:31);
- the Good Samaritan (Luke 10);
- "faith… not accompanied by action, is dead" (James 2:17).

| Function | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Food and poverty** | **food banks**: many are run from churches, including much of the Trussell network; community cafés and lunch clubs; "**warm spaces**" in winter |
| **Debt and money** | free debt advice through local churches, e.g. **Christians Against Poverty** |
| **Homelessness** | winter night shelters rotating between churches; soup runs |
| **Families and young people** | parent-and-toddler groups; youth clubs; holiday clubs; church schools |
| **Older and lonely people** | lunch clubs, visiting the housebound, befriending |
| **Safety at night** | **Street Pastors**: trained volunteers from local churches who patrol town centres at weekends, helping vulnerable people |
| **Support in crisis** | chaplains in hospitals, prisons and schools; counselling; bereavement support |
| **Community space** | church halls hired for clubs, meetings and polling stations |

**Diverse views on the main role**:

- Some Christians, especially in **liturgical** traditions, see **worship** as the church's first purpose, because loving God is the first commandment (Mark 12:30) and the Eucharist is the "source and summit" of Catholic life. Social work flows from that.
- Others see **service** as equally central: Jesus spent much of his ministry healing and feeding people, and the Salvation Army began among the poor.
- Many say the two cannot be separated: worship sends Christians out to serve, and service is itself a form of worship.

### Mission, evangelism and church growth

**Mission** means being **sent out** by God to share the good news (the **gospel**) and to serve the world. **Evangelism** is **spreading the Christian gospel** in order to bring people to faith in Jesus.

**Why evangelism matters to Christians**:

- **The Great Commission**: the risen Jesus told his disciples to "go and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit", teaching them to obey all he commanded, and promised, "I am with you always, to the very end of the age" (**Matthew 28:19-20**).
- Christians believe salvation comes through Jesus (John 14:6), so sharing the gospel is an act of **love**. Paul asks how people can believe in someone they have never heard of (Romans 10:14).
- Christians should "always be prepared to give an answer" for the hope they have (1 Peter 3:15).
- At **Pentecost**, the Spirit empowered the disciples to preach, and about three thousand were baptised (Acts 2).

**Methods today**:

- **Alpha** courses, which began at a London church and are now run worldwide: a meal, a talk and open discussion about the meaning of life;
- **church planting**: starting new congregations, often in schools, cafés or homes;
- **Fresh Expressions** of church, such as **Messy Church** (crafts, a short celebration and a meal for families) and café churches;
- street evangelism, open-air services, festivals, **online** services and social media;
- **missionaries** working abroad;
- and, perhaps most of all, the **witness** of ordinary Christians: their lives, kindness and service.

**Church growth**:

- **Declining**: overall church attendance in Britain has fallen over many decades, especially in many traditional denominations.
- **Growing**: some churches are growing, especially **Pentecostal** and **Black-majority** churches, many **Evangelical** and newer independent churches, and churches serving migrant communities, such as Polish Catholic, Romanian Orthodox and African congregations.
- **What this means**: Christianity in Britain is becoming more **ethnically diverse** and more **charismatic**. Church growth matters to Christians because they want more people to know God, and because a living church can serve its community.

**Diverse views on evangelism**:

- **Evangelicals** put a strong emphasis on personal conversion.
- **Catholic** teaching calls for a "**new evangelisation**" of countries where faith has faded.
- **Liberal** Christians often prefer **dialogue** and witness through action, and some worry that pressure to convert can show **disrespect** to people of other faiths and none.
- Most agree that evangelism must be **free** and **respectful**, never forced.

::: terms
- **Census**: a survey of every household carried out every ten years, which includes a voluntary question on religion.
- **Secularisation**: the process by which religion loses influence in public life and society.
- **Established Church**: a church officially linked to the state, as the Church of England is in England.
- **Multi-faith society**: a society in which people of many religions and none live side by side.
- **Church**: the building where Christians worship, and the whole community of Christian believers.
- **Food bank**: a place, often run by churches, that gives emergency food parcels to people in need.
- **Street Pastors**: trained church volunteers who patrol town centres at night to care for vulnerable people.
- **Mission**: being sent out by God to share the gospel and serve others.
- **Evangelism**: spreading the Christian gospel in order to bring people to faith in Jesus.
- **Great Commission**: Jesus' command to "go and make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19-20).
- **Church growth**: an increase in the number of churches or worshippers, for example through church planting.
:::

::: example (c) evangelism
Explain the importance of evangelism for Christians. (8)

Evangelism is spreading the gospel so that others can come to faith in Jesus. It is important because the risen Jesus commanded his followers to "go and make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19-20), so Christians see it as obedience to Christ. Christians believe Jesus is "the way and the truth and the life" (John 14:6), so sharing this good news is an act of love, and Paul asks how people can believe in someone they have never heard of (Romans 10:14). At Pentecost the Spirit gave the disciples courage to preach (Acts 2), and Christians believe the same Spirit helps them today. In Britain, where fewer than half now call themselves Christian, many churches use the Alpha course, church plants and Messy Church to reach people. However, Christians differ: Evangelicals stress personal conversion, while many liberal Christians prefer to witness through actions and dialogue, respecting other faiths. All agree evangelism must be free, not forced.

[Band 4, 8 marks: sources explained, methods, diverse views, context.]
:::

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'The main role of the church is to be used as a place of worship.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Many Christians would agree, because the first commandment is to love God with all one's heart (Mark 12:30), and Jesus promised to be present where believers gather in his name (Matthew 18:20). For Catholics, the Eucharist celebrated in church is the centre of their faith, and baptisms, weddings and funerals give the building a sacred role in people's lives. However, others argue that the church's main role is service, since Jesus said that whatever we do for the least of these, we do for him (Matthew 25:40), and "faith without action is dead" (James 2:17). Churches run food banks, debt advice and Street Pastors, often reaching people who would never attend a service. If "church" means the people rather than the building, service may be the more important role. Overall, the strongest view is that worship and service belong together: worship gives Christians the strength and motive to serve.
:::
