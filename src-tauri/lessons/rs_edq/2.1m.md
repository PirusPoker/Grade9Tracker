## What it is

The last part of **Theme 4: Issues of Human Rights** is about **wealth and poverty**. You need: ethical considerations about the **acquisition** (getting) and **use** of wealth, with **Luke 16:19-31** and **Qur'an 2:177**; and the **actions and attitudes** of two religious charities working in 21st-century Britain to **alleviate poverty**: **Christian Aid** and **Islamic Relief**. The concepts **relative poverty**, **absolute poverty** and **social justice** come from this topic.

### Relative and absolute poverty

| | Absolute poverty | Relative poverty |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Lacking the **basic necessities** of life: enough food, clean water, shelter, clothing and basic healthcare. | Being poor **compared with others** in the same society: unable to afford what most people regard as a normal standard of living. |
| Measure | Living below the World Bank's international poverty line of a few US dollars a day. | In the UK, a household income below **60%** of the median (middle) income. |
| Example | A family in a famine area with no reliable food. | A UK family that cannot afford heating, school trips or a computer for homework. |

**Causes of poverty** include unemployment and low wages, debt, illness and disability, family breakdown, war and conflict, natural disasters and climate change, corruption, and unfair trade. Many believers argue that poverty is not only bad luck but often the result of **injustice**, so the answer must include **social justice** (a fair sharing of wealth and opportunity) as well as charity.

### Christian teaching on wealth

**Acquiring wealth**: Christians may earn money by honest work; wealth itself is not evil. But Christians should not gain it by exploiting others, cheating or harmful trades, and many avoid gambling. "The **love of money** is a root of all kinds of evil" (1 Timothy 6:10): the danger is greed, not money as such. "You cannot serve both God and money" (Matthew 6:24).

**Using wealth**: wealth is a gift from God, to be used responsibly (**stewardship**) and shared with those in need.

- **Luke 16:19-31, the rich man and Lazarus**: a rich man lived in luxury while **Lazarus**, a sick beggar, lay at his gate longing for scraps. After death, Lazarus was comforted with Abraham, while the rich man suffered torment. When he begged Abraham to warn his brothers, Abraham replied that they already had Moses and the Prophets to teach them. Christians interpret this as a warning that ignoring the poor on your doorstep has eternal consequences: the rich man was condemned not for being rich but for doing nothing.
- **The rich young man** (Mark 10): Jesus told him to sell his possessions and give to the poor, and said it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter God's kingdom.
- **The sheep and the goats** (Matthew 25:31-46): those who fed the hungry and clothed the naked served Jesus himself.
- **Diversity**: many Christians **tithe** (give a tenth of income to the church and charity). **Catholic Social Teaching** speaks of a "preferential option for the poor". Methodists remember John Wesley's advice to earn, save and give all you can. **Quakers** value simple living. Some Christians in the "**prosperity gospel**" movement teach that wealth can be a sign of God's blessing, a view many other Christians reject.

### Muslim teaching on wealth

**Acquiring wealth**: Muslims may earn wealth by **halal** (permitted) means: honest trade and work are encouraged, and the Prophet was himself a merchant. Wealth gained by **haram** means is forbidden: **riba** (charging or paying interest, 2:275), **gambling** (5:90), selling alcohol or drugs, cheating and exploitation. Islamic banks offer interest-free finance.

**Using wealth**: everything belongs to **Allah**; humans hold wealth as a **trust** (amanah) and will be asked on the Day of Judgement how they earned and spent it. Wealth is a **test** (64:15).

- **Qur'an 2:177**: righteousness is not simply turning your face east or west in prayer; true righteousness is believing in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Book and the prophets, and **giving wealth, despite loving it**, to relatives, **orphans**, the **needy**, the **traveller**, those who ask, and to **free slaves**; establishing prayer and giving **zakah**; keeping promises; and being patient in hardship. Muslims interpret this as meaning that faith must be shown in **generous action**: worship without care for the poor is not true righteousness.
- **Zakah** (one of the Five Pillars): every adult Muslim with savings above a minimum (**nisab**) for a year gives **2.5%** to set groups, including the poor and needy. It purifies wealth and redistributes it.
- **Sadaqah**: voluntary charity at any time, including a smile or a kind act. **Khums**: Shi'a Muslims also give a fifth of their surplus income annually, partly for religious leaders and the needy.
- **Diversity**: Muslims agree on zakah but differ on how much further to go; some Sufi traditions stress simple living, while others see legitimate wealth as a blessing to be shared.

### Christian Aid

**Christian Aid** is the official relief and development agency of many British and Irish churches, founded in **1945** to help refugees after the Second World War. Its slogan is "We believe in life before death".

- **Emergency relief**: food, water, shelter and medical help after disasters and in conflicts.
- **Long-term development**: working through **local partner organisations** in poor countries on farming, clean water, health, education and helping people earn a living, so communities can escape poverty for good.
- **Campaigning for justice**: on unfair **debt**, **tax** dodging by big companies, and **climate** change, which hits the poorest hardest. It was part of campaigns such as Jubilee 2000 and Make Poverty History.
- **Fundraising in Britain**: especially **Christian Aid Week** each May, when churches collect door to door.
- **Attitude**: it helps people of **all faiths and none**, without preaching, and sees poverty as an injustice that must be tackled at its roots. Its work is motivated by **agape** and Jesus' teaching in Matthew 25.

### Islamic Relief

**Islamic Relief** was founded in **Birmingham** in **1984** by a group of Muslims responding to famine in Africa. It has grown into one of the largest Muslim charities in the world.

- **Emergency response**: food, water, shelter and medical care after earthquakes, floods and in war zones.
- **Long-term development**: clean water and sanitation, education, healthcare, and helping people start small businesses, including **interest-free** loans in line with Islamic teaching.
- **Orphan sponsorship**: supporting children's food, schooling and care.
- **Seasonal programmes**: **Ramadan** food packs for poor families and **Qurbani** meat distribution at **Id-ul-Adha**.
- **Campaigning and climate**: speaking up on climate change and for the rights of people affected by conflict.
- **In the UK**: it also helps families in poverty in Britain, for example with food and winter support.
- **Attitude**: it helps people **regardless of race, religion or gender**; donors often give their **zakah** and **sadaqah** through it. Its work reflects 2:177 and the duty of the **ummah** to care for all.

### Short-term and long-term aid

**Short-term (emergency) aid** saves lives immediately but does not solve the causes of poverty and can create dependence. **Long-term (development) aid** helps people become **self-sufficient** ("give a man a fish… teach a man to fish"). Both charities do both, and both campaign, because they see poverty as a matter of **justice**, not only charity.

::: terms
- **Absolute poverty**: lacking the basic necessities of life, such as food, clean water, shelter and healthcare.
- **Relative poverty**: being poor compared with most people in your society, unable to afford a normal standard of living.
- **Social justice**: fairness in society, so that wealth, opportunities and rights are shared fairly.
- **Stewardship**: the duty to look after something, including wealth, responsibly on God's behalf.
- **Tithing**: the Christian practice of giving a tenth of one's income to the church or to charity.
- **Zakah**: the compulsory giving of 2.5% of savings above a minimum each year to those in need; a Pillar of Islam.
- **Sadaqah**: voluntary charity in Islam, which can be given at any time and in any form.
- **Riba**: charging or paying interest on money, forbidden in Islam as exploitation of those in need.
- **Long-term aid**: development work that helps people become self-sufficient and escape poverty for good.
:::

::: example (c) use of wealth in two religions
Explain, from either two religions or two religious traditions, beliefs about the use of wealth. (8)

**Christianity.** Christians believe wealth is a gift from God to be used responsibly and shared. In the parable of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19-31), the rich man is condemned not for his wealth but for ignoring the beggar at his gate, so Christians believe they must notice and help the poor. Jesus warned that it is hard for the rich to enter God's kingdom (Mark 10), and Paul that the love of money is a root of evil. Many Christians tithe, and Catholics speak of a preferential option for the poor, though some in the prosperity gospel movement see wealth as God's blessing.

**Islam.** Muslims believe all wealth belongs to Allah and is held in trust. Qur'an 2:177 teaches that true righteousness includes giving wealth, despite loving it, to orphans, the needy and travellers, as well as paying zakah. Every Muslim with enough savings gives 2.5% each year in zakah, and sadaqah can be given at any time; Shi'a Muslims also pay khums. Wealth must be earned in halal ways, so riba and gambling are forbidden.

[Band 4, 8 marks: two religions, sources interpreted, diversity.]

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'It is wrong for religious believers to be rich.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Some Christians would agree, pointing to Jesus telling the rich young man to sell everything and saying a camel could pass through the eye of a needle more easily than a rich man enter God's kingdom. When others lack food, keeping great wealth seems hard to justify. However, most Christians and Muslims argue that the problem is not wealth but how it is gained and used. The rich man in Luke 16 is condemned for ignoring Lazarus, not for being rich, and the Qur'an praises those who give wealth "despite love for it" (2:177), which assumes believers will have wealth to give. This second view is stronger, because wealth honestly earned can fund zakah, tithes and charities like Islamic Relief and Christian Aid. So being rich is not wrong in itself, but being rich and indifferent to the poor is.
:::

::: watch
- Defining **relative poverty** as "being less poor" or mixing it up with absolute poverty.
- Describing a charity as "helping poor people" without saying **what** it does.
- Saying Christians or Muslims think **wealth** is evil: the problem is greed, haram earning or failing to share.
- Retelling Luke 16 without **interpreting** it: the rich man is condemned for ignoring Lazarus.
- Saying zakah is "giving to charity whenever you want": that is sadaqah. Zakah is **compulsory** and fixed at 2.5%.
:::
