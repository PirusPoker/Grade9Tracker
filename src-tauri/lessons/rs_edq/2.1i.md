## What it is

This lesson covers **crime and punishment** in Theme 3. You need: the **causes of crime**; the four **aims of punishment** (justice, retribution, deterrence, reformation), with **Qur'an 16:90**; the **treatment of criminals** and the work of **prison reformers** and **prison chaplains**; varied conservative and liberal **Christian** responses to the **death penalty** (**Exodus 20:13**, **Matthew 5:38-39, 43-47**); and varied **Muslim** responses, including interpretations of **Shari'ah**.

The death penalty is a sensitive topic on which sincere believers disagree. Present each view as its holders would.

### Causes of crime

| Cause | How it can lead to crime |
|---|---|
| **Poverty** | Stealing to survive or to feed a family. |
| **Upbringing and environment** | Growing up around crime, neglect or abuse; peer pressure and gangs. |
| **Addiction** | Drugs, alcohol or gambling can lead to theft or violence. |
| **Greed** | Wanting more than one needs: fraud, theft, corruption. |
| **Hate** | Prejudice against a group can lead to hate crimes. |
| **Mental illness** | Some crimes are linked to illness that affects judgement. |
| **Opposition to an unjust law** | Breaking a law out of conscience (civil disobedience). |

Christians and Muslims believe humans have **free will** and are responsible for their crimes, but many also say society has a duty to tackle the causes, such as poverty and addiction. Christians speak of **sin**; Muslims speak of giving in to the whispers of Shaytan or to selfish desires.

### The aims of punishment

| Aim | Meaning | Religious link |
|---|---|---|
| **Justice** | Fairness: the punishment is fair to the victim, society and the offender, and puts things right. | Qur'an 16:90: Allah commands **justice** and good conduct, and forbids wrongdoing and oppression. Christians: "Let justice roll down like waters" (Amos 5:24). |
| **Retribution** | The offender gets what they deserve; the punishment fits the crime. It is given by a lawful authority, so it is **not revenge**. | "Eye for eye" (Exodus 21:24) limited punishment to what was fair; Islam has **qisas** (equal retaliation) in the Shari'ah. |
| **Deterrence** | Punishment puts off the offender and others from committing crime. | Some believers support strict penalties as a deterrent; others point to evidence that harsh punishment does not reduce crime. |
| **Reformation** | Helping the offender change and become a law-abiding member of society. | Christians: the parable of the lost son; Jesus forgave sinners. Muslims: **tawbah** (repentance) is always open; Allah is the Most Forgiving. |

Other aims sometimes mentioned are **protection** of society and **reparation** (making amends to the victim).

### Christian attitudes to punishment

Most Christians accept that punishment is needed (Romans 13 teaches respect for lawful authority), but stress **reformation** and **forgiveness**. Jesus taught "do not resist an evil person" and to turn the other cheek (**Matthew 5:38-39**) and to "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (**Matthew 5:43-44**). Many Christians therefore believe punishment should be just but never cruel, and should aim to restore the offender.

### Muslim attitudes to punishment

Muslims believe the **Shari'ah** sets out punishments, so punishment is part of obeying Allah. **Qur'an 16:90** shows the aim is **justice**, along with good conduct (ihsan). Punishments fall into: **hudud** (fixed penalties for certain crimes named in the Qur'an, such as theft), **qisas** (equal retaliation for murder and injury, where the victim's family may choose **forgiveness** or **diyah**, compensation, 2:178) and **ta'zir** (punishments decided by a judge). Hudud require very strict standards of evidence. Allah's mercy and the offender's **repentance** are also central.

### The treatment of criminals

Both religions teach that criminals remain human beings with **dignity**, made by God. Most Christians and Muslims therefore believe prisoners should be treated humanely: no torture, decent conditions, and the chance to learn, work, practise their faith and keep in touch with family. Alternatives such as **community service** and **probation** can aid reformation. Jesus taught that visiting those in prison is like visiting him (Matthew 25:36). The UK's **Human Rights Act 1998** protects prisoners from torture and inhuman treatment.

### Prison reformers

- **Elizabeth Fry** (1780-1845) was a **Quaker** who visited women in **Newgate Prison**, London, and was shocked by the overcrowding and conditions. She set up a school for the children in the prison, organised sewing and Bible reading so women could learn skills, and campaigned for female warders and better treatment, including for women transported on convict ships. Her Quaker belief that there is "**that of God**" in everyone drove her work.
- **John Howard** (18th century) inspected prisons across Britain and Europe and campaigned for cleaner, healthier conditions; the **Howard League for Penal Reform** is named after him.
- Today, many religious groups continue this work, supporting prisoners' families, education and mentoring for ex-offenders.

### Prison chaplains

**Prison chaplains** are religious leaders who work in prisons. In England and Wales there are chaplains of many faiths, including Christian ministers and Muslim **imams**. They:

- lead **worship**: services, Friday prayers (Jumu'ah), festivals such as Christmas and Id;
- give **pastoral care**: listening, support in crises, after bad news or a bereavement;
- help prisoners face their crime, **repent** and change (reformation);
- support prisoners of any faith or none, and help with preparation for release.

For Christians this is **agape** in action and following Matthew 25:36; for Muslims it shows care for the **ummah** and the belief that Allah accepts sincere repentance.

### The death penalty

**Capital punishment** (the death penalty) is execution as punishment for a crime. In Britain it was abolished for murder in **1965** and for all crimes by **1998**. Many countries still use it.

**Arguments for**: retribution for the worst crimes; deterrence; protects society permanently; justice for victims' families. **Against**: innocent people may be executed and cannot be compensated; evidence that it deters is disputed; it removes any chance of reform; it can be applied unequally; many say it is inhumane.

### Christian responses to the death penalty

| Conservative Christian responses | Liberal Christian responses |
|---|---|
| Some conservative Christians, especially in parts of the USA, support it for murder. They cite **Genesis 9:6** (whoever sheds human blood, by humans shall their blood be shed, because humans are made in God's image) and **Romans 13:4** (the authority "does not bear the sword for no reason"). They read **Exodus 20:13** as "You shall not **murder**", which forbids unlawful killing, not lawful execution by the state. | Most churches in Britain oppose it. They read **Exodus 20:13** as a principle protecting all human life. Jesus rejected "eye for eye" (**Matthew 5:38-39**) and taught love for enemies (**Matthew 5:43-47**): God sends sun and rain on good and evil alike, so Christians should love even those who do great wrong. Every person can repent and be reformed, and only God should take life. |

**Diversity of churches**: the **Catholic Church** revised its Catechism in **2018** to teach that the death penalty is "**inadmissible**", because it attacks human dignity and modern prisons can protect society. The **Church of England**, Methodists and **Quakers** oppose it. Many Christians also point to Jesus saving the woman caught in adultery from stoning (John 8).

### Muslim responses to the death penalty

- The **Shari'ah** permits the death penalty for certain crimes. **Qur'an 5:32** says killing a soul is like killing all humanity, "unless for a soul or for corruption in the land", which many scholars read as allowing execution for **murder** and for serious crimes that threaten society (e.g. terrorism or armed robbery). Classical law also set it for some **hudud** offences.
- For murder, **qisas** gives the victim's family the right to ask for the death penalty, but the Qur'an encourages **forgiveness**: the family may accept **diyah** (compensation) instead (2:178). Many Muslims see forgiveness as the better path.
- **Strict conditions**: hudud need very high standards of proof (e.g. confession or several reliable witnesses), and doubt should prevent the penalty, so it was meant to be rare.
- **Varied views today**: some Muslim-majority countries use the death penalty; others rarely or never do. Some Muslims argue it is a God-given law that must be kept; others argue that the strict conditions for a fair trial can rarely be met in modern states, so it should not be used in practice. British Muslims follow UK law, which does not allow it.

::: terms
- **Justice**: fairness; making sure that people are treated fairly and that wrongs are put right.
- **Punishment**: a penalty given by a lawful authority to someone who has broken the law.
- **Retribution**: the aim that offenders get what they deserve, so that the punishment fits the crime.
- **Deterrence**: the aim of putting people off committing crime through fear of punishment.
- **Reformation**: the aim of helping an offender change their behaviour and become law-abiding.
- **Prison chaplain**: a religious leader who provides worship and pastoral care for prisoners and staff.
- **Capital punishment**: the death penalty; execution as a punishment for a serious crime.
- **Shari'ah**: Islamic law, based on the Qur'an and Sunnah, guiding all areas of a Muslim's life.
- **Qisas**: equal retaliation in Shari'ah, where the victim's family may instead forgive or accept diyah.
- **Hudud**: fixed punishments in Shari'ah for crimes named in the Qur'an, needing strict proof.
:::

::: example (b) two aims

**Christianity.** Christians disagree. ::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'Reformation is the most important aim of punishment.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Many liberal Christians would agree, because Jesus taught that people can change: he forgave sinners and told Peter to forgive "seventy-seven times". Reformation also benefits society, since a reformed offender does not reoffend, which seems more useful than simply causing pain. However, Muslims would say justice must come first: Qur'an 16:90 commands justice, and the victim and their family deserve to see a wrong put right. This point is strong, because a punishment that ignored the harm done would be unfair to victims. Yet the two aims need not compete: a just sentence can still include education and support, and Islam teaches that tawbah is always possible. So reformation may be the most hopeful aim, but it is most convincing when combined with justice rather than replacing it.
