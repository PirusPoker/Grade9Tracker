## What it is

This lesson covers Muslim **festivals and commemorations** and how they are kept **in Britain and worldwide**: **Id-ul-Adha** (the festival of sacrifice); **Id-ul-Fitr** (the festival of fast-breaking after Ramadan); **Ashura** (the Shi'a Day of Remembrance); and the **Night of Power** (Laylat al-Qadr): the importance of the **revelation of the Qur'an** and how the Qur'an is **viewed and treated** in Islam.

It is examined in Question 2 (Practices) of Component 3: (a) 2, (b) 5, (c) 8, (d) 15, with **no SPaG**. The June 2025 (d) was "All Muslims, wherever they are, celebrate festivals in the same way"; the mark scheme credited the **shared** practices of the two Ids, the **different** ways Sunni and Shi'a Muslims keep Ashura, how practice in Britain differs from Muslim-majority countries, and cultural traditions. Be ready to discuss **diversity**, not just describe festivals.

### The Islamic calendar

Islamic festivals follow the **lunar** calendar of twelve months, each beginning with the sighting of the **new crescent moon**. A lunar year is about **11 days shorter** than the solar year, so each festival moves through the seasons, falling about 11 days earlier each year. Because months begin with moon sighting, Muslims sometimes celebrate on **different days**: in Britain some communities follow a local sighting, some follow announcements from Saudi Arabia, and some use astronomical calculation, so two mosques in the same town may hold Id on different days.

| Festival | Date | Who | Mood |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Id-ul-Fitr** | 1 Shawwal, after Ramadan | All Muslims | Joyful thanksgiving |
| **Id-ul-Adha** | 10 Dhul-Hijjah, during Hajj | All Muslims | Joyful, remembering sacrifice |
| **Ashura** | 10 Muharram | Shi'a: mourning; Sunni: fasting | Shi'a: grief and remembrance |
| **Night of Power** | One of the last ten nights of Ramadan | All Muslims | Prayer and devotion |

The two Ids are the only two festivals that all Muslims agree were established by the Prophet; the Qur'an itself does not set out how to celebrate them, so there is room for **cultural variety**.

### Id-ul-Fitr: the festival of fast-breaking

**When and why**: on the first day of **Shawwal**, the month after Ramadan, once the new moon is seen. It celebrates the **end of the month of fasting** and gives **thanks to Allah** for the strength to complete it. Many see it as celebrating success in the **Greater Jihad** of Ramadan.

**How it is celebrated**:

1. **Zakat al-fitr** (also called sadaqat al-fitr): a small fixed charity payment for each member of the household, paid **before the Id prayer**, so that the poor can celebrate too.
2. Muslims **wash** (ghusl), put on **new or best clothes**, and often eat something sweet, such as **dates**, before going out, following the Prophet's example.
3. **Id prayer** (Salat al-Id): a special congregational prayer of two rak'ahs in the morning, followed by a **khutbah** (sermon). It is often held in the **open air** or very large spaces so the whole community can pray together; worshippers recite **takbirs** ("Allah is the Greatest") on the way.
4. **Greetings**: "**Eid Mubarak**" (blessed Id); people embrace and **forgive** one another.
5. **Family and community**: visiting relatives and friends, big **meals**, sweet dishes, **gifts** or money for children; some visit the graves of relatives to pray for them.

**In Britain**:

- Id is **not a public holiday**, so Muslims ask for **time off** work or school; many schools authorise absence, and some schools in areas with large Muslim populations close.
- Because the date depends on the moon, families may not know until the night before which day it will be.
- Mosques fill up and hold several prayer sessions; very large outdoor prayers take place in parks, such as **Small Heath Park in Birmingham**, which has hosted one of the largest Id gatherings in Europe.
- Public events such as **Eid in the Square** in London's Trafalgar Square bring Muslims and non-Muslims together.
- Shops sell Id cards and gifts; Muslims often share food with non-Muslim neighbours.

**Worldwide**:

- In Muslim-majority countries Id is a **public holiday**, often of **three days**, with decorated streets and fairs.
- Names and customs vary: **Hari Raya** in Malaysia, where families hold open houses; **Lebaran** in Indonesia, when millions travel home to their villages (mudik); the "**sugar festival**" in Turkey, where children receive sweets; **sheer khurma** (a sweet vermicelli dish) in many South Asian families.

### Id-ul-Adha: the festival of sacrifice

**When and why**: on **10 Dhul-Hijjah**, the day after pilgrims stand at **Arafat** on Hajj (see 2.3f), and it lasts up to **four days**. It is celebrated by **all** Muslims, not only those on Hajj, so the whole **ummah** shares in the pilgrimage.

**Origin**: it remembers **Ibrahim**'s willingness to **sacrifice his son** when Allah commanded him in a dream. Most Muslims believe the son was **Isma'il** (the Qur'an does not name him in the account in Surah 37). Isma'il accepted, and Shaytan tried three times to tempt Ibrahim to disobey, but Ibrahim drove him away. As Ibrahim was about to obey, Allah stopped him and provided a **ram** to sacrifice instead. The story shows complete **submission** and **trust** in Allah, and that Allah does not want human sacrifice.

**How it is celebrated**:

1. **Id prayer** in the morning, with a khutbah, as at Id-ul-Fitr.
2. **Qurbani** (udhiyah): Muslims who can afford it sacrifice an animal (a sheep, goat, cow or camel) that is healthy and of a set age, slaughtered in the halal way while saying Allah's name.
3. The meat is usually divided into **three parts**: one for the **family**, one for **friends and neighbours**, and one for the **poor**.
4. Visiting, meals, new clothes and gifts, as at Id-ul-Fitr.
5. Many who are not on Hajj **fast on the Day of Arafat** the day before.

**Meaning**: **Qur'an 22:37** teaches that neither the meat nor the blood of the animals reaches Allah; what reaches Him is the **devotion** (taqwa) of the believer. So the sacrifice is about obedience, gratitude and sharing with the poor, not about the animal itself.

**In Britain**:

- By law, animals must be slaughtered in a **licensed slaughterhouse**, not at home. Some families arrange qurbani at a local halal farm or abattoir and collect the meat.
- Many British Muslims instead **pay a charity**, such as **Islamic Relief** or **Muslim Aid**, to carry out qurbani **overseas**, where the meat goes to people in poverty who rarely eat meat. This changes the practice but keeps its purpose of sharing with the poor.
- As with Id-ul-Fitr, time off is needed, and it may fall on a different day for different communities.

**Worldwide**:

- A public holiday in Muslim-majority countries; animal markets appear in towns before the festival; families often slaughter at home or through local butchers, and meat is distributed to neighbours and the poor.
- In Pakistan it is often called "Bakra Eid" (goat Id); in Turkey the "festival of sacrifice"; in Saudi Arabia millions of animals are sacrificed for the pilgrims, and much of the meat is processed and sent to poorer countries.

### Ashura: the Day of Remembrance

**When**: **10 Muharram**, the first month of the Islamic year. It means "tenth".

**For Shi'a Muslims, a day of mourning**: Ashura commemorates the **martyrdom of Imam Husayn**, the grandson of the Prophet and son of Ali and Fatimah, at **Karbala** in Iraq in **680 CE**. Husayn refused to give allegiance to the caliph **Yazid**, whom he saw as unjust. With a small group of family and companions (traditionally about **72**), he was surrounded by a much larger army, cut off from the water of the river Euphrates for days, and then killed with almost all the men; the women and children were taken captive. For Shi'a Muslims, Husayn's death is the supreme example of **standing up for justice and truth against oppression**, whatever the cost, and of **sacrifice** for Islam.

**How Shi'a Muslims commemorate Ashura**:

- **Majlis** (gatherings) during the first ten days of Muharram, in mosques, **imambargahs** or **Husayniyyas** and homes: a scholar recounts the events of Karbala and their lessons, and poems of mourning are recited; people **weep** for Husayn.
- **Matam**: rhythmic beating of the chest as a sign of grief and solidarity.
- **Processions** through the streets, with black flags, banners and models of Husayn's shrine.
- **Black clothes**, no weddings or parties during the mourning period.
- **Ta'ziyeh**: passion plays re-enacting the events of Karbala, especially in Iran.
- **Giving water and food** freely to anyone, remembering the thirst of Husayn's family; charity.
- **Ziyarah** to Karbala, and forty days later the **Arba'een** walk by millions (see 2.3g).
- **Self-harm** (cutting or striking oneself until bleeding) is practised by a minority in some countries, but many senior Shi'a scholars **discourage or forbid** it, and many communities encourage **donating blood** instead.

**In Britain**: majlis in Shi'a centres across the country, often with sermons in **English** for young people; **processions** in central London and other cities; **blood donation** drives with the NHS, such as the "Who is Hussain?" campaign, which turn grief into helping others; free food and water handed out to passers-by; schools and employers may be asked for time off.

**For Sunni Muslims**: Ashura is the day on which, according to hadith, Allah saved **Musa** and the Israelites from **Pharaoh**. The Prophet fasted on it and encouraged Muslims to fast, so many Sunni Muslims **fast** on Ashura (often with the day before or after). Sunni Muslims also honour Husayn and regard his death as a tragedy. So Ashura is kept by many Muslims, but in **very different ways**.

**Importance for Shi'a Muslims**: it keeps alive **love for the Ahl al-Bayt** (tawalla) and rejection of injustice (tabarra); it teaches courage, patience and sacrifice; it is a model of **jihad** against oppression; it unites the community and passes on Shi'a identity to the next generation; and weeping for Husayn is believed to bring spiritual reward.

### The Night of Power: Laylat al-Qadr

**What it remembers**: the night in **610 CE** when the angel **Jibril** first appeared to **Muhammad**, aged about forty, while he was meditating in the **cave of Hira** on Mount Nur near Makkah. Jibril commanded him to "**Read**" (or "Recite"), and revealed the first verses of the Qur'an (**Surah 96:1-5**), about Allah who created humans and taught by the pen. Revelation then continued for about **23 years**. **Qur'an 2:185** says Ramadan is the month in which the Qur'an was sent down as **guidance** for people.

**Surah 97** (al-Qadr) teaches that the Qur'an was sent down on the Night of Power; that this night is **better than a thousand months**; that the **angels and the Spirit** (Jibril) descend by Allah's permission; and that it is **peace until dawn**.

**When**: its exact date is **not known**. Muslims seek it in the **last ten nights of Ramadan**, especially the **odd nights**. Many Sunni Muslims focus on the **27th** night; many Shi'a Muslims give special attention to the **19th, 21st and 23rd** nights, linked also to the anniversary of Imam Ali's death.

**How Muslims keep it**:

- **Staying up through the night** in prayer, reciting the Qur'an, making du'a and **asking forgiveness**; a hadith teaches that whoever stands in prayer on this night with faith, hoping for reward, will have past sins forgiven.
- **I'tikaf**: some Muslims, often men, stay in the **mosque** for the last ten days of Ramadan in spiritual retreat; women may do so at home or in the mosque where space allows.
- Many mosques complete the **recitation of the whole Qur'an** in tarawih prayers near this night.
- Extra **charity**, because good deeds on this night are believed to be multiplied.
- In Britain, mosques stay open all night, and Muslims may need to balance the night of worship with school or work the next day; worldwide, the great mosques in Makkah and Madinah are packed.

### The importance of the revelation, and how the Qur'an is viewed and treated

**How the Qur'an is viewed**:

- It is the **literal word of Allah**, revealed through Jibril to Muhammad, not written by the Prophet.
- It is the **final** revelation, correcting and completing the earlier books (see 2.3b), for all people and all time.
- It has been **preserved unchanged** in Arabic; translations are seen as **interpretations of its meaning**, not the Qur'an itself.
- It is **guidance** for every part of life and, with the Sunnah, the main source of **shariah**.
- Its language is considered **miraculous**: Muslims see its beauty as proof that it comes from Allah.

**How the Qur'an is treated**:

- Many Muslims perform **wudu** before touching the Arabic Qur'an (many link this to **Qur'an 56:79**, that only the purified touch it).
- It is kept on a **high shelf**, often **wrapped** in cloth, with nothing placed on top of it; it is never put on the floor or taken into the toilet.
- It is read on a **stand** (a rehal) and recited in **Arabic** with correct pronunciation (**tajwid**); people sit quietly and respectfully when it is recited.
- Many Muslims **memorise** the whole Qur'an and are honoured with the title **hafiz**; children learn to read it at the **madrasah**.
- Worn-out copies are not thrown away but disposed of respectfully, for example by burial.
- It is recited at key moments of life: at birth, marriage, in every salah, in illness and at death.

::: terms
- **Id-ul-Fitr**: the festival of fast-breaking at the end of Ramadan, on 1 Shawwal.
- **Id-ul-Adha**: the festival of sacrifice on 10 Dhul-Hijjah, remembering Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice his son.
- **Zakat al-fitr**: a small fixed charity payment per person, made before the Id-ul-Fitr prayer so the poor can celebrate.
- **Salat al-Id**: the special congregational Id prayer of two rak'ahs, followed by a sermon.
- **Qurbani**: the sacrifice of an animal at Id-ul-Adha, with the meat shared between family, friends and the poor.
- **Ashura**: the 10th of Muharram; for Shi'a Muslims a day of mourning for Imam Husayn's martyrdom at Karbala.
- **Karbala**: the place in Iraq where Imam Husayn was killed in 680 CE, and where his shrine stands.
- **Majlis**: a Shi'a gathering in Muharram where the story of Karbala is told and Husayn is mourned.
- **Matam**: rhythmic chest-beating by Shi'a Muslims as an expression of grief for Husayn.
- **Night of Power**: Laylat al-Qadr, the night the Qur'an was first revealed, better than a thousand months.
- **I'tikaf**: a spiritual retreat in the mosque during the last ten days of Ramadan.
- **Hafiz**: a Muslim who has memorised the whole Qur'an.

::: example (c) Ashura
Explain the importance of Ashura for Shi'a Muslims. (8)

Ashura, on the 10th of Muharram, is a day of mourning for Shi'a Muslims, not a celebration. It remembers the martyrdom of Imam Husayn, the Prophet's grandson, at Karbala in 680 CE. Husayn refused to accept the caliph Yazid, whom he saw as unjust, and with about seventy-two family members and companions was surrounded, denied water and killed. For Shi'a Muslims this is the greatest example of standing up for justice and truth against oppression, whatever the cost.

Because of this, Shi'a Muslims gather in majlis during the first ten days of Muharram to hear the story of Karbala and weep for Husayn, showing tawalla, their love for the Prophet's family. They perform matam, beating their chests in grief, wear black and walk in processions. Remembering the thirst of Husayn's family, they give out free water and food, so grief becomes charity.

In Britain, Shi'a centres hold majlis with sermons in English so young people understand, processions take place in London, and many give blood with the NHS, through campaigns such as "Who is Hussain?", instead of the self-harm that a minority practise elsewhere and that many scholars discourage. Ashura therefore teaches courage and sacrifice, strengthens Shi'a identity, and inspires Shi'a Muslims to stand against injustice in their own lives.

::: example (c) Night of Power
Explain why the Night of Power is important for Muslims. (8)

The Night of Power, Laylat al-Qadr, is the night in 610 CE when Jibril first appeared to Muhammad in the cave of Hira and revealed the first verses of the Qur'an, beginning with the command "Read" (96:1-5). Qur'an 2:185 says Ramadan is the month in which the Qur'an was sent down as guidance, and Surah 97 says this night is better than a thousand months, that angels and the Spirit descend, and that it is peace until dawn.

It is important because it marks the start of the revelation of the Qur'an, which Muslims believe is the literal and final word of Allah, preserved unchanged in Arabic and guiding every part of life. Because a hadith teaches that whoever prays on this night with faith will be forgiven, Muslims seek it in the last ten odd nights of Ramadan, especially the 27th for many Sunnis and the 19th, 21st and 23rd for many Shi'a. They stay up all night praying, reciting the Qur'an and asking forgiveness, and some make i'tikaf in the mosque.

The night also shapes how Muslims treat the Qur'an all year: they perform wudu before touching it, keep it on a high shelf, recite it in Arabic with tajwid, and honour those who memorise it as hafiz.

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'All Muslims, wherever they are, celebrate festivals in the same way.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Some would agree, because every Muslim celebrates the same two Ids, set by the Prophet: all begin with the Id prayer, all give charity (zakat al-fitr or a share of the qurbani meat), and all remember the same events, the end of Ramadan and Ibrahim's obedience, which shows the unity of the ummah. However, this argument is weaker for Ashura: Shi'a Muslims mourn Husayn's martyrdom with majlis, matam and processions, while many Sunni Muslims simply fast, remembering Musa's escape from Pharaoh. Place also matters: in Britain Id is not a public holiday and qurbani must be done in a licensed slaughterhouse, so many pay a charity to sacrifice overseas, unlike families in Pakistan or Turkey. Even moon sighting means Muslims in the same town can celebrate on different days. Therefore Muslims share the same core beliefs and purposes in their festivals, but they do not celebrate them in the same way.
