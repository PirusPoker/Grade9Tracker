## What it is

This part of **Theme 2: Issues of Life and Death** is about **humans' responsibility for the world** and the **value of animal life**. You need: Christian and non-religious beliefs about **dominion, stewardship, environmental responsibility, sustainability and global citizenship** (**Genesis 1:28**, **Psalm 8:6**, **Humanist Climate Action**); Muslim beliefs about **fitra, khalifah**, environmental responsibility, sustainability and global citizenship (**Qur'an 7:54**); and non-religious views on the importance of human and animal life, especially **Peter Singer's** idea of **speciesism**.

Questions often ask **why** believers are responsible for the environment. The answer is a belief (God is the owner, humans are stewards or khalifah, there will be a judgement), not a list of environmental problems.

### Key ideas

- **Environmental sustainability**: using the Earth's resources in a way that meets present needs **without damaging** the ability of future generations to meet theirs (e.g. renewable energy, recycling, protecting forests).
- **Environmental responsibility**: the duty to look after the natural world and not harm it.
- **Global citizenship**: seeing yourself as a member of the whole world community, with duties to people everywhere, including future generations; e.g. buying fairly traded goods, cutting emissions that affect poorer countries.

### Christianity: dominion and stewardship

**Dominion** means having **authority or rule over** something.
- **Genesis 1:28**: God blessed the first humans and told them to "be fruitful and increase", to "fill the earth and subdue it" and to **rule over** the fish, birds and every living creature.
- **Psalm 8:6**: God made humans **rulers** over the works of his hands and put everything "under their feet".

Some Christians in the past read dominion as permission to use nature however humans liked. Most Christians today read it as **responsible rule**: a king in the Bible is meant to care for his people, so ruling creation means caring for it on God's behalf.

**Stewardship** means looking after something that belongs to someone else.
- "The earth is the LORD's, and everything in it" (Psalm 24:1): humans do not own the world, they **manage** it for God.
- God put Adam in Eden "to work it and take care of it" (Genesis 2:15).
- Stewards will be **accountable**: the parable of the talents (Matthew 25:14-30) shows God expects a return on what he entrusts.
- God called creation "very good" (Genesis 1:31), so damaging it disrespects the Creator.

**In practice**
- **Catholic**: Pope Francis's encyclical ***Laudato Si'*** (2015) calls the Earth "our common home", links care for the environment with care for the **poor** (who suffer most from climate change), and calls for an "ecological conversion". This is **global citizenship** in Catholic form.
- **Church of England** and other churches: environmental programmes such as **Eco Church** (run by the Christian charity A Rocha UK) help congregations cut energy use, care for churchyards as wildlife habitats and campaign; the Church has set itself targets to reduce its carbon emissions.
- Individual Christians recycle, use less energy, choose fair trade and support Christian Aid's climate campaigning.
- Diversity: some Christians focus on saving souls rather than the planet, or believe the world will be renewed by God at the end; most still see stewardship as a duty now.

### Islam: khalifah, fitra and amanah

- **Qur'an 7:54**: Allah created the heavens and the earth in six days; the sun, moon and stars are **subjected by His command**; "His is the creation and the command". So everything **belongs to Allah** and runs by His will. Humans are not owners. The next verses warn people not to cause **corruption on the earth** after it has been set in order (7:56).
- **Khalifah** (vicegerent, steward, trustee): Allah told the angels He would place a khalifah on the earth (2:30). Humans are given the **amanah** (trust) to look after creation, and will be judged on how they did it on the **Day of Judgement** (akhirah). A hadith teaches that the world is green and beautiful and Allah has appointed you **stewards** over it to see how you behave.
- **Fitra**: the natural, pure disposition every human is born with, inclined to recognise Allah and live in harmony with His creation (30:30). Damaging nature goes against that original nature; caring for it returns to it.
- **Mizan** (balance): Allah set the balance of creation, and humans must not upset it (55:7-9).
- **Tawhid**: because one God made all things, everything is connected; harming one part harms the whole.
- Hadith also encourage planting trees even if the world is about to end, and using water sparingly even at a flowing river.

**In practice**: Muslim scholars issued an **Islamic Declaration on Global Climate Change** in 2015, calling on Muslims to reduce emissions. In Britain, **Cambridge Central Mosque** was designed as an eco-mosque (natural light, heat pumps, rainwater). Islamic Relief runs climate and water projects. Muslims avoid waste (israf), including of food and water.

**Global citizenship in Islam**: the **ummah** is worldwide, and all humans are created by Allah "into peoples and tribes that you may know one another" (49:13), so Muslims see duties to all people, especially the poor who suffer most from environmental damage.

### Non-religious views: Humanist Climate Action

**Humanists** believe this life is the only one we have, and the Earth the only home humans have, so caring for it is a **moral duty** to people now and in the future, not to God. They base action on **reason and science** (the evidence of climate change) and on **empathy** for others.

**Humanist Climate Action** is a network within **Humanists UK** that brings humanists together to take action on the climate and ecological crisis: encouraging members to reduce their own impact, campaigning for science-based government policy, and working with others for a sustainable future. It shows **global citizenship** without religion: responsibility comes from shared humanity.

Many **atheists** reach the same conclusion through **utilitarian** reasoning: environmental damage causes enormous suffering, so preventing it brings the greatest good for the greatest number.

### The value of animal life

| View | Teaching |
|---|---|
| **Christian** | Animals are created by God and "good" (Genesis 1:25). Humans have **dominion** and may use animals for food, clothing and, where necessary, medical research, but **cruelty is wrong**: "The righteous care for the needs of their animals" (Proverbs 12:10). Catholic teaching says causing animals needless suffering is contrary to human dignity. **St Francis of Assisi** is honoured for his love of creatures. Humans are uniquely made in God's **image** with a soul, so human life has greater value. Some Christians are vegetarian out of compassion. |
| **Muslim** | Animals are "communities like you" (6:38) and praise Allah in their own way. Hadith tell of a woman punished for starving a cat, and a man forgiven for giving water to a thirsty dog. **Halal** slaughter must be quick, with a sharp knife, invoking Allah's name, and not in sight of other animals, to reduce suffering. Animals may be used for food and necessary research, as part of the **amanah**; humans as **khalifah** have special status and responsibility. |
| **Peter Singer** | See below. |

### Peter Singer and speciesism

**Peter Singer** is an Australian **utilitarian** philosopher. In ***Animal Liberation*** (1975) he made famous the term **speciesism**: treating beings differently simply because of the **species** they belong to.
- Singer argues speciesism is like **racism** or **sexism**: an unjustified bias in favour of one's own group.
- What matters morally is the **capacity to suffer**. Following Jeremy Bentham, he says the question is not "Can they reason?" but whether they can suffer.
- So we should give **equal consideration of interests**: an animal's suffering counts as much as a similar human suffering.
- He concludes that **factory farming** and many **animal experiments** are wrong, and he supports **vegetarianism** or veganism.
- He also argues that being human does not, in itself, make a life more valuable: some animals are more self-aware than some humans. This is very controversial.

**Responses**
- **Christians** and **Muslims** reject the idea that species membership is irrelevant: humans alone are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27) or appointed khalifah, with a soul and moral responsibility. But many agree with Singer that **cruelty** and needless suffering are wrong, and that factory farming deserves criticism.
- **Humanists** vary: most value human life highly because of human reason and relationships, while agreeing that animal suffering matters.

::: example (c) responsibility for the environment
Explain, from either two religions or two religious traditions, beliefs about being responsible for the environment. (8)

**Christianity.** Christians believe they are responsible for the environment because it belongs to God: "The earth is the LORD's, and everything in it" (Psalm 24:1). **Islam.** Muslims believe Allah created everything and controls it, "His is the creation and the command" (7:54), so humans own nothing. Allah appointed humans as khalifah (2:30), trustees who hold creation as an amanah and must keep the balance (mizan) He set. On the Day of Judgement they will answer for how they treated it. So Muslims avoid waste, even of water at a river, and scholars issued an Islamic Declaration on Global Climate Change in 2015.

::: example (d) using Singer
'Humans are more important than animals.' One paragraph using a non-religious view. (15)

Peter Singer would reject the statement. He argues that favouring humans simply because they are human is speciesism, an unjustified bias like racism, and that what matters is the capacity to suffer. On his principle of equal consideration of interests, a pig's pain in a factory farm counts as much as similar human pain. This is a strong argument against cruelty, and many religious believers would accept that part of it: Proverbs 12:10 and the hadith of the woman punished for starving a cat both condemn needless suffering. However, Singer's wider claim that human life has no special value is weaker, because it ignores the features that make humans morally responsible at all, such as conscience and reason; Christians explain these by the image of God (Genesis 1:27) and Muslims by humans' role as khalifah. So humans may be more important, but that importance gives them greater duties towards animals, not licence to exploit them.
