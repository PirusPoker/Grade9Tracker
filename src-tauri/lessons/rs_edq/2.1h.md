## What it is

This lesson opens **Theme 3: Issues of Good and Evil** with three questions: **what makes an act wrong?**, **where does evil come from?**, and **can belief in a good, all-powerful God survive evil and suffering?** You need Christian answers (relative and absolute morality, conscience, virtues, sin; Original Sin; soul-making in **Irenaeus** and **John Hick**) and Muslim answers (conscience, free will, doing the **will of Allah**; the Devil tests humans, **Qur'an 2:34** and **2:155**; **al-Qadr** and free will).

This theme is Question 3 of Component 1. Its (d) says "You must refer to religion and belief"; there is **no SPaG** and no compulsory non-religious strand, though an atheist view makes a good alternative viewpoint.

### Good, evil and morality

- **Good** is what is morally right and beneficial (for believers, in line with God's will); **evil** is what is morally wrong or harmful, against God's will. **Morality** is the set of principles used to decide right and wrong.
- **Moral evil** is suffering caused by human choices (murder, war); **natural evil** is suffering not caused by humans (earthquakes, disease). A question on the **origins** of evil wants the religious explanation of where evil came from, not this list.

### Absolute and relative morality

| | Absolute morality | Relative morality |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | Some actions are **always** right or wrong, whatever the situation or culture. | Right and wrong **depend** on the situation, the consequences or the culture. |
| Example | Murder and lying are always wrong. | Lying to protect someone's life could be right. |
| Religious use | Many Christians treat the Ten Commandments ("You shall not murder", Exodus 20:13) as absolute; most Muslims treat the clear haram acts of the Qur'an as absolute. | Some liberal Christians follow **situation ethics** (Joseph Fletcher): do the most loving thing in each situation, guided by **agape**. |

Absolute rules are clear and protect the vulnerable but can seem rigid when rules clash; relative approaches are compassionate in hard cases but can be used to excuse almost anything.

### Christian responses: what makes an act wrong

- **The Bible and Church**: an act is wrong if it breaks God's commands (the Ten Commandments) or Jesus' teaching (love God and your neighbour; the Sermon on the Mount). Catholics also follow the **Magisterium**, the teaching authority of the Pope and bishops.
- **Conscience**: an inner sense of right and wrong. **Thomas Aquinas** described it as reason making moral judgements; many Christians call it the "voice of God". Paul speaks of the law "written on their hearts" (Romans 2:15). Catholics teach that conscience must be **informed** by Church teaching, as it can be mistaken.
- **Virtues**: good qualities of character: the **cardinal virtues** (prudence, justice, fortitude, temperance) and the **theological virtues** of faith, hope and love (1 Corinthians 13:13). A wrong act shows the opposite, such as greed or cruelty.
- **Sin**: any thought or action against God's will, which damages the relationship with God and others. Christians distinguish **Original Sin** (the inherited tendency to do wrong) from **personal sin**.

### Muslim responses: what makes an act wrong

- **Doing the will of Allah**: right and wrong are set out in the **Shari'ah** (Islamic law), drawn from the **Qur'an**, the **Sunnah** (the Prophet's example) and scholars' reasoning. Acts range from **fard** (compulsory) to **haram** (forbidden); murder, theft and drinking alcohol are wrong because Allah forbids them. Scholars apply these commands to new situations.
- **Conscience**: every person is born with **fitra**, a natural inclination to know Allah and recognise good. **Niyyah** (intention) matters too: deeds are judged by their intentions.
- **Free will**: humans are **khalifah** (stewards), free to choose and **accountable** on the Day of Judgement. Wrong acts are those freely chosen against Allah's guidance.

### Christian views on the origin of evil

**Original Sin and free will (St Augustine).** God created a perfect world; evil is not something God made but a lack or corruption of good. It entered through the **free will** of angels and humans: Adam and Eve disobeyed God by eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (**Genesis 3**). This **Fall** brought sin, death and suffering into the world and passed **Original Sin** to all humanity. God is not to blame: love must be freely chosen. Through Jesus, God offers **grace** and salvation.

**Soul-making (Irenaeus and John Hick).** **Irenaeus** (2nd century) taught that humans were made in God's **image** but must grow into his **likeness**, so the world is a place to develop. **John Hick** (20th century) called the world a "**vale of soul-making**". God keeps an **epistemic distance** (his existence is not overwhelmingly obvious) so humans can freely choose good. Suffering lets people develop courage, compassion and patience, which could not exist in a world without pain.

**Satan and mystery.** Many Christians believe **Satan** tempted Jesus in the wilderness and tempts people today; others see the devil as a symbol of human evil. Some say the origin of evil is a **mystery** beyond human understanding.

### Muslim views on the origin of evil

- **The Devil tests humans (Qur'an 2:34)**: when Allah commanded the angels to bow to Adam, all did except **Iblis**, who refused out of **pride** and became one of the disbelievers. Iblis (also called **Shaytan**) was allowed to tempt humans until the Day of Judgement. He **whispers** temptation but cannot force anyone, so humans stay responsible.
- **Suffering as a test (Qur'an 2:155)**: Allah will test people with fear, hunger and loss of wealth, lives and crops, and gives good news to the **patient** (sabr). Life itself is a test of who is best in deeds (67:2). In loss, Muslims say that they belong to Allah and to Him they return (2:156).
- **Qur'an 42:30**: misfortune can result from what people's own hands have done, though Allah pardons much.
- **Al-Qadr and free will**: **al-Qadr** is Allah's decree: Allah knows and has power over all that happens. Sunni Muslims hold it as one of the six articles of faith, yet teach that humans still **freely choose** and are accountable; Allah's knowledge does not force their choices. Shi'a Muslims stress Allah's **justice** (Adalat), so they emphasise genuine human freedom. How the two fit together is accepted as partly beyond human understanding.

### The problem of evil and suffering

The challenge is often set out as an **inconsistent triad**: God is **omnipotent**; God is **omnibenevolent**; **evil and suffering exist**. Associated with **Epicurus** and later David Hume, it argues that all three cannot be true: if God is willing but unable to stop evil, he is not all-powerful; if able but unwilling, he is not all-loving. Many atheists and Humanists conclude that suffering, especially natural evil striking the innocent, is strong evidence against a loving God.

**Religious responses (theodicies)**: free will; soul-making; life as a test (Islam); the afterlife will put things right; God's ways are beyond us (the Book of Job; Qur'an 2:216 says people may dislike what is good for them); and believers should respond with prayer and practical help. Christians add that God shares human suffering in **Jesus on the cross**. Critics reply that the amount and unfairness of suffering seem greater than any growth could justify.

::: terms
- **Good**: that which is morally right, beneficial, and in line with God's will for religious believers.
- **Evil**: that which is morally wrong or harmful; for believers, what goes against God's will.
- **Morality**: the principles and standards people use to decide what is right and wrong behaviour.
- **Absolute morality**: the view that some actions are always right or wrong, whatever the situation.
- **Relative morality**: the view that right and wrong depend on the situation, consequences or culture.
- **Conscience**: an inner sense of right and wrong; for many believers, God guiding them from within.
- **Free will**: the ability to make choices for oneself, for which a person can be held responsible.
- **Sin**: any thought or action that goes against the will of God and damages the relationship with God.
- **Original Sin**: the Christian belief that the Fall of Adam and Eve passed a tendency to sin to all humans.
- **Suffering**: pain or distress, physical or mental, which may be caused by humans or by nature.
- **Theodicy**: an argument that tries to show why a good, all-powerful God allows evil and suffering.
- **Al-Qadr**: the Muslim belief in Allah's decree: Allah knows and has power over everything that happens.
:::

::: example (b) what makes an act wrong, Islam
Describe, from one religion, what makes an act 'wrong'. (5)

For Muslims, an act is wrong if it goes against the will of Allah as set out in the Shari'ah, which is based on the Qur'an and the Sunnah of the Prophet Muhammad. Actions are graded from fard (compulsory) to haram (forbidden), so stealing, murder and drinking alcohol are wrong because Allah has forbidden them. Muslims also believe every person is born with fitra, a natural sense of what is right, so conscience helps them recognise wrong, and intention (niyyah) matters: deeds are judged by the intention behind them. Because humans have free will, they are accountable for wrong acts on the Day of Judgement, so a Muslim tries to check their actions against Allah's guidance every day.

[Band 3, 5 marks: one religion, accurate terms, sources, influence on believers.]

::: example (c) origins of evil
Explain, from either two religions or two religious traditions, the origins of evil. (8)

**Christianity.** Many Christians, following St Augustine, believe God made a perfect world and evil came from the misuse of free will. In Genesis 3 Adam and Eve disobeyed God, and the Fall brought sin and suffering into the world and passed Original Sin to all humans. Other Christians follow Irenaeus and John Hick: God made humans immature and placed them in a "vale of soul-making" where suffering lets them grow into God's likeness. Many Christians also believe Satan tempts people, while some say the origin of evil is a mystery.

**Islam.** Muslims believe evil entered through the pride of Iblis, who refused Allah's command to bow to Adam (Qur'an 2:34). He may tempt humans until the Day of Judgement, but can only whisper, so humans are responsible for their choices. Suffering is part of the test of life (2:155), and the patient are rewarded; 42:30 teaches that some misfortune results from people's own actions. Everything happens within al-Qadr, Allah's decree, but both Sunni and Shi'a Muslims teach that humans have free will and will be judged.

[Band 4, 8 marks: two religions, diversity, sources used and explained.]

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'A loving God would not allow suffering.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Many atheists would agree, using the inconsistent triad: an all-powerful, all-loving God would want to and be able to stop suffering, so a child dying of disease suggests no such God exists. This is powerful because natural evil cannot be blamed on human free will. However, John Hick would reply that a world without suffering would be a world without growth: courage and compassion only make sense where real pain is possible. This reply is strong for suffering that produces growth, but weaker for suffering that crushes people. Muslims add that life is a test (2:155) and that justice is completed in Akhirah, which answers the unfairness of this life, though only for those who already accept the afterlife. On balance the statement is too simple, since a loving parent also lets children struggle, but the scale of suffering remains the hardest challenge for believers.
:::
