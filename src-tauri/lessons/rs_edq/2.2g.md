## What it is

This lesson covers **pilgrimage** and **festivals**. You need: **the importance of pilgrimage**, with the two named places **Walsingham** and **Taizé**, and **how Christians celebrate Christmas and Easter**.

This is part of Question 2 (Practices) of Component 2. The two named pilgrimages are very different: **Walsingham** is a **shrine to Mary** in **Norfolk**, England; **Taizé** is an **ecumenical monastic community** in **France**. Mixing them up is a frequent error. For festivals, always link what Christians **do** to what they **believe**: Christmas celebrates the **incarnation**, Easter the **resurrection** (2.2c).

### What is pilgrimage?

A **pilgrimage** is a **journey to a holy place** for religious reasons. A **pilgrim** travels as an act of **faith**, and the journey itself is part of the experience. Christians often see pilgrimage as a picture of the whole of life: a journey towards God.

**Why Christians go on pilgrimage**:

- to feel **closer to God** in a place where he is believed to have acted, or where holy people lived;
- to **pray** and **reflect** away from the distractions of everyday life;
- to **repent** and seek forgiveness, or to make a fresh start;
- to seek **healing** of body or mind, for themselves or others;
- to **give thanks**, e.g. for a recovery or a new baby;
- to meet and worship with **other Christians**, which strengthens faith and community;
- to follow in the steps of **Jesus**, who travelled to Jerusalem for festivals (Luke 2:41-42).

**Places**: the Holy Land (Jerusalem, Bethlehem, Galilee); Rome; Lourdes in France (Catholic healing shrine); Santiago de Compostela in Spain; in Britain, Iona, Lindisfarne, Canterbury and **Walsingham**.

**Diverse views**: pilgrimage is especially important to **Catholics**, **Orthodox** and many **Anglicans**. Some **Protestants** are wary of shrines, relics and statues, which they think can distract from faith in Christ alone, and stress that God can be met **anywhere**. Many Protestants still value journeys to the Holy Land or retreats such as Taizé, seen as a time of renewal rather than a holy duty. Pilgrimage is **not compulsory** for any Christian.

### Walsingham

- **Where**: a village in **Norfolk**, England, sometimes called "England's Nazareth".
- **Origins**: according to tradition, in the **eleventh century** a Saxon noblewoman, **Richeldis de Faverches**, had a **vision of the Virgin Mary**, who showed her the house in **Nazareth** where the angel Gabriel announced Jesus' birth (the **Annunciation**, Luke 1:28-33). Mary asked her to build a **replica of the Holy House** in Walsingham, and she did.
- **History**: it became one of the greatest shrines of medieval Europe, visited by kings and ordinary people. It was **destroyed** during the **Reformation** under Henry VIII. Pilgrimage was revived in the **twentieth century**.
- **Today**: there are **Anglican** and **Catholic** shrines, and also an Orthodox chapel.
  - The **Anglican Shrine** contains a reconstructed **Holy House** and a **holy well**.
  - The Catholic National Shrine centres on the medieval **Slipper Chapel**, about a mile outside the village, where medieval pilgrims are said to have removed their shoes to walk the last **"Holy Mile"** barefoot.
- **What pilgrims do**: attend **Mass** or Eucharist; walk in **processions** carrying a statue of **Our Lady of Walsingham**; pray the **Stations of the Cross** and the rosary; drink or are **sprinkled** with water from the **holy well**, and some ask for **healing**; light candles and leave prayer intentions; some walk the Holy Mile barefoot. Large national pilgrimages draw thousands each year, and many parishes and schools visit.
- **Importance**: it focuses on the **incarnation**: God becoming human in Mary's womb at Nazareth. Pilgrims honour **Mary** as a model of faith and obedience, who said yes to God (Luke 1:38). It is a place of prayer and healing, and Anglicans and Catholics worshipping in the same village is a sign of growing **unity**.

### Taizé

- **Where**: a village in **Burgundy**, eastern **France**.
- **Origins**: **Brother Roger**, a Swiss Protestant, settled there in **1940**, during the Second World War. He sheltered **refugees**, including Jewish people fleeing the Nazis. Others joined him, and they formed a **monastic community** of brothers who take lifelong vows.
- **Ecumenical**: the brothers come from **Catholic and Protestant** backgrounds and from many countries. The community's aim is **reconciliation**: between Christians of different denominations, and between peoples divided by war, poverty or culture.
- **What happens**: every year very large numbers of **young people** from all over the world come, usually for a **week**.
  - Three times a day, everyone gathers in the **Church of Reconciliation** for **common prayer**: short **chants** repeated many times, in many languages; a Bible reading; and a long **silence**.
  - Each morning a brother gives a **Bible introduction**, followed by **small-group discussion** with people from other countries.
  - Visitors share **practical work** (cooking, cleaning), eat **simple food** and sleep in simple accommodation.
- **Importance**: young people experience a **simple, prayerful life** and meet Christians from other traditions and nations; the silence and chants help people to **listen to God**; the "Taizé style" of prayer is now used in churches of every denomination around the world. Brother Roger was killed during evening prayer in **2005**, but the community continues his work.

### Walsingham and Taizé compared

| | Walsingham | Taizé |
|---|---|---|
| Place | Norfolk, England | Burgundy, France |
| Focus | shrine to **Mary**; Holy House; incarnation | **community**; prayer; reconciliation |
| Tradition | Catholic and Anglican shrines | **ecumenical** brothers, all denominations |
| Who goes | parishes, families, the sick, all ages | mainly **young people** |
| Activities | Mass, processions, holy well, Holy Mile | chants, silence, Bible study, shared work |

### Christmas

**Christmas** celebrates the **birth of Jesus**: the **incarnation**, God becoming human. "The Word became flesh" (John 1:14). Jesus is **Emmanuel**, "God with us".

- **Advent**: the four weeks before Christmas, a time of preparation and waiting. Churches light an **Advent wreath**, with one candle more each Sunday; it looks forward to Christ's birth and to his **Second Coming** (2.2d).
- **Carol services**, including services of **lessons and carols** that tell the story from creation to the nativity.
- **Nativity plays** and **cribs** (models of the stable) retell the stories in **Luke 2** (shepherds, angels, manger) and **Matthew 2** (the wise men).
- **Christingle** services, especially for children: an orange (the world), a candle (Jesus, the light of the world), a red ribbon (his blood) and fruit or sweets on sticks (God's gifts).
- **Midnight Mass** or a late-night Communion on Christmas Eve, and services on **Christmas Day** (25 December). Many Orthodox Christians celebrate on **7 January**, because they follow the older Julian calendar.
- **Giving gifts** recalls God's gift of his Son (John 3:16) and the wise men's gifts; many Christians also give to charity or volunteer, for example with the homeless.
- **Epiphany** (6 January) remembers the visit of the **wise men**: Jesus revealed to all nations.

**Diversity and debate**: some Christians worry that commercialism and **Santa Claus** hide the religious meaning; others see family, generosity and peace as true Christian values.

### Easter

**Easter** celebrates the **resurrection** of Jesus, his victory over **sin and death** (1 Corinthians 15:55). It is the **most important** Christian festival, because without the resurrection "our preaching is useless" (1 Corinthians 15:14). Its date moves each year.

**Lent**: the forty days before Easter, remembering Jesus' forty days in the wilderness. It begins on **Ash Wednesday**, when many receive a cross of **ashes** on the forehead as a sign of repentance. Christians may **fast**, give something up, pray more and give to charity.

**Holy Week**:

| Day | Remembers | Celebration |
|---|---|---|
| **Palm Sunday** | Jesus' entry into **Jerusalem** on a donkey, greeted with palm branches | **palm crosses**, processions |
| **Maundy Thursday** | the **Last Supper**; Jesus **washing** his disciples' feet | Eucharist; foot-washing; altars stripped |
| **Good Friday** | the **crucifixion** | solemn services, often noon to three; veneration of the cross; **Stations of the Cross**; processions of witness; no Mass is celebrated in Catholic churches |
| **Holy Saturday** | Jesus in the tomb | quiet waiting |
| **Easter Vigil** (Saturday night) | the resurrection | the church in darkness; a **new fire** is lit and the **Paschal candle** carried in ("the light of Christ"); the Gloria and bells return; baptisms |
| **Easter Sunday** | the **empty tomb** (Luke 24:1-9) | joyful services, flowers, "**Christ is risen!**" "He is risen indeed!"; sunrise services; **Easter eggs** as symbols of new life and the empty tomb |

**Orthodox Easter (Pascha)** often falls on a different date. At midnight the priest comes out with a lit candle, the congregation light their candles from it, and they process around the church; the joyful greeting "Christ is risen!" is repeated many times. Eggs may be dyed **red**, recalling Christ's blood and new life.

**Diversity**: Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican churches keep the full liturgy of Holy Week; many Evangelical and other Protestant churches mark Good Friday and Easter Sunday with simpler services and outdoor events. Some Christians follow Lent strictly; others hardly observe it.

::: terms
- **Pilgrimage**: a journey to a holy place for religious reasons, as an act of faith.
- **Pilgrim**: a person who makes a pilgrimage.
- **Shrine**: a holy place linked to a saint, Mary or an event, where people come to pray.
- **Walsingham**: a village in Norfolk with Catholic and Anglican shrines to Mary, built around a replica of the Holy House of Nazareth.
- **Taizé**: an ecumenical monastic community in Burgundy, France, founded by Brother Roger, visited by many young people.
- **Ecumenical**: bringing together Christians from different denominations to promote unity.
- **Advent**: the four weeks before Christmas, a time of preparing for Jesus' birth and his Second Coming.
- **Lent**: the forty days before Easter, a time of repentance, fasting and prayer.
- **Holy Week**: the week before Easter, from Palm Sunday to Holy Saturday.
- **Paschal candle**: the large candle lit at the Easter Vigil to symbolise the risen Christ, the light of the world.
- **Incarnation**: God becoming human in Jesus, which Christians celebrate at Christmas.
:::

::: example (b) Taizé
Describe pilgrimage to Taizé. (5)

Taizé is a village in Burgundy, France, where Brother Roger, a Swiss Protestant, settled in 1940 and sheltered refugees during the war. He founded an ecumenical monastic community whose brothers come from Catholic and Protestant traditions. Each year many young people from around the world stay for a week. Three times a day they join the brothers in the Church of Reconciliation for prayer made up of short, repeated chants in many languages, a Bible reading and a long silence. In the mornings they hear a Bible introduction and discuss it in small international groups, and they share simple food and practical work. ::: example (c) Easter
Explain how Christians celebrate Easter. (8)

Easter celebrates Jesus' resurrection, which Paul says is the foundation of faith (1 Corinthians 15:14). Christians prepare in Lent, beginning with ashes on Ash Wednesday as a sign of repentance, and fasting or giving things up to remember Jesus' forty days in the wilderness. In Holy Week, palm crosses on Palm Sunday recall Jesus' entry into Jerusalem, and on Maundy Thursday many churches celebrate the Last Supper and wash feet, as Jesus did. Good Friday is solemn: services, often from noon until three, remember the crucifixion, and some walk the Stations of the Cross. At the Easter Vigil, Catholics and Anglicans light a new fire and the Paschal candle in a dark church, showing Christ as the light that overcomes death. On Easter Sunday joyful services proclaim "Christ is risen!", and eggs symbolise new life from the empty tomb (Luke 24:1-9). Orthodox Christians hold a midnight candlelit procession. Many Protestant churches keep simpler services, but all celebrate Jesus' victory over sin and death.

[Band 4, 8 marks: in order, practices linked to beliefs and sources, diversity.]

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'Easter is more important than Christmas.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Many Christians would agree, because the resurrection is the heart of the faith: Paul says that if Christ has not been raised, "our preaching is useless and so is your faith" (1 Corinthians 15:14). Easter shows that Jesus' death brought atonement and that death is defeated, which is why the Church spends forty days of Lent and a whole Holy Week preparing for it. However, others argue that Christmas matters just as much, because without the incarnation, "the Word became flesh" (John 1:14), there would be no saving death and resurrection at all. Christmas also reaches far more people in Britain, through carol services and nativity plays. On balance, Easter is the more important festival theologically, as the churches' own calendars show, but the two celebrate one story and cannot really be separated.
:::
