### Al-Qadr: predestination

**Al-Qadr** (Allah's decree, measure or power to determine) is the belief that **Allah has knowledge of, and control over, everything that happens**. It is the **sixth article of faith** in Sunni Islam: belief "in al-Qadr, its good and its bad" (Hadith of Jibril). The Qur'an says "We have created all things with predestination" (Qur'an 54:49), and that no disaster strikes on earth or in yourselves without being in a record before it happens (57:22).

Many scholars explain al-Qadr in **four parts**:

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Knowledge** ('ilm) | Allah knows everything: past, present and future, including every choice each person will make |
| **Writing** (kitabah) | Allah has written everything in the **Preserved Tablet** (al-Lawh al-Mahfuz, Qur'an 85:22) |
| **Will** (mashi'ah) | Nothing happens unless Allah wills or permits it |
| **Creation** (khalq) | Allah is the Creator of all things, including the world in which humans act |

**In daily life**: Muslims say **insha'Allah** ("if Allah wills") about future plans; they accept hardship with **patience** (sabr), trusting that it has a purpose and may be a test; they avoid pride in success, recognising it as Allah's gift; and they find comfort in the belief that nothing is random.

### Al-Qadr and human freedom

If Allah knows and has written everything, are humans free? Muslims believe **both**: Allah is in control, and humans are **free and responsible**.

- **The Qur'an commands choice**: "whoever wills, let him believe; and whoever wills, let him disbelieve" (Qur'an 18:29); Allah showed humans the way, whether they are grateful or ungrateful (76:3). Commands and warnings only make sense if humans can choose.
- **Knowledge is not force**: Allah, outside time, **knows** what a person will freely choose, but His knowing does not make them choose it. Many Muslims compare it to a teacher who knows a pupil well enough to predict their result, without causing it.
- **Sunni explanations**: many Sunni theologians (the Ash'ari school) teach that Allah **creates** all actions, but humans **acquire** (kasb) them by choosing and intending them, so they are responsible. Others stress that Allah sets the **framework** (when and where we are born, our abilities, events beyond our control) while we choose **how to respond**.
- **Shi'a explanation**: Shi'a Muslims stress **Adalat** (divine justice): a just God would not punish people for actions He forced on them, so humans must be genuinely free. A saying attributed to **Imam Ja'far as-Sadiq** puts it as "neither compulsion nor total delegation, but a matter between the two". Allah gives humans the power to act, but they choose how to use it.
- **The limits of understanding**: many Muslims accept that the full relationship between Allah's will and human freedom is beyond human understanding, and that it is enough to believe both.
- **Humans versus angels**: angels have no free will (2.3d); humans have the trust (**amanah**) of choice, which is why they will be **judged**.

**Responsibility and change**: "Allah will not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves" (Qur'an 13:11). Muslims are expected to **act**, work, plan and make good choices, while trusting Allah with the outcome; a hadith tells a man to "tie your camel" and then trust in Allah.

### Akhirah: responsibility and accountability

**Akhirah** is the belief in **life after death**: this life is a **test**, and everyone will be **resurrected** and **judged** by Allah. It is one of the six articles of faith (the Last Day) and, as **Mi'ad**, one of the five Shi'a roots.

- Humans are **khalifah** and have free will, so they are **accountable** for their beliefs, intentions and actions.
- **Every deed counts**: "whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it" (Qur'an 99:7-8).
- **Intentions** matter: a famous hadith teaches that actions are judged by intentions (niyyah).
- Allah is **just** (no one is wronged, Qur'an 4:40) and **merciful** (sincere repentance is accepted; good deeds are multiplied).
- Belief in the Akhirah gives life **meaning and purpose**, encourages **good behaviour**, gives **hope** to those who suffer injustice, and helps Muslims face death without despair.

### The journey after death

| Stage | Muslim belief |
|---|---|
| **Death** | The **angel of death** takes the soul at its appointed time (32:11); the righteous soul is drawn out gently |
| **The grave and barzakh** | The body is buried quickly, facing Makkah. **Munkar and Nakir** question the soul: "Who is your Lord? What is your religion? Who is your prophet?" The soul waits in **barzakh** (a barrier or in-between state, Qur'an 23:100) until the resurrection; the righteous feel peace, the wicked discomfort |
| **The trumpet** | **Israfil** blows the trumpet: all die, then all are **resurrected** in body and soul (39:68) |
| **The Day of Judgement** (Yawm ad-Din) | All gather before Allah. Each person is given their **book of deeds**, recorded by the angels: in the **right hand** for the saved, the **left hand** for the lost (69:19-25) |
| **The scales** (mizan) | Deeds are **weighed**; "We shall set up the scales of justice" (21:47) |
| **The bridge** (as-Sirat) | In hadith, everyone must cross a bridge over hell: the righteous cross swiftly to paradise, the wicked fall |
| **Intercession** | Many Muslims believe the Prophet will intercede for believers by Allah's permission; Shi'a Muslims also believe the **Imams** and the Prophet's family will intercede |

### Heaven (Jannah): nature, stages and purpose

**Nature**: **Jannah** means "garden". The Qur'an describes it as a **paradise** of eternal peace, joy and reward:

- **Gardens beneath which rivers flow**; rivers of pure water, of milk that never changes, of wine that does not intoxicate, and of clear honey (Qur'an 47:15).
- Shade, fruit, fine clothes of silk, couches, cups and companions; **no pain, tiredness, illness, hatred or death**; greetings of "**peace**" (salam).
- Above all, **nearness to Allah** and His **pleasure**: "the approval of Allah is greater" than the gardens themselves (Qur'an 9:72). Many Muslims believe the righteous will **see Allah**, the greatest reward; Shi'a theologians understand this "seeing" as a spiritual vision of the heart rather than with the eyes.
- **Eternal**: the righteous will remain there for ever.
- **Physical or spiritual?** Many Muslims take the descriptions **literally**, believing in a real, physical paradise with resurrected bodies; others believe the images point to joys **beyond human imagination**, since a hadith qudsi says Allah has prepared for His servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no heart has imagined.

**Stages (levels)**: Jannah has **different levels**. A hadith says there are a hundred levels, and the highest is **al-Firdaws**, nearest to Allah's throne. The **higher a person's faith and good deeds**, the **higher their level**. Hadith also describe **eight gates**, each for those who excelled in a particular good deed, such as **ar-Rayyan** for those who fasted, and gates for those who prayed, gave charity or strove in Allah's way.

**Purpose**:

1. **Reward**: for faith, worship and good deeds, as Allah has **promised** (fulfilment of His word).
2. **Justice**: the righteous who suffered or were treated unfairly in this life receive what they deserve.
3. **Mercy**: entry is ultimately by Allah's **mercy**; a hadith says no one enters paradise by their deeds alone, but by Allah's mercy.
4. **Nearness to Allah**: the fulfilment of human life, which was created to worship and know Him.
5. **Motivation now**: hope of Jannah encourages Muslims to pray, fast, give zakah and treat others well, and gives hope in hard times.

### Hell (Jahannam): nature and purpose

**Nature**:

- A place of **fire** and **punishment**, of scorching heat, boiling water and bitter food; the Qur'an warns that whenever skins are burnt they are replaced so that the punishment continues (4:56).
- It has **seven gates** (15:44) and many traditions describe **levels**, the lowest for the worst wrongdoers.
- Guarded by angels, with **Malik** as their keeper (43:77).
- Above all, **separation from Allah's mercy**: the greatest loss.
- **Eternal or temporary?** Most Muslims believe hell is **eternal** for those who reject Allah and die committing shirk, while **sinful believers** may be **punished for a time and purified**, then admitted to paradise through Allah's mercy and the intercession of the Prophet. A few scholars have argued that hell may eventually end for all, but this is a minority view.
- Many Muslims take the descriptions literally; others see them as describing real spiritual suffering in vivid language.

**Purpose**:

1. **Justice**: wrongdoers who were never punished in this life face consequences; victims see that evil does not win.
2. **Warning**: the Qur'an's descriptions are meant to **turn people back** to Allah before it is too late.
3. **Purification**: for some believers, temporary punishment cleanses sin before paradise.
4. **Responsibility**: shows that free choices have real consequences.

### How belief in the Akhirah influences Muslims

- **Daily conduct**: knowing every deed is recorded encourages honesty, kindness, prayer and avoiding sin.
- **Repentance** (tawbah): Muslims seek forgiveness regularly, knowing Allah is merciful.
- **Charity and justice**: zakah, sadaqah and standing up for the oppressed, all recorded for the Last Day.
- **Facing death and suffering**: patience and hope; funerals are simple, with prayers for the dead person's forgiveness.
- **Balance**: Muslims are taught to live between **hope** (in Allah's mercy) and **fear** (of His justice).

::: terms
- **Al-Qadr**: predestination; the belief that Allah knows, has written and wills everything that happens.
- **Preserved Tablet**: al-Lawh al-Mahfuz, the record on which Allah has written all that will happen.
- **Insha'Allah**: "if Allah wills", said by Muslims when speaking of the future.
- **Akhirah**: the afterlife; belief in life after death, resurrection and judgement.
- **Accountability**: being answerable to Allah on the Day of Judgement for one's beliefs, intentions and actions.
- **Barzakh**: the barrier or waiting state between death and the resurrection.
- **Day of Judgement**: Yawm ad-Din; the day when all people are resurrected and judged by Allah.
- **Jannah**: heaven or paradise, a garden of eternal reward and nearness to Allah, with many levels.
- **Al-Firdaws**: the highest level of paradise, closest to Allah.
- **Jahannam**: hell, a place of fire and punishment, and separation from Allah's mercy.
- **Kasb**: acquisition; the Sunni teaching that humans acquire the actions Allah creates by choosing them.
- **Amanah**: the trust of free will and responsibility that Allah gave to humans.
:::

::: example (c) al-Qadr and freedom
Explain Muslim beliefs about al-Qadr and human freedom. (8)

Al-Qadr is the sixth article of faith in Sunni Islam: Allah has "created all things with predestination" (Qur'an 54:49). Many scholars explain it in four parts: Allah knows everything, has written it in the Preserved Tablet, wills it, and creates all things. This is why Muslims say "insha'Allah" about the future and accept hardship with patience, trusting Allah's plan.

However, Muslims also believe humans are free and responsible. The Qur'an says "whoever wills, let him believe; and whoever wills, let him disbelieve" (18:29), and that Allah will not change a people's condition until they change themselves (13:11). Allah's knowledge does not force a person's choice: being outside time, He knows what they will freely do. Many Sunni scholars explain that Allah creates actions but humans acquire them (kasb) by choosing, so they are accountable. Shi'a Muslims stress Adalat: a just God would not punish people for what He forced them to do, so freedom must be real; a saying of Imam Ja'far as-Sadiq calls it "a matter between the two".

Because humans have free will, unlike angels, they will be judged on the Day of Judgement for their choices, so belief in al-Qadr does not make Muslims passive: they work hard, make good choices and then trust Allah with the outcome, as in the hadith "tie your camel".

[Band 4, 8 marks: detailed, sources, Sunni and Shi'a diversity, influence.]

::: example (d) evaluative paragraph
'Belief in al-Qadr means humans have no free will.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Some might agree, because al-Qadr teaches that Allah knows, has written and wills everything (54:49), and Muslims say "insha'Allah" because nothing happens outside His will; if everything is written in the Preserved Tablet, it seems humans cannot do otherwise. However, most Muslims reject this conclusion. The Qur'an repeatedly calls on people to choose (18:29), and the whole of the Akhirah depends on humans being accountable: judging people for actions they could not avoid would be unjust, and Allah is perfectly just (4:40). This second argument is stronger, because it is supported by Allah's own attributes, and Shi'a Muslims make it a root of faith in Adalat. Knowing a choice in advance is not the same as forcing it. Therefore al-Qadr does not remove free will; it means humans choose freely within a world that Allah knows and controls, which is a mystery Muslims accept in faith.
:::
