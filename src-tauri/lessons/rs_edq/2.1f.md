## What it is

This part of **Theme 2: Issues of Life and Death** asks what gives human life its value, and applies the answer to **abortion** and **euthanasia**. You need: diverse Christian beliefs about the **origin and sanctity of human life** (**Genesis 1:31**, **Jeremiah 1:5**); Muslim beliefs (**Qur'an 5:32**, **6:151**); diverse Christian attitudes and Muslim attitudes (**Qur'an 30:40**) to abortion and euthanasia; **non-religious views** on the importance of human life; and the **"Dignity in Dying"** movement, which the spec groups with humanist views.

The (d) question here must use **religious and non-religious** beliefs. These are deeply personal issues: present every view as its holders would, without mockery and without stating the lesson's own opinion.

### Sanctity of life and quality of life

- **Sanctity of life**: human life is **sacred**, holy and of infinite worth because it is given by God; only God has the right to end it. It does not depend on health, ability or age.
- **Quality of life**: how good or fulfilling a person's life is, considering health, pain, independence and relationships. Some people argue that when quality of life is very poor, ending life may be the compassionate choice. Religious believers also care about quality of life, but most say it cannot outweigh the sanctity of life.

### The law in Britain (background)

- **Abortion**: the **Abortion Act 1967** (amended in 1990) allows abortion in Great Britain up to **24 weeks** if **two doctors** agree that continuing the pregnancy would risk the physical or mental health of the woman or her existing children more than ending it. There is **no time limit** if the woman's life is at serious risk, to prevent grave permanent injury, or if there is a substantial risk of serious disability in the child.
- **Euthanasia and assisted suicide** are illegal in England and Wales: active euthanasia counts as murder or manslaughter, and assisting a suicide is a crime under the Suicide Act 1961. Parliament has been debating whether to change this: in 2025 MPs voted for a bill to allow some terminally ill adults to have help to end their lives, with safeguards, and it then went on to further stages. Check the current position before quoting it.
- **Withdrawing** treatment that is not helping, or giving **pain relief** that may shorten life, can be lawful.

**Types of euthanasia**: **voluntary** (at the person's own request); **non-voluntary** (the person cannot ask, e.g. in a long-term coma, and others decide); **active** (a deliberate act that ends life, such as a lethal injection); **passive** (withholding or withdrawing treatment so that the person dies of their illness). In **assisted suicide**, the person takes the final action themselves with help.

### Christian beliefs about the origin and sanctity of life

- God is the **origin** of life: he made humans in his image, and when he looked at everything he had made it was "**very good**" (**Genesis 1:31**). So every human life has value given by God.
- God knows and plans each person **before birth**: "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you" (**Jeremiah 1:5**). Many Christians take this to mean that personhood begins **before birth**, from conception.
- "You shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13); the body is a temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:19).

### Christian attitudes to abortion

| Tradition | Attitude |
|---|---|
| **Catholic** | Life begins at **conception**, so abortion is **always gravely wrong**: it deliberately ends an innocent human life. Under the principle of **double effect**, a life-saving operation on the mother (e.g. removing a cancerous womb or treating an ectopic pregnancy) may be allowed, because the death of the foetus is foreseen but not intended. The Church supports women in difficult pregnancies, and adoption. |
| **Church of England** | Abortion is wrong **in principle**, because the foetus is a potential human being with a right to life; but in some cases, such as serious risk to the mother's life or health, it may be the **lesser of two evils**. It calls for abortion to be rare and for better support for mothers. |
| **Liberal Christians** (e.g. some Methodists and Quakers) | The decision belongs to the woman, made prayerfully and in conscience; **agape** (selfless love) and compassion may sometimes point to abortion, e.g. after rape or when the foetus cannot survive. |
| **Many evangelicals** | Close to the Catholic view; often campaign against abortion, citing Jeremiah 1:5 and Psalm 139:13 ("you knit me together in my mother's womb"). |

### Christian attitudes to euthanasia

- **Catholic**: euthanasia is always wrong, because it rejects the sanctity of life and God's sovereignty over death. But a person is **not obliged to accept burdensome or futile treatment**, and strong pain relief may be given even if it shortens life (double effect), because the aim is to relieve pain, not to kill.
- **Church of England**: opposes changing the law on assisted dying, arguing that it would put pressure on vulnerable, elderly or disabled people to feel a burden; supports **palliative care**.
- **Hospice movement**: **Dame Cicely Saunders**, a Christian, founded **St Christopher's Hospice** (1967), the first modern hospice. Hospices give **palliative care** (pain relief and emotional and spiritual support) so people can die with dignity without euthanasia.
- **Some liberal Christians** support voluntary euthanasia for the terminally ill out of compassion and respect for conscience; for example, Archbishop **Desmond Tutu** said he would want that choice for himself. They argue that Jesus' command to love outweighs keeping someone alive in great suffering.

### Muslim beliefs about the sanctity of life

- **Qur'an 5:32**: whoever kills a soul, unless for murder or corruption in the land, it is as if he had killed **all mankind**; and whoever saves one, it is as if he had saved all mankind. Every life is of immense worth.
- **Qur'an 6:151**: do not kill your children because of poverty, for Allah provides for you and for them; and do not kill the soul which Allah has made sacred, **except by right** (lawful justice).
- **Qur'an 30:40**: it is Allah who **created** you, then **provided** for you, then will cause you to **die**, then will **give you life** again. The timing of death belongs to Allah alone.
- Life is an **amanah** (trust) from Allah; suffering can be a **test** (2:155) that brings reward for patience.

### Muslim attitudes to abortion

- Abortion is generally **haram**, because the foetus is a developing life created by Allah (6:151; 17:31).
- Many scholars teach that the soul (ruh) is breathed into the foetus at about **120 days**, based on a hadith describing stages of development. So many allow abortion **before 120 days** for serious reasons (e.g. severe foetal abnormality or a threat to the mother's health); some schools allow it only in the first **40 days**; some scholars do not allow it at any stage except to save the mother.
- **After ensoulment** almost all scholars allow abortion only if the **mother's life** is in danger, because she is an established life with responsibilities to others.
- Abortion for convenience or because of poverty is forbidden (6:151).

### Muslim attitudes to euthanasia

- Euthanasia and assisted suicide are **haram**: only Allah decides the moment of death (30:40), and taking life without right is condemned (6:151; 5:32). Suicide is forbidden.
- Suffering may be a **test** (2:155), and patience in illness brings reward; the community must care for the dying.
- However, many scholars allow **withdrawing futile treatment** when doctors agree there is no hope of recovery, because this allows death to come at Allah's time rather than causing it. Pain relief is allowed.

### Non-religious views

- **Humanists** value human life because of the experiences, relationships and choices it contains; this life is the only one. They put weight on **quality of life**, **autonomy** (the right to decide about one's own life) and **compassion**.
- On **abortion**, Humanists UK supports a woman's right to choose, seeing a foetus as gaining moral status gradually as it develops, especially once it can feel pain or survive outside the womb.
- On **euthanasia**, humanists generally support a change in the law to allow **assisted dying** for mentally competent adults who are terminally ill or incurably suffering, with strong safeguards.
- **Dignity in Dying** is a British campaign group (founded in the 1930s and renamed in 2006) that campaigns for terminally ill, mentally competent adults to have the choice of an **assisted death**, with safeguards, and for better end-of-life care. It is not itself a religious or humanist organisation, but humanists support its aims; the spec calls this the humanist "Dignity in Dying" movement. Its case: people should be spared unbearable suffering and be able to die with **dignity** and **control**.
- **Atheist utilitarians** argue from reducing suffering; **Peter Singer** argues that the wrongness of killing depends on a being's capacities (such as self-awareness and preferences), not simply on being human, and supports voluntary euthanasia.
- **Non-religious opposition** also exists: some doctors and disability campaigners fear a **slippery slope**, pressure on vulnerable people, and mistakes in diagnosis.

::: terms
- **Sanctity of life**: the belief that human life is sacred because it is given by God, so only God may end it.
- **Quality of life**: how good or fulfilling a person's life is, judged by factors such as health, pain and independence.
- **Abortion**: the deliberate ending of a pregnancy by removing the foetus from the womb before it can survive.
- **Euthanasia**: ending someone's life painlessly to relieve suffering, sometimes called mercy killing.
- **Voluntary euthanasia**: euthanasia carried out at the request of the person who wants to die.
- **Passive euthanasia**: allowing someone to die by withholding or withdrawing treatment.
- **Assisted suicide**: helping a person to end their own life, where they take the final action themselves.
- **Palliative care**: care that relieves pain and supports the dying and their families, rather than trying to cure.
- **Double effect**: the Catholic principle that an action with a good intention may be allowed even if a bad side effect is foreseen.
- **Ensoulment**: the moment the soul enters the foetus; many Muslims place it at about 120 days.
- **Autonomy**: a person's right to make decisions about their own life and body.
:::

::: example (c) euthanasia in two religions
Explain, from either two religions or two religious traditions, attitudes to euthanasia. (8)

**Christianity.** Most Christians oppose euthanasia because of the sanctity of life: humans are made by God and his creation is "very good" (Genesis 1:31), and "You shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13). The Catholic Church teaches that euthanasia is always wrong, but that people need not accept burdensome treatment, and that pain relief may be given even if it shortens life, because the intention is to relieve pain (double effect). Christians founded the hospice movement, beginning with Dame Cicely Saunders, to offer palliative care instead. Some liberal Christians, such as Desmond Tutu, have argued that compassion and agape can support a choice to die for the terminally ill.

**Islam.** Muslims believe euthanasia is haram because Allah alone decides when life ends: He created you, provides for you, "then will cause you to die" (30:40). Taking a life without right is forbidden (6:151), and suffering can be a test that brings reward for patience (2:155). However, many scholars allow life-support to be withdrawn when doctors agree recovery is impossible, because this lets death come at Allah's time instead of causing it. Families are expected to care for the dying.

::: example (d) paragraph with non-religious view
'A person should always be allowed to choose euthanasia.' One evaluative paragraph. (15)

Humanists and the Dignity in Dying campaign would largely agree. They argue that a mentally competent adult who is terminally ill has the autonomy to decide how their life ends, and that forcing someone to endure unbearable suffering is cruel when quality of life has gone. This argument is powerful because it rests on compassion as well as freedom. But even Dignity in Dying does not support the word "always": it campaigns for assisted dying only for terminally ill, mentally competent adults, with safeguards, because a depressed or pressured person might choose death when help could have changed their mind. Religious opponents go further: Catholics and Muslims hold that life belongs to God (Genesis 1:31; Qur'an 30:40), and the Church of England fears vulnerable people would feel a duty to die. So the statement is too absolute even for most supporters of euthanasia, though the case for a limited, safeguarded choice is much stronger.
