## What it is

**Sound-symbol correspondences (SSCs)** are the rules that link Spanish letters to Spanish sounds. Spanish spelling is very regular: once you know the rules, you can say any word you see and write almost any word you hear. Edexcel tests this twice:
- **Paper 1, Task 1 (read aloud, 8 marks):** you read a 50–55-word text. It is marked on how clear and comprehensible your pronunciation is, judged on the SSCs and, at Higher, on **key stress**. An English accent is fine as long as the sounds are right.
- **Paper 2, Section B (dictation, 10 marks):** you write six short sentences you hear. Each correctly spelt word scores, and **at Higher a missing or wrongly added stress accent loses the word**. Pearson builds two stress-test words into each Higher dictation.

This lesson covers Pearson's SSC list and the **Higher stress rules** (Appendix 2). The descriptions use these conventions:
- syllables are split with hyphens, and the **stressed syllable is in CAPITALS**: película = "peh-LEE-koo-lah";
- **th** is the sound in English "think": this is the standard pronunciation in most of Spain. Latin America and parts of southern Spain say "s" instead, and both are correct Spanish;
- **kh** is the throaty sound in Scottish "loch", and **gh** is a hard g as in "ghost" (used before e and i, where English "g" might be read as "j");
- **rr** is a rolled r, while a single **r** is one quick tap of the tongue.

### The five pure vowels

Spanish vowels are **short and pure**. They never slide into a second sound the way English "go" does ("goh-oo"), and they are never swallowed into "uh". Every vowel is said, including a final e.

| letter | sound | example | said as |
|---|---|---|---|
| a | "ah", as in father but short | para | "PAH-rah" |
| e | "eh", as in bed | este | "EHS-teh" |
| i | "ee", as in see | mil | "meel" |
| o | "oh", short and round, with no "oo" glide | no | "noh" |
| u | "oo", as in food | uno | "OO-noh" |

**I** and **u** next to another vowel usually blend into one syllable: **gracias** is two syllables, "GRAH-thyahs"; **cuando** is "KWAHN-doh". An accent on the i or u splits them: **día** is "DEE-ah"; **país** is "pah-EES".

### The spelling patterns behind the sounds

The c and g rules work in both directions, so they explain many spellings:

| sound | before a, o, u | before e, i |
|---|---|---|
| "k" | **ca, co, cu** | **que, qui** |
| "th" | **za, zo, zu** | **ce, ci** |
| hard "g" | **ga, go, gu** | **gue, gui** |
| "kh" | **ja, jo, ju** | **ge, gi** or **je, ji** |

That is why the spelling changes when an ending is added:
- **vez → veces**, **feliz → felices**: z becomes c before e;
- **empezar → empecé**: z becomes c before é;
- **llegar → llegué**: g becomes gu to stay hard;
- **buscar → busqué**, **rico → riquísimo**: c becomes qu to stay "k".

You learnt the preterite forms in G3b; the rule above explains them.

**Pairs that change meaning:**
- **pero** "PEH-roh" (but) vs **perro** "PEH-rroh" (dog): tap vs roll;
- **cuatro** "KWAH-troh" (four) vs **cuarto** "KWAHR-toh" (quarter, room);
- **ano** vs **año**: always write the ñ.

### Higher: where the stress falls (reading aloud)

Three rules tell you which syllable to stress:
1. **A written accent wins**: stress that vowel. **Rápido** "RAH-pee-doh", **útil** "OO-teel".
2. **Words ending in a vowel, n or s** are stressed on the **second-to-last** syllable: **problema** "proh-BLEH-mah", **examen** "ehk-SAH-mehn", **nosotros** "noh-SOH-trohs".
3. **All other words** (ending in a consonant except n or s) are stressed on the **last** syllable: **salir** "sah-LEER", **azul** "ah-THOOL", **ciudad** "thyoo-DAHD", **responsabilidad**.

Wrong stress can turn one word into another. **Desayuno** "deh-sah-YOO-noh" means "I have breakfast"; **desayunó** "deh-sah-yoo-NOH" means "he had breakfast". **Esta** (this) is not **está** (is). Pearson flags exactly these slips, along with English-sounding cognates. **Hotel** is "oh-TEHL", with a silent h and the stress on the last syllable. **Aeropuerto** is "ah-eh-roh-PWEHR-toh". **Habitación** is "ah-bee-tah-THYOHN".

### Higher: when to write an accent (transcribing)

Turn the rules round. Write an accent **only when the stress breaks the normal rule**:

| the stress is on… | write an accent when the word… | examples |
|---|---|---|
| the last syllable | ends in a **vowel, n or s** | así, corazón, inglés, estación, después |
| the second-to-last syllable | ends in **any other consonant** | fácil, dólar, azúcar, árbol, móvil |
| the third-to-last syllable | **always** | número, película, sábado, música, teléfono |

So, when you hear a word in the dictation:
1. **Count the syllables** and **find the stressed one**.
2. **Look at the last letter.**
3. **Apply the table.**

**Examen** ("ehk-SAH-mehn"): the stress is second-to-last and the word ends in n, so it follows the rule: no accent. **Fácil** ("FAH-theel"): the stress is second-to-last but the word ends in l, so it breaks the rule: accent.

**Plurals can gain or lose an accent** because they add a syllable:
- **examen → exámenes**, **joven → jóvenes**: the stress is now third-to-last, so an accent is added;
- **inglés → ingleses**, **canción → canciones**, **estación → estaciones**: the stress is now second-to-last on a word ending in s, so the accent goes.

**A stressed i or u next to another vowel always takes an accent**, which splits the syllables: **día, país, biología**.

Some accents mark meaning, not stress: **sí** (yes) / **si** (if), **tú** (you) / **tu** (your), **él** (he) / **el** (the), **más** (more), and question words **¿qué? ¿cómo? ¿dónde?** Learn these as spellings.

### What the dictation forgives, and what it does not

Pearson rewards a word spelt exactly, or with a slip that **does not change the sound**:
- **b / v**: vastante for bastante;
- **y / ll**: yevar for llevar;
- **ge / je** and **gi / ji**: jente for gente, colejio for colegio.

It does **not** reward a spelling that would be **read aloud differently** under the SSC rules, even if it looks close:
- **gitarra** reads as "khee-TAH-rrah", so it is not guitarra;
- **dansa** for danza (s is not "th");
- **pequeno** for pequeño;
- **passar** for pasar (Spanish has no ss);
- **pero** for perro, or **cala** for calla: one consonant instead of two changes the sound;
- at Higher, **movil** for móvil, or **sociáles** with an accent that is not needed.

A phonetic spelling so far off that the word cannot be recognised on its own also scores 0. Pearson's list names only those four swaps, so **always write the silent h** too.

**Off-list words can appear in the dictation**, so you must be able to spell a word you have never seen, from its sound alone. That is what the SSC rules are for.

::: terms
- **SSC (sound-symbol correspondence)**: the link between a Spanish spelling and its sound, such as j = throaty kh, ñ = ny.
- **pure vowel**: a short Spanish vowel with no glide: a, e, i, o, u are always ah, eh, ee, oh, oo.
- **soft c and z**: ce, ci and z are said "th" in most of Spain ("s" in Latin America): centro, cabeza.
- **silent u**: the u in que, qui, gue and gui is not said; it only keeps the c or g hard.
- **throaty kh**: the sound of j, and of g before e or i: viejo, gente, colegio.
- **tap and roll**: a single r inside a word is one tap (pero); rr, initial r and r after n, l, s are rolled (perro).
- **stressed syllable**: the syllable said loudest; at Higher it decides how you read a word and whether it needs an accent.
- **second-to-last rule**: words ending in a vowel, n or s are stressed second-to-last; others on the last syllable.
- **written accent**: marks a stress that breaks the rule: estación, fácil, película.
- **tolerated misspelling**: a dictation slip that keeps the sound and still scores: b/v, y/ll, ge/je, gi/ji.
- **rewardable word**: a dictation word Pearson counts; 30 are available, three per mark.
:::

::: method
1. **Read aloud:** in the preparation time, mark every **ll, j, ge/gi, ce/ci, z, ñ, h, rr** and **qu/gu** on the card, and underline the stressed syllable of every long word and cognate.
2. **Keep the vowels pure** and say every one, including a final e.
3. **For stress**, follow the written accent if there is one; otherwise use the second-to-last rule for words ending in a vowel, n or s, and the last syllable for the rest.
4. **Dictation:** write each sentence on the first hearing, check the spelling on the second, and check the accents on the third.
5. **For each sound, choose the spelling the rules allow**: "k" before e/i is qu; "th" before a/o/u is z; hard g before e/i is gu.
6. **For accents, find the stressed syllable, look at the last letter, and apply the transcription table.** Only write an accent the rules demand.
7. **In the 2 minutes' checking time**, look for missing ñ, single r for rr, ss, and the endings that show tense (hablo / habló).
:::

::: example Spelling a sentence from its sound
Dictation. Write the sentence in Spanish. It sounds like "mee koh-LEH-khyoh ehs-TAH THEHR-kah deh lah ehs-tah-THYOHN". (7)

**Answer.** **Mi colegio está cerca de la estación.** (1 per word)
- **colegio**: kh before i is gi or ji; colejio would still score.
- **está**: stressed on the last syllable and ends in a vowel, so it needs an accent. Esta is a different word.
- **cerca**: "th" before e is c; serca does not score.
- **estación**: stressed on the last syllable and ends in n, so it needs an accent.
:::

::: example Accent or no accent?
Each word is written without accents, followed by how it sounds. Add an accent where the rules require one: (a) lunes "LOO-nehs" (b) arbol "AHR-bohl" (c) telefono "teh-LEH-foh-noh" (d) cancion "kahn-THYOHN". (4)

**Answer.**
- (a) **lunes** (1): it ends in s and is stressed second-to-last, which is the normal rule, so no accent.
- (b) **árbol** (1): second-to-last stress, but it ends in l, so it needs an accent.
- (c) **teléfono** (1): third-to-last stress always takes an accent.
- (d) **canción** (1): last-syllable stress on a word ending in n.
:::

::: example Marking a read-aloud sentence
Mark the stressed syllables and the SSC traps in: "Hoy hace mucho calor y llevo un sombrero." (5)

**Answer.** Any five (1 each):
- **hoy** "oy" and **hace** "AH-theh": silent h;
- **hace**: c before e is "th";
- **calor** "kah-LOHR": ends in r, so the stress is on the last syllable;
- **llevo** "YEH-boh": ll is "y" and v is "b";
- **sombrero** "sohm-BREH-roh": single tapped r twice, second-to-last stress;
- **mucho**: ch as in church; vowels pure, "MOO-choh".
:::

::: example Does it score?
The dictation sentence was "Mi vecino viaja mucho." Which of these spellings would be rewardable? (a) becino (b) viaha (c) mutcho (d) vesino. (4)

**Answer.** (a) **Yes** (1): b/v swap, same sound. (b) **No** (1): h is silent, so viaha would sound "BYAH-ah". (c) **No** (1): tch is not a Spanish spelling for ch. (d) **No** (1): s instead of c changes "th" to "s".
:::

::: example Stress that changes meaning
Explain the difference between: (a) hablo and habló (b) cuatro and cuarto. (2)

**Answer.** (a) **Hablo** "AH-bloh" is "I speak"; **habló** "ah-BLOH" is "he/she spoke": the accent moves the stress and changes the tense (1). (b) **Cuatro** "KWAH-troh" is "four"; **cuarto** "KWAHR-toh" is "quarter / room": the r moves position (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Keep vowels pure and say them all**: "EHS-teh", not "est"; "noh", not "noh-oo".
- **Make the h silent and the j throaty**: hotel is "oh-TEHL"; hamburguesa starts with "ahm".
- **Never pronounce cognates the English way**: hotel, aeropuerto, televisión, habitación and wifi must sound Spanish.
- **Roll rr and initial r, tap single r**: pero is "but", perro is "dog".
- **Stress verb endings correctly**: desayuno (I have breakfast) is not desayunó (he had breakfast).
- **Use qu and gu before e and i**: quiero, guitarra; kiero and gitarra score nothing.
- **At Higher, an accent missing or wrongly added loses the word**: móvil, inglés, número; never sociáles.
- **Always write ñ, never ss**: año, pasar.
:::
