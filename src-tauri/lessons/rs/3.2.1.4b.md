## What it is

The second half of Theme D looks at conflict **today**: whether religion causes war, the ethics of **nuclear weapons** and other **weapons of mass destruction (WMD)**, how religious people work to **make peace**, and how they help the **victims of war**. You need Christian and Muslim teaching (with differences inside each) and non-religious views. **Weapons of mass destruction** is one of the three Theme D issues on which you must explain **contrasting beliefs**: the specification requires **Christianity and at least one other religious tradition** (here, Islam). Differences within Christianity and a non-religious view can be added, but a non-religious view can never be the only contrast. The specification also asks for the work of **individuals influenced by religious teaching** and of **one present-day religious organisation**.

### Religion and belief as a cause of war and violence

Some people argue that religion is a major cause of conflict. The atheist writers **Richard Dawkins** and **Christopher Hitchens** argued that religious certainty divides people into "us" and "them" and can make violence seem justified by God. Examples often given:

| Conflict | The religious element | Other causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Northern Ireland** ("the Troubles", late 1960s to the Good Friday Agreement of 1998) | the two communities were largely Catholic and Protestant | mainly political and national: whether Northern Ireland should stay in the UK or join Ireland; discrimination in jobs and housing |
| **Israel and Palestine** | Jerusalem and the land are holy to Jews, Christians and Muslims | disputes over land, borders, security and refugees |
| **The former Yugoslavia** (1990s, including the Srebrenica massacre of Bosnian Muslims in 1995) | Orthodox Serbs, Catholic Croats and Muslim Bosniaks | ethnic nationalism and the break-up of a state |
| **"Islamic State" (ISIS)** in Iraq and Syria from 2014 | claimed to fight for Islam | civil war, political collapse; its actions were condemned by Muslim scholars and leaders worldwide |

**The other side**: most conflicts have political, ethnic or economic causes, and religion is often a **label for identity** or is **misused** by leaders. Most of the deadliest wars of the twentieth century — the World Wars, Stalin's purges — were not fought over religion. Religious teaching itself calls for peace — "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9); Islam comes from the same root as *salam*, peace — and religious people are often at the forefront of peace-making.

- **Christians** accept that Christians have used violence in God's name (e.g. the Crusades) but say this goes against Jesus' teaching of love.
- **Muslims** stress that terrorism and wars of aggression break the conditions for lesser jihad, and that "whoever kills a soul... it is as if he had slain mankind entirely" (Qur'an 5:32).
- **Humanists** argue that dogmatic beliefs of *any* kind, religious or political, can fuel conflict; the answer is reason, tolerance and human rights.

### Nuclear weapons and nuclear deterrence

**Weapons of mass destruction (WMD)** are weapons that can kill huge numbers of people indiscriminately and cause long-lasting damage:

- **Nuclear** — atomic bombs dropped by the USA on **Hiroshima** (6 August 1945) and **Nagasaki** (9 August 1945) killed well over 100,000 people, many instantly and many later from burns and radiation sickness.
- **Chemical** — poison gases such as sarin and mustard gas; used by Saddam Hussein's forces against Kurds at **Halabja** in 1988, and in the Syrian civil war (e.g. near Damascus in 2013). Banned by the Chemical Weapons Convention.
- **Biological** — spreading disease-causing organisms such as anthrax. Banned by the Biological Weapons Convention (1972).

Nine countries are believed to have nuclear weapons, including the UK, whose **Trident** missiles are carried on submarines. The **Non-Proliferation Treaty** (1968) commits nuclear states to work towards disarmament and others not to acquire them; the **Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons** (2017) bans them outright, but the nuclear-armed states, including the UK, have not signed it.

**Nuclear deterrence** is the idea of having nuclear weapons to stop others attacking you, because they know you could retaliate with devastating force — "mutually assured destruction".

| Arguments for deterrence | Arguments against |
|---|---|
| it has helped prevent a world war between major powers since 1945 | it relies on being willing to commit mass murder of civilians |
| other states have them, so giving them up could leave a country open to attack or blackmail | the risk of accident, misunderstanding or use by an unstable leader or terrorists |
| it gives a country influence and security | enormous cost (billions of pounds) that could be spent on health, education or overseas aid |
| a weapon that is never used kills no one | it encourages other countries to seek nuclear weapons (proliferation) |

### Contrasting beliefs about weapons of mass destruction

| View | Belief |
|---|---|
| **Catholic Church** | the Second Vatican Council taught that any act of war aimed indiscriminately at the destruction of entire cities with their people "is a crime against God and man himself" (*Gaudium et Spes* 80). Pope Francis went further: visiting Hiroshima in 2019, he said that the **possession** of atomic weapons, not only their use, is immoral. The Holy See was among the first states to ratify the 2017 ban treaty |
| **Church of England and other Protestant churches** | using nuclear weapons could never meet the just war conditions of **discrimination** and **proportionality**. Some Christians have accepted holding them as a **deterrent** while working for multilateral disarmament; in 2018 the Church of England's General Synod voted to welcome the 2017 ban treaty and urged the government to respond positively. Methodists, Baptists, the United Reformed Church and the Church of Scotland have made similar calls |
| **Christian pacifists** | Quakers, Pax Christi and **Christian CND** want all WMD abolished; **Bruce Kent**, a Catholic priest, led the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament (CND) |
| **Islam** | WMD break Islamic rules of war: they kill civilians, women and children, and destroy crops, animals and the environment, "transgressing limits" (Qur'an 2:190). They also damage Allah's creation, of which humans are khalifah. Many Muslims call for their abolition; some argue a Muslim state may hold them only as a **deterrent** for self-defence, never to use first |
| **Non-religious** | humanists judge by consequences: the suffering caused by WMD is so great that most humanists support disarmament; some accept deterrence as the lesser evil because it has kept the peace |

Almost all religious believers agree that **using** WMD is wrong. The main disagreement is about **possessing** nuclear weapons as a deterrent.

### Religion and peace-making in the contemporary world

**Peace-making** is actively working to end conflict and build lasting peace — through negotiation, bringing enemies together, campaigning, and addressing the injustice that causes conflict. Religious believers are motivated by teachings such as "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9), "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44), "if they incline to peace, then incline to it also" (Qur'an 8:61), and "make peace between your brothers" (Qur'an 49:10).

**Individuals influenced by religious teaching**

- **Archbishop Desmond Tutu** (1931–2021), Anglican Archbishop of Cape Town, opposed apartheid in South Africa non-violently and won the **Nobel Peace Prize in 1984**. After apartheid ended he chaired the **Truth and Reconciliation Commission**, where those who confessed their crimes fully could receive amnesty, so that the country could heal rather than seek revenge. He said his work came from his belief that every person is made in God's image.
- **Imam Muhammad Ashafa and Pastor James Wuye**, from Kaduna in Nigeria, once led rival Muslim and Christian militias in violent clashes. They were reconciled and together founded the **Interfaith Mediation Centre**, training young people and communities in peace-making, and helping to calm conflicts between Muslims and Christians.
- **Leymah Gbowee**, a Liberian Christian, organised Christian and Muslim women together in mass prayer and non-violent protest that helped bring an end to Liberia's civil war in 2003; she shared the **Nobel Peace Prize in 2011**.
- In **Northern Ireland**, **Father Alec Reid**, a Catholic priest, helped to open the talks that led to the peace process, and with **Rev Harold Good**, a Methodist minister, witnessed the IRA's decommissioning of weapons in 2005. The **Corrymeela Community**, a Christian community founded in 1965, still brings Catholics and Protestants together.

**Organisations**: **Pax Christi** (Catholic peace movement), the **Quakers**, the **Community of the Cross of Nails** (Coventry Cathedral), and interfaith groups such as **Religions for Peace**. Many Muslim leaders work through mosques and councils to prevent radicalisation and build community relations.

### Religious responses to the victims of war

War leaves people killed and injured, homeless, as **refugees**, traumatised, and without food, water or medical care. Religious believers respond because of their teachings:

- **Christianity**: the Parable of the **Good Samaritan** (Luke 10:25-37) — help anyone in need, even an "enemy"; the **Sheep and the Goats** — "I was a stranger and you invited me in" (Matthew 25:35); love your neighbour (Matthew 22:39).
- **Islam**: saving one life is as if saving all humanity (Qur'an 5:32); **zakah** and **sadaqah** support the poor and suffering; the Prophet taught that none truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself (Hadith, Bukhari and Muslim); the ummah is one community.
- **Non-religious**: humanists help from compassion and belief in human rights; many support secular charities such as the Red Cross and Médecins Sans Frontières.

Believers respond by praying, giving money, volunteering, welcoming refugees (e.g. church and mosque projects) and campaigning for governments to help.

**Present-day religious organisations — know one in detail**

| | **Christian Aid** | **Islamic Relief** |
|---|---|---|
| **Origins** | founded in 1945 by British and Irish churches to help refugees in Europe after the Second World War | founded in **Birmingham** in 1984 by **Dr Hany El-Banna**, first responding to famine in Sudan |
| **Beliefs behind it** | Christian love of neighbour; its slogan is "We believe in life before death" | Islamic teaching on compassion, zakah and sadaqah, and saving life (Qur'an 5:32) |
| **Work with victims of war** | emergency food, water, shelter and cash for families displaced by conflict, working through local partners in places such as Ukraine, Syria and Gaza; long-term rebuilding; campaigning on the causes of poverty and conflict | emergency food, water, medical aid and shelter in war zones such as Syria, Yemen and Gaza; support for orphans and refugees; rebuilding and livelihoods |
| **Who it helps** | anyone in need, regardless of religion | anyone in need, regardless of religion |

Both are members of the **Disasters Emergency Committee (DEC)**, which runs joint UK appeals in major crises. Other examples: **CAFOD** (Catholic, part of Caritas), **Tearfund** (evangelical Christian) and **Muslim Aid**.

::: terms
- **Weapons of mass destruction**: weapons — nuclear, chemical or biological — that can kill huge numbers of people indiscriminately and cause lasting damage.
- **Nuclear deterrence**: holding nuclear weapons to stop others attacking, because they fear a devastating response.
- **Nuclear disarmament**: getting rid of nuclear weapons, either by one country alone (unilateral) or by agreement between countries (multilateral).
- **Proliferation**: the spread of nuclear weapons to more and more countries.
- **Peace-making**: actively working to end conflict and build lasting peace, for example by bringing enemies together to negotiate.
- **Refugee**: a person forced to leave their country because of war, persecution or disaster.
- **Truth and Reconciliation Commission**: the South African body chaired by Desmond Tutu where people who confessed apartheid-era crimes could receive amnesty.
- **Islamic Relief**: a Muslim aid charity founded in Birmingham in 1984 that helps victims of war and disaster, whatever their religion.
- **Christian Aid**: a Christian aid charity founded by British and Irish churches in 1945 that helps people in poverty and victims of conflict.
:::

::: method
**Paper 2A gives each theme one five-part question: 1, 2, 4, 5 and 12 marks (24 in all).** Allow about 25 minutes per theme.

1. **1 mark — multiple choice**: four options A–D. Know the three kinds of WMD and the meaning of deterrence, proliferation and disarmament.
2. **2 marks — "Give two..."**: two short, correct, different points — e.g. two types of WMD, two ways a charity helps victims. Only the first two count.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two contrasting (or similar) religious beliefs about..." — "In your answer you must refer to one or more religious traditions."** Two beliefs, each 1 mark simple + 1 mark detailed. **Weapons of mass destruction** is a named "contrasting beliefs" issue: the specification requires **Christianity and at least one other religious tradition**, so set a Christian view against a Muslim one — e.g. Christians who accept nuclear weapons as a deterrent against Muslims who say WMD can never be justified because they kill civilians. The contrast within Christianity (Pope Francis against supporters of deterrence) or a humanist view can be added, but never as the only contrast. The 2023 paper asked for two **similar** beliefs about WMD — then both beliefs must agree (e.g. Christians and Muslims both condemn using them), and a contrasting pair loses marks.
4. **4 marks — "Explain two ways / reasons..."**: 2 + 2 — the point, then detail, an example or the effect.
5. **5 marks — "Explain two... Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching in your answer."** Two developed points plus **1 mark for a relevant, accurate source**: the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37), Matthew 25:35, Matthew 5:9, Qur'an 5:32, 2:190 or 8:61, *Gaudium et Spes* 80. Name the organisation or person accurately for full development — Christian Aid, Islamic Relief, Desmond Tutu.
6. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should give reasoned arguments in support of this statement; should give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; should refer to religious arguments; may refer to non-religious arguments; should reach a justified conclusion."** Plus **3 SPaG** marks. Spell deterrence, disarmament, reconciliation and organisation correctly.

**How the 12-mark levels are reached** (the whole answer goes in one level):
- **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with reasons stated.
- **Level 2 (4–6)**: one point of view argued with a logical chain, **or** different views recognised, each with reasons. **Capped at Level 2 with no reference to religion.**
- **Level 3 (7–9)**: reasoned consideration of **different** points of view, with logical chains that draw on knowledge, and **clear reference to religion**.
- **Level 4 (10–12)**: a well-argued response — different views weighed with logical chains **leading to a justified judgement**, religion *applied* to the issue.

For "religion causes war" statements, use real conflicts and separate the religious label from the political causes; then balance them against religious peacemakers. For nuclear statements, apply the just war criteria (discrimination, proportionality) and the Islamic rules of war, and weigh them against the deterrence argument.
:::

::: example 4-mark contrasting beliefs
Explain two contrasting religious beliefs about nuclear weapons. In your answer you must refer to one or more religious traditions. (4)

Some Christians believe it is acceptable for a country to possess nuclear weapons as a deterrent (1). They argue that because other countries have them, the threat of retaliation prevents attack, so holding them helps keep peace and protects innocent people — though they believe actually using them would break the just war condition of discrimination (1).

In contrast, many Muslims believe nuclear weapons can never be justified (1). Islamic rules of war forbid harming civilians, women, children, crops and animals, and the Qur'an says "do not transgress limits" (Qur'an 2:190); because a nuclear bomb kills indiscriminately and destroys Allah's creation, Muslims who hold this view call for all such weapons to be abolished (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark with a source
Explain two reasons why some religious believers choose to work for an organisation that helps victims of war. Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

Christians believe they must love their neighbour, whoever they are (1). In the Parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37) a Samaritan helps an injured Jew, a traditional enemy, so Christians working for Christian Aid help victims of war regardless of their religion or which side they were on (1) (source 1).

Muslims believe that saving life is one of the greatest acts of worship (1). The Qur'an teaches that saving one life is as if saving the whole of humanity (Qur'an 5:32), so working for Islamic Relief to provide food, water and medical care in places such as Syria and Yemen is a way of serving Allah and the ummah (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark evaluation
"Religion is the main cause of war in the world today." Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should give reasoned arguments in support of this statement; should give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; should refer to religious arguments; may refer to non-religious arguments; should reach a justified conclusion. (12)

Some people agree. Atheist writers such as Richard Dawkins argue that religious certainty divides the world into believers and unbelievers, and makes violence seem to have God's approval. There are modern examples: the group calling itself Islamic State claimed to be fighting for Islam in Iraq and Syria, and in the former Yugoslavia Orthodox, Catholic and Muslim communities fought each other. Jerusalem is holy to three religions, which makes the conflict over it harder to solve. Throughout history holy wars such as the Crusades were fought in God's name.

However, many people disagree. In most of these conflicts religion is a label for identity while the real causes are political: the Troubles in Northern Ireland were mainly about whether it should belong to the UK or Ireland, and about discrimination in jobs and housing. Muslim scholars around the world condemned Islamic State, pointing out that the Qur'an forbids killing innocent people (Qur'an 5:32) and requires peace when the enemy seeks it (Qur'an 8:61). Jesus taught "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9). Many wars today are about land, resources, power or ethnic identity, and the deadliest wars of the last century, such as the World Wars, were not religious. Religion also works for peace: Desmond Tutu led reconciliation in South Africa, and Imam Ashafa and Pastor Wuye brought Muslims and Christians together in Nigeria.

In conclusion, I disagree. Religion can make some conflicts worse, especially when leaders misuse it, but in most conflicts it is mixed with deeper political and economic causes. Since the teachings of Christianity and Islam call for peace, and religious believers are among the most active peacemakers, religion is more often a force for peace than the main cause of war.

(Level 4: arguments on both sides developed with real examples, Christian and Muslim teaching applied, and a justified conclusion.)
:::
