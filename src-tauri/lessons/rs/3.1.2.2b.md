## What it is

### What a sacrament is

A **sacrament** is a religious ceremony in which Christians believe God's grace is given or shown through an outward, physical sign. The classic definition, going back to St Augustine and used in the Church of England's Book of Common Prayer catechism, is "an outward and visible sign of an inward and spiritual grace". The outward sign is something you can see and touch — water, bread, wine, oil; the inward grace is God's free, undeserved love and help at work in the person.

Christians disagree about **how many** sacraments there are and **what they do**:

| Tradition | Sacraments | What they believe sacraments do |
|---|---|---|
| **Catholic** | Seven: baptism, confirmation, Eucharist, reconciliation (penance), anointing of the sick, marriage, holy orders | They really give grace — they are channels of God's grace instituted by Christ, not just reminders |
| **Orthodox** | Seven "holy mysteries" (baptism, chrismation, Eucharist, confession, holy unction, marriage, ordination) — some Orthodox say there is no fixed number | God truly acts in them; *how* is a mystery beyond words |
| **Anglican (Church of England)** | Two "sacraments of the Gospel" — baptism and Holy Communion — because Christ himself commanded them; the other five are recognised as sacramental rites by many Anglicans | Means of grace; views range from Catholic-style to Protestant |
| **Most Protestant churches** (Methodist, Baptist, Presbyterian, Pentecostal) | Two: baptism and the Lord's Supper (Baptists often call them **ordinances** — acts Christ ordered) | Signs and pledges of grace, or acts of obedience and remembrance |
| **Quakers (Religious Society of Friends)** and **The Salvation Army** | None performed | God's grace does not need outward rituals; the whole of life can be sacramental — an inner, spiritual relationship |

Why the difference? Protestants accept as sacraments only what Jesus clearly commanded in the Gospels: baptism (Matthew 28:19) and the Lord's Supper (Luke 22:19, 1 Corinthians 11:24–25). Catholics and Orthodox also draw on the tradition of the Church, which they believe is guided by the Holy Spirit.

### The sacrament of baptism

**Baptism** is the ceremony of washing with water that makes a person a member of the Church. Jesus himself was baptised by John the Baptist in the River Jordan, when the Spirit descended like a dove and a voice from heaven said "This is my Son, whom I love; with him I am well pleased" (Matthew 3:13–17; Mark 1:9–11). After the resurrection Jesus sent his disciples to "make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19) — the **Great Commission**. Every baptism uses these Trinitarian words.

**Significance for Christians:**
- It **washes away sin** — for Catholics and Orthodox, including **original sin** — and gives new life: "Repent and be baptised... for the forgiveness of your sins. And you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit" (Acts 2:38).
- It is **joining Christ's death and resurrection**: Paul says Christians were "buried with him through baptism into death" so that they may "live a new life" (Romans 6:3–4). Going under the water is dying to the old life; coming up is rising with Christ.
- It **welcomes the person into the Church**, the family of God — the first sacrament, the "door" to the others (a Catholic must be baptised before receiving the Eucharist).
- It is being "born of water and the Spirit" (John 3:5): the person receives the Holy Spirit and becomes a child of God.
- It is a **public sign of faith** and a commitment to follow Jesus.

### Infant baptism

**Infant baptism** is practised by Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Methodist and Presbyterian (e.g. Church of Scotland) churches.

**What happens (Catholic and Anglican):**
- The **parents and godparents** bring the baby to the font. They **reject evil and sin** and declare the Christian faith on the child's behalf, promising to bring the child up as a Christian.
- The priest or minister makes the **sign of the cross** on the baby's forehead.
- **Water** is poured over the head three times "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit".
- In Catholic baptism the baby is anointed with **chrism** (holy oil), dressed in a **white garment** (new, pure life) and given a **lit candle**, lit from the Paschal (Easter) candle — the child has received the light of Christ.
- The congregation welcomes the new member.

**Orthodox** babies are usually **fully immersed three times** in the font, then immediately receive **chrismation** (anointing with holy oil — the equivalent of confirmation) and their **first Holy Communion**.

**Reasons given for infant baptism:**
- It removes **original sin** and brings the child into God's grace from the start — the Catholic Church teaches baptism is necessary for salvation.
- **Grace is a gift**, not something earned: a baby cannot choose, but God's love comes first.
- Whole **households** were baptised in the New Testament — Lydia "and the members of her household" (Acts 16:15), the Philippian jailer "and all his household" (Acts 16:33) — which may have included children.
- Jesus welcomed children: "Let the little children come to me" (Mark 10:14).
- It welcomes the child into the Christian community, which will help raise them in the faith. The child can later confirm the promises for themselves at **confirmation**.

### Believers' baptism

**Believers' baptism** is baptism of people old enough to decide for themselves — usually teenagers or adults — practised by **Baptists, Pentecostals** and many evangelical and independent churches.

**What happens:**
- The person gives a **testimony** — a short account of how they came to faith.
- They are asked whether they **repent of their sins** and put their trust in Jesus as Lord and Saviour.
- They are **fully immersed** — usually lowered backwards under the water in a pool called a **baptistry** (or in a river or the sea), then raised up. This shows being buried with Christ and rising to new life (Romans 6:4).
- A Bible passage is read and the church may pray over them.

**Reasons given for believers' baptism:**
- **Jesus was baptised as an adult** (Matthew 3:13–17).
- In the New Testament, baptism follows **repentance and faith**: "Repent and be baptised" (Acts 2:38); the Ethiopian official believed, then asked to be baptised in water by the roadside (Acts 8:36–38).
- A baby **cannot repent or believe**, so baptism would have no meaning for them; it should be a personal, informed commitment.
- For many Baptists baptism is a **symbol** of salvation already received through faith, not what saves — so an unbaptised child is not lost.
- Many of these churches hold a **dedication** or **thanksgiving** service for babies instead, where parents thank God and promise to bring the child up in the faith.

### Different beliefs about infant baptism — summary

| | Infant baptism is right | Believers' baptism only |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Methodist, Presbyterian | Baptist, Pentecostal, many evangelical churches |
| Key belief | Baptism gives grace and removes original sin; grace does not depend on the person's choice | Baptism is a public act of obedience by someone who has already chosen to follow Christ |
| Evidence | Household baptisms (Acts 16:15, 16:33); "let the little children come to me" | Jesus baptised as an adult; "Repent and be baptised" (Acts 2:38) |
| Method | Water poured on head (full immersion for Orthodox) | Full immersion |
| Later commitment | Confirmation | Already given at baptism |

**Quakers and the Salvation Army** do not baptise with water at all: they believe what matters is inner baptism by the Holy Spirit, shown in a changed life.

### The sacrament of Holy Communion (Eucharist)

**Holy Communion** re-enacts or remembers the **Last Supper**, when Jesus took bread, gave thanks, broke it and said "This is my body, which is for you; do this in remembrance of me", then took the cup and said "This cup is the new covenant in my blood" (1 Corinthians 11:23–26; also Luke 22:19–20, Mark 14:22–24). It has many names:

- **Mass** — Catholic.
- **Divine Liturgy** — Orthodox.
- **Holy Communion** or **Eucharist** (from the Greek for "thanksgiving") — Anglican and others.
- **Lord's Supper** or **Breaking of Bread** — many Protestant churches (Baptists, Brethren).

**Significance for Christians:**
- It **remembers Jesus's sacrifice** on the cross and his resurrection: "whenever you eat this bread and drink this cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes" (1 Corinthians 11:26).
- It brings Christians into **union with Christ** — Jesus said "Whoever eats my flesh and drinks my blood remains in me, and I in them" (John 6:56).
- It expresses **unity** of the Church: "we, who are many, are one body, for we all share the one loaf" (1 Corinthians 10:17).
- It gives **grace and spiritual strength** for daily life, and hope for eternal life.
- It is **thanksgiving** for salvation. The Catholic Church calls the Eucharist "the source and summit of the Christian life" (Catechism 1324).

### Different interpretations of its meaning

| View | Held by | What happens to the bread and wine |
|---|---|---|
| **Transubstantiation** | Catholic Church (defined at the Council of Trent, 16th century) | At the consecration the **substance** of the bread and wine becomes the **real body and blood of Christ**, though the appearance, taste and texture (the "accidents") remain. Christ is **really present**. The Mass makes present the one sacrifice of Calvary. Consecrated hosts are kept in the **tabernacle** and adored. |
| **Real presence as a mystery** | Orthodox | The bread and wine truly become Christ's body and blood, but the Orthodox do not try to define how — it is a holy mystery. |
| **Sacramental union** (sometimes called consubstantiation) | Lutherans | Christ's body and blood are truly present "in, with and under" the bread and wine, which remain bread and wine. |
| **Spiritual (real) presence** | Many Anglicans, Methodists, Reformed/Presbyterian churches | Christ is really present **spiritually** to those who receive in faith; the bread and wine do not change physically. Anglican views range widely — Anglo-Catholics hold something close to the Catholic view. |
| **Memorial (symbolic)** | Baptists and many evangelical and free churches (following the reformer Zwingli) | The bread and wine are **symbols** that help believers **remember** Christ's death — "do this in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19). Nothing changes; Christ is present through the Holy Spirit among believers. |
| **No outward sacrament** | Quakers, The Salvation Army | Communion with God is inward and spiritual; every meal can be a reminder of Christ. |

### Different ways it is celebrated

- **Catholic Mass** — celebrated daily and every Sunday (attendance on Sunday is an obligation). It has two main parts: the **Liturgy of the Word** (Bible readings, homily) and the **Liturgy of the Eucharist**, when the priest says Jesus's words over the bread and wine (the consecration). People come forward to receive the **host** (a thin wafer of unleavened bread), usually on the tongue or in the hand; the priest says "The Body of Christ" and they reply "Amen". Only Catholics in a state of grace normally receive; children make their **First Holy Communion** at around seven or eight.
- **Orthodox Divine Liturgy** — the bread and wine are prepared behind the **iconostasis** (screen of icons). **Leavened bread** is used; the priest gives the bread soaked in the wine on a **spoon**. Worshippers usually stand; the service is sung, with incense and icons.
- **Anglican Holy Communion** — a priest presides, following a set liturgy (e.g. *Common Worship*). People often **kneel at the altar rail** and receive a wafer or bread and drink from a **shared chalice**. Some parishes are very formal ("high church"), others simple ("low church").
- **Nonconformist / Baptist Lord's Supper** — often less frequent (e.g. once or twice a month). A minister or elder leads; **ordinary bread** is broken and passed round the congregation in their seats, with wine or **non-alcoholic grape juice** in **small individual cups**. The emphasis is on remembrance and fellowship. The Brethren hold a "breaking of bread" every Sunday.

::: terms
- **Sacrament**: a religious rite that is an outward and visible sign of an inward and spiritual grace given by God.
- **Grace**: God's free, undeserved love and help, given to people through faith and, many believe, through the sacraments.
- **Infant baptism**: baptism of babies, with parents and godparents making promises for them; practised by Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican and Methodist churches.
- **Believers' baptism**: baptism, usually by full immersion, of people old enough to repent and declare their own faith; practised by Baptists and Pentecostals.
- **Original sin**: the inbuilt tendency to sin inherited from the disobedience of Adam and Eve, which Catholics believe baptism washes away.
- **Godparents**: adults who promise at an infant baptism to help bring the child up in the Christian faith.
- **Eucharist**: the service of thanksgiving in which Christians share bread and wine as Jesus did at the Last Supper; also called Holy Communion, Mass or the Lord's Supper.
- **Transubstantiation**: the Catholic belief that the substance of the bread and wine becomes the body and blood of Christ while the appearance stays the same.
- **Memorial view**: the belief, held by many Baptists and evangelicals, that the bread and wine are symbols to help Christians remember Jesus's death.
- **Real presence**: the belief that Christ is truly present in the Eucharist, either physically (Catholic, Orthodox) or spiritually (many Anglicans).
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 (Christianity), Practices question: five parts worth 1, 2, 4, 5 and 12 marks.** Spend about 25 minutes on the whole question.

1. **1 mark — multiple choice**: four options, choose one. Watch the look-alikes: transubstantiation vs consubstantiation; baptism vs confirmation; Eucharist vs Evangelism.
2. **2 marks — "Give two…"**: two short, separate, correct points ("water is poured three times"; "godparents make promises"). No explanation is needed; only the first two answers are credited.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two ways…" / "Explain two contrasting…"**: two points, each **developed**: 1 mark for the simple point, a second for developing it with detail, an example, a teaching or its effect on a Christian. For **contrasting** questions (e.g. "Explain two contrasting ways Christians celebrate Holy Communion" or "two contrasting beliefs about infant baptism") the two points must genuinely differ — Catholic transubstantiation vs Baptist memorial view; infant baptism vs believers' baptism — and name who holds each.
4. **5 marks — "Explain two… Refer to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching in your answer"**: two developed points (4 marks) **plus 1 mark for a relevant, accurate source** — e.g. Matthew 28:19, Romans 6:3–4, Acts 2:38, 1 Corinthians 11:23–26, John 6:56, the Catechism. Paraphrase with the reference if you are not sure of the wording, and **link the source to your point**.
5. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement."** Paper 1 bullets: *refer to Christian teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion.* On Paper 1 the SPaG marks go on the Beliefs 12-mark questions, so this Practices 12-mark carries no SPaG — but write accurately anyway.

**How the 12-mark levels are reached** (the whole answer is placed in one level):
- **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with a reason stated.
- **Level 2 (4–6)**: one view argued with a logical chain of reasoning, **or** different views recognised, each with reasons. **Maximum Level 2 with no reference to religion.**
- **Level 3 (7–9)**: reasoned consideration of **different** points of view, with logical chains that draw on knowledge, and **clear reference to religion**.
- **Level 4 (10–12)**: a **well-argued** response — different views weighed with logical chains **leading to a justified judgement**, with religious teaching *applied* to the issue.

Structure: two arguments for (point → explain → evidence → evaluate), two arguments against, then a conclusion that says which side is stronger **and why**. On this topic the "different points of view" are usually **denominational**: Catholic/Orthodox vs Baptist vs Quaker. Naming the denomination and giving its reason is what lifts an answer into Levels 3 and 4.
:::

::: example 4-mark — contrasting beliefs about infant baptism
Explain two contrasting Christian beliefs about infant baptism. (4)

Catholics, Anglicans and Orthodox Christians believe babies should be baptised (1). Catholics teach that baptism washes away original sin and brings the child into God's grace and the Church straight away; grace is a gift that does not depend on the baby's choice, and the child can confirm the promises later at confirmation (1). In contrast, Baptists and Pentecostals believe only people old enough to believe should be baptised (1), because in the New Testament baptism follows repentance and faith — "Repent and be baptised" (Acts 2:38) — and a baby cannot repent, so they hold a dedication service for babies instead (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark — importance of Holy Communion
Explain two reasons why Holy Communion is important to Christians. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

First, it is how Christians obey Jesus's command to remember his death (1). At the Last Supper Jesus said "do this in remembrance of me" (1 Corinthians 11:24) (source, 1), so every time they share the bread and wine Christians recall that he gave his body and blood for their salvation, which renews their gratitude and faith (1). Second, it unites Christians with Christ and with each other (1). Catholics believe that through transubstantiation they really receive the body and blood of Jesus, which gives grace and strength to live as Christians during the week; Paul says "we, who are many, are one body, for we all share the one loaf" (1 Corinthians 10:17), so sharing it builds the Church into one family (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark — believers' baptism
"Only adults should be baptised." Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should: refer to Christian teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion. (12)

Baptists and Pentecostals would agree. In the New Testament baptism always seems to follow a personal decision: Peter tells the crowd at Pentecost "Repent and be baptised" (Acts 2:38), and the Ethiopian official believed before he was baptised (Acts 8:36–38). A baby cannot repent or believe, so for these Christians baptism of an infant is a ceremony with no meaning for the person being baptised. Jesus himself was baptised as an adult in the Jordan, and Christians try to follow his example. Believers' baptism is also a powerful public witness: the person gives a testimony and is fully immersed, showing they have died to their old life and risen with Christ (Romans 6:4). Because they chose it, many find it a stronger commitment than promises made for them by others.

However, Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican Christians disagree. They believe baptism is not mainly something we do for God but something God does for us: it washes away original sin and gives grace, and grace is a free gift that does not depend on our understanding. The Catholic Church teaches baptism is necessary for salvation, so parents should not delay it. The New Testament records whole households being baptised (Acts 16:15, 16:33), which probably included children, and Jesus said "Let the little children come to me" (Mark 10:14). The child is not left without a choice: at confirmation they make the promises for themselves.

Some Christians, such as Quakers, would reject the statement for a different reason: they do not think outward baptism is needed at any age, because what matters is the inner work of the Holy Spirit.

In conclusion, I think the statement is too strong. The Bible examples of baptism are of adults because the Church was new and most converts were adults, so they do not prove that babies should be excluded; the household baptisms leave the question open. Both sides take baptism seriously and both expect a personal commitment in the end — through believers' baptism or through confirmation. The argument from grace is stronger because Christians believe God's love comes before any human response, so infant baptism is a valid practice, even though believers' baptism may make a more personal commitment.
:::

::: q
Explain two contrasting Christian beliefs about the meaning of the bread and wine in the Eucharist. (4)
---
Catholics believe in transubstantiation (1): at the consecration the substance of the bread and wine becomes the real body and blood of Christ, though they still look and taste the same, so Christ is truly present and hosts are reverenced in the tabernacle (1). Baptists and many evangelicals hold a memorial view (1): the bread and wine are symbols that help believers remember Jesus's sacrifice, following "do this in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19); nothing changes physically (1). (Also creditable: Anglican/Methodist spiritual presence; Orthodox mystery; Lutheran sacramental union.)
:::

::: q
Explain two reasons why baptism is important for Christians. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching in your answer. (5)
---
Two developed reasons (2 + 2) plus a relevant source (1). E.g.: it obeys Jesus's command in the Great Commission to baptise "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19), so it is how people are welcomed into the Church; it washes away sin (original sin for Catholics) and gives the Holy Spirit (Acts 2:38); it unites the Christian with Christ's death and resurrection — "buried with him through baptism into death" so that they "live a new life" (Romans 6:4); it follows Jesus's own example (Matthew 3:13–17).
:::

::: q
Why do Quakers and The Salvation Army not celebrate the sacraments? (2)
---
They believe God's grace is received inwardly and directly through the Spirit, not through outward rituals (1); the whole of life should be lived as a sacrament, and rituals could become empty or divisive — The Salvation Army also wanted to avoid alcohol and not exclude anyone (1).
:::

::: q
"The Eucharist is the most important act of Christian worship." Evaluate this statement. (12)
---
For: Jesus commanded it at the Last Supper — "do this in remembrance of me"; Catholics call it "the source and summit of the Christian life" (Catechism 1324) and believe they receive Christ himself through transubstantiation; Orthodox Divine Liturgy centres on it; it unites Christians as one body (1 Corinthians 10:17); it re-presents Christ's sacrifice, the heart of salvation.
Against: many Protestants celebrate it only monthly and see preaching the Word of God as central; Quakers and The Salvation Army do not celebrate it at all yet live deeply Christian lives; Jesus said the greatest commandments are to love God and neighbour (Mark 12:30–31) — service may matter more than ritual; private prayer and Bible reading may matter more to many individuals.
Justified conclusion, e.g. it is the most important for Catholic, Orthodox and many Anglican Christians, but not for all Christians, so the statement is true only within those traditions. Levels as in the method box.
:::

- Calling Baptists' view of Holy Communion "not believing in it" — they do celebrate it; they understand it as remembrance.
- For "contrasting" questions, giving two points that are really the same (e.g. "water is used" and "it is done in church").
- Saying Protestants have seven sacraments, or that all Christians baptise — Quakers and The Salvation Army have none.
- On the 5-mark, forgetting the **source**, or quoting one that does not fit the point (e.g. Matthew 28:19 for Holy Communion).
- 12-marks that only describe a ceremony — you must **argue** and reach a **justified** conclusion.
:::
