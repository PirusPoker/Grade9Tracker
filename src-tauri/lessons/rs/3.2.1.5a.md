## What it is

This is the first half of **Theme E: Religion, crime and punishment**. It asks *why* people do wrong and how religious and non-religious people respond to them. You need to know: good and evil **intentions and actions** (including whether it can ever be good to cause suffering); the **reasons for crime** — poverty and upbringing, mental illness and addiction, greed and hate, and opposition to an unjust law; **views about people who break the law** for those reasons; and views about **types of crime**, especially **hate crimes, theft and murder**.

The theme also has three "contrasting beliefs" issues — **corporal punishment, the death penalty and forgiveness** — which are taught in the punishment lesson. For those three, a 4-mark answer must contrast **Christianity** (the main religious tradition in Britain) with **one or more other religious traditions** (here, Islam). For everything in this lesson you may also use non-religious views (humanist and atheist), and the 12-mark answer rewards them.

### Crime, sin and the law

- A **crime** is an action that breaks the law of the land and can be punished by the state.
- A **sin** is an action that goes against God's will or religious teaching. Some sins are also crimes (murder, theft); many are not (lying to a friend, envy); and a few crimes might not be sins (breaking an unjust law for a good reason).
- **Law** exists to protect people, keep order and make society fair. Christians and Muslims generally believe they should **obey the law**:
  - Christianity: "Let everyone be subject to the governing authorities, for there is no authority except that which God has established" (**Romans 13:1**). Jesus said, "Give back to Caesar what is Caesar's and to God what is God's" (**Mark 12:17**).
  - Islam: Muslims are told to obey God, the Messenger and "those in authority among you" (**Qur'an 4:59**). Muslims living in Britain regard keeping the law of the country they live in as part of keeping their word.
  - Humanists: laws made democratically, which protect human rights and wellbeing, should be kept because they make a shared life possible — not because a god commands them.

### Good and evil intentions and actions

An **intention** is what you mean to do; an **action** is what you actually do. Religions judge both.

**Christianity**
- God looks at the heart: "People look at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart" (**1 Samuel 16:7**).
- Jesus taught that evil begins inside: anger is the root of murder and lust of adultery (**Matthew 5:21–28**), and evil thoughts, theft and murder come "from within, out of a person's heart" (**Mark 7:21–23**).
- **Human nature**: many Christians (following Augustine, and in Catholic teaching) believe in **original sin** — since the **Fall** of Adam and Eve (**Genesis 3**) humans have a tendency to do wrong: "all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (**Romans 3:23**). Others stress that humans are made in the **image of God** (**Genesis 1:27**) and are basically good; **Quakers** speak of "that of God in everyone".
- Many Christians believe **Satan** tempts people to do evil; others see "the devil" as a way of describing the evil humans choose.
- Humans have **free will** — they are responsible for what they choose.

**Islam**
- "Actions are judged by intentions" (**hadith, Bukhari**): the same action can be good or bad depending on the **niyyah** behind it, and a good intention that is never carried out is still rewarded.
- Humans are born in a state of **fitrah** — a natural inclination to good and to God ("every child is born upon the fitrah", **hadith, Bukhari and Muslim**). There is **no original sin**: Adam repented and God forgave him (**Qur'an 2:37**).
- God inspired every soul with both "its wickedness and its righteousness"; the one who purifies it succeeds (**Qur'an 91:7–10**). Humans have free will: "whoever wills — let him believe; and whoever wills — let him disbelieve" (**18:29**).
- **Shaytan (Iblis)** whispers and tempts people to do evil (**Qur'an 114**; **7:11–18**), but he cannot force anyone: humans are responsible, and every action is recorded for the **Day of Judgement**.

**Non-religious views**
- Humanists judge actions mainly by their **consequences** for human (and animal) wellbeing, and by intentions such as kindness or cruelty. There is no devil and no original sin: people do wrong because of choices, circumstances, upbringing and brain chemistry — which also means those things can be changed.
- A **utilitarian** asks whether an action produces the greatest happiness and the least suffering for the greatest number.

### Can it ever be good to cause suffering?

| Yes, sometimes | No / only with great caution |
|---|---|
| Suffering can lead to a greater good: surgery, a painful injection, a hard training programme, or discipline that teaches a child right from wrong. Christians note that "no discipline seems pleasant at the time" but it later brings a harvest of righteousness (**Hebrews 12:11**). | Jesus taught his followers to love their enemies (**Matthew 5:44**) and not to repay evil with evil: "Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good" (**Romans 12:21**). |
| **Punishment** causes suffering, but it may be just — the state is God's servant to punish wrongdoers (**Romans 13:4**). In Islam, Shari'ah punishments are set by God for the good of society. | **Quakers** and other Christian pacifists believe deliberately harming a person made in God's image is never right; reform works better than pain. |
| Jesus' own suffering on the cross brought salvation — Christians believe God brought the greatest good out of the worst suffering. | Muslims believe only God knows the full purpose of suffering; people must not harm others and call it good, and must never go beyond what is just (**Qur'an 2:190**). |
| Islam teaches that suffering is a **test** that builds patience (**Qur'an 2:155**). | Humanists: causing suffering is only justified if it clearly prevents greater suffering, and it must be proportionate. |
| Some Christians, like **Dietrich Bonhoeffer** (a German Lutheran pastor involved in the plot against Hitler, executed in 1945), believed that using force to stop great evil can be the lesser evil. | Many argue that "the end justifies the means" is dangerous: every criminal can claim a good reason. |

### Reasons for crime

| Reason | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Poverty** | People cannot afford food, heating or rent. | Shoplifting food or nappies; benefit fraud. |
| **Upbringing** | A childhood with neglect, violence, crime or no positive role models can make crime seem normal. | Joining a gang; copying family members. |
| **Mental illness** | A person's judgement or self-control is affected by illness. | Violence during a psychotic episode. |
| **Addiction** | Dependence on drugs, alcohol or gambling drives crime, or people commit crime while intoxicated. | Theft to pay for drugs; drink-driving; violence when drunk. |
| **Greed** | Wanting more money or possessions than you need or have earned. | Fraud, burglary, tax evasion. |
| **Hate** | Hostility towards a person or group. | Assault, hate crime, murder. |
| **Opposition to an unjust law** | Breaking a law to protest against it or change it (civil disobedience). | Rosa Parks refusing to give up her seat (1955); protesters blocking roads. |

Other reasons are also credited in the exam: peer pressure, revenge, jealousy, boredom, and religious belief — including the belief that religious conviction is more important than the law.

### Views about people who break the law for these reasons

**Poverty and upbringing**
- Christians and Muslims feel great **compassion**: the circumstances are not wholly the person's fault, and society shares the blame for leaving them poor. Christians work to tackle the causes — for example food banks run with the **Trussell Trust** and debt help from **Christians Against Poverty**. In Islam, **zakah** and **sadaqah** exist so that nobody is forced into crime by poverty; it is reported that **Caliph Umar** suspended the punishment for theft during a famine.
- But stealing is still wrong: "You shall not steal" (**Exodus 20:15**); "Anyone who has been stealing must steal no longer, but must work… that they may have something to share with those in need" (**Ephesians 4:28**). A poor person should seek help rather than take from others.
- Upbringing: "Start children off on the way they should go, and even when they are old they will not turn from it" (**Proverbs 22:6**). Muslims see raising children well as a duty they will answer for on the Day of Judgement. Humanists stress early intervention, education and youth work.

**Mental illness and addiction**
- UK law treats serious mental illness differently: a court can order hospital treatment instead of prison, and a killing may be treated as manslaughter on the grounds of **diminished responsibility**.
- Christians believe people who are ill need **treatment and care**, not simply punishment — Jesus healed the sick and outcast. The **Salvation Army** runs addiction recovery services.
- In Islam, a hadith teaches that "the pen is lifted" (no sin is recorded) for three people: the sleeper, the child and the one who has lost their reason — so a person who is truly mentally ill is not fully responsible. Intoxicants, however, are forbidden ("the work of Satan", **Qur'an 5:90–91**), so a person who chooses to drink or take drugs remains responsible for crimes they commit while intoxicated.
- Most people accept that addicts need **rehabilitation**, but also that they chose to take the drug at first, so they are partly responsible.

**Greed and hate**
- These attract the **least sympathy**, because they are deliberate choices to harm others for selfish reasons.
- Christianity: "The love of money is a root of all kinds of evil" (**1 Timothy 6:10**); the Tenth Commandment forbids coveting (**Exodus 20:17**); hating someone is like murder in the heart (**1 John 3:15**).
- Islam: do not "consume one another's wealth unjustly" (**Qur'an 4:29**); the true believer loves for his brother what he loves for himself (**hadith, Bukhari and Muslim**).
- Humanists: greed and hate ignore the golden rule — treating others as you would want to be treated — and harm the whole community.

**Opposition to an unjust law**
- Many religious believers think it can be **right to break an unjust law**, especially peacefully. The apostles told the authorities, "We must obey God rather than human beings!" (**Acts 5:29**). **Martin Luther King Jr** (a Baptist minister) wrote from Birmingham Jail in 1963 that people have a moral responsibility to disobey unjust laws — and he accepted prison for doing so.
- In Islam, there is no obedience to any created being in disobedience to God (**hadith**), and Muslims should stand firm for justice (**Qur'an 4:135**). The boxer **Muhammad Ali** refused to be drafted into the US army in 1967 because of his Muslim beliefs; his conviction was overturned by the US Supreme Court in 1971.
- Others warn that if everyone chose which laws to obey there would be chaos; they prefer to campaign **legally** (petitions, voting, lobbying). Most religious believers reject **violence** as a way of protesting. Humanists support peaceful civil disobedience where a law clearly violates human rights, but generally prefer democratic change.

### Types of crime

Crimes are often grouped as crimes **against the person** (assault, murder), **against property** (theft, vandalism, burglary) and **against the state** (terrorism, treason, fraud against the government). Other categories include religious crimes, cyber crime and "victimless" crimes such as illegal drug use.

**Hate crimes**
- In the UK a hate crime is a crime motivated by **hostility or prejudice** based on **race, religion, sexual orientation, disability or transgender identity**. Courts can give a **tougher sentence** when hostility is proven.
- Christianity: everyone is made in the image of God (**Genesis 1:27**); "There is neither Jew nor Gentile… for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (**Galatians 3:28**); Jesus' parable of the **Good Samaritan** (**Luke 10:25–37**) shows love across racial and religious divides.
- Islam: God made humans "peoples and tribes that you may know one another"; the most noble is the most righteous (**Qur'an 49:13**). In his **Farewell Sermon**, Muhammad taught that no Arab is superior to a non-Arab, nor white to black, except by piety. Muslims are also frequent victims of hate crime in Britain; the organisation **Tell MAMA** records anti-Muslim incidents.
- Humanists: hate crimes attack human dignity and equality; they support equality laws and education.

**Theft**
- Christianity: forbidden by the Ten Commandments (**Exodus 20:15**). Christians condemn it but many are sympathetic when it is caused by desperate need, and stress **reparation** (paying back) — like Zacchaeus, who paid back four times what he had cheated (**Luke 19:8**).
- Islam: theft is a serious crime; the Qur'an sets cutting off the hand as a punishment and a deterrent (**Qur'an 5:38**), followed by forgiveness for the one who repents (**5:39**). Scholars attached **strict conditions** (proof, a minimum value, not taken out of need, taken from a secure place). This punishment is **not** used in Britain, and British Muslims keep UK law.
- Humanists: theft harms victims and trust; causes such as poverty should be tackled.

**Murder**
- The most serious crime, because it takes a life, which cannot be restored. In the UK it carries a mandatory **life sentence**.
- Christianity: "You shall not murder" (**Exodus 20:13**); humans are made in the image of God (**Genesis 9:6**); life is sacred and only God should end it. Yet even murderers can be forgiven if they repent.
- Islam: "whoever kills a soul… it is as if he had slain mankind entirely; and whoever saves one — it is as if he had saved mankind entirely" (**Qur'an 5:32**); "do not kill the soul which Allah has forbidden, except by right" (**17:33**). The victim's family may demand **qisas** (equal retribution) or accept **diyah** (compensation) and forgive (**2:178**).
- Humanists: murder is wrong because each person has only one life and it destroys every future they could have had.

::: terms
- **Crime**: an action that breaks the law of the land and can be punished by the state.
- **Sin**: an action or thought that goes against God's will or religious teaching; not every sin is a crime.
- **Evil**: the opposite of good; a force or action that is morally wrong and causes harm.
- **Intention**: what a person means or plans to do; in Islam the niyyah by which actions are judged.
- **Free will**: the ability to choose between right and wrong, for which a person is held responsible.
- **Original sin**: the Christian belief that since the Fall of Adam and Eve all humans have a tendency to sin.
- **Fitrah**: the Islamic belief that every human is born with a natural inclination to good and to God.
- **Hate crime**: a crime motivated by hostility based on race, religion, sexual orientation, disability or transgender identity.
- **Unjust law**: a law that is unfair, discriminates against some people or denies human rights.
- **Civil disobedience**: deliberately and usually peacefully breaking a law to protest against it or change it.
- **Addiction**: a physical or mental dependence on a substance or activity, such as drugs, alcohol or gambling.
:::

::: method
Paper 2 (Thematic studies) has **one five-part question per theme**: 1 + 2 + 4 + 5 + 12 = 24 marks. SPaG (up to **3 marks**) is assessed on the 12-mark question of every theme, and the best of your SPaG marks counts.

1. **1 mark — multiple choice.** Four options A–D; choose one. Typical: which word means breaking God's law (sin), which is an example of a hate crime, which reason for crime is illegal drug use (addiction).
2. **2 marks — "Give two…"** Two short, separate, accurate points, e.g. "Give two reasons why people commit crime" — poverty; addiction. Only your first two answers count, and do not give a *cause* when asked for a *type* of crime (the 2023 mark scheme rejected "jealousy" as a type of crime).
3. **4 marks — "Explain two ways / reasons…", "Explain two contrasting / similar religious beliefs about…".** Two points; each earns 1 for a simple explanation and 2 for a detailed one (develop with an example, evidence or a different view). "You must refer to one or more religious traditions" — name Christianity or Islam. In a **contrasting** question the two points must genuinely differ (e.g. a Christian who supports breaking an unjust law against one who stresses obeying the authorities, Romans 13:1); two similar points only earn one set of marks. The three set "contrasting beliefs" issues for this theme (corporal punishment, death penalty, forgiveness) require Christianity contrasted with another religious tradition.
4. **5 marks — "Explain two… Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching in your answer."** Two developed points (2 + 2) **plus 1** for an accurate source. Useful sources: Romans 13:1, Acts 5:29, 1 Timothy 6:10, Exodus 20:13–15, Genesis 1:27, Galatians 3:28, Ephesians 4:28, Mark 7:21–23; Qur'an 5:32, 5:38–39, 5:90, 49:13, 4:135, 91:7–10; the hadith "actions are judged by intentions".
5. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement. In your answer you: should give reasoned arguments in support of this statement; should give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; should refer to religious arguments; may refer to non-religious arguments; should reach a justified conclusion."** (Plus 3 SPaG.) Levels:
   - **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with reason(s) stated.
   - **Level 2 (4–6)**: one view with a logical chain of reasoning, OR different views each supported by reasons. **Maximum Level 2 if there is no reference to religion.**
   - **Level 3 (7–9)**: reasoned consideration of different points of view, with logical chains of reasoning and **clear reference to religion**.
   - **Level 4 (10–12)**: a well-argued response — different views developed, religion **applied to the issue** (not just quoted), arguments weighed, and a conclusion that follows from them.
   Build each paragraph as a chain: *claim → because → religious teaching → so*. Use Christian and Muslim views on both sides, add a humanist or utilitarian argument, and finish with a conclusion that says which side is stronger and *why*.
:::

Poverty (1). Addiction to drugs or alcohol (1).

::: example 4-mark contrasting
Explain two contrasting religious beliefs about breaking the law to protest against an unjust law. In your answer you must refer to one or more religious traditions. (4)

Some Christians believe they must obey the law, because Paul taught that everyone should be subject to the governing authorities, which are established by God (Romans 13:1) (1). They would campaign to change an unjust law through legal means such as petitions and voting, because breaking the law could lead to disorder (1).

Other Christians believe it is right to break an unjust law, because the apostles said, "We must obey God rather than human beings!" (Acts 5:29) (1). Martin Luther King Jr, a Baptist minister, broke segregation laws peacefully and accepted prison, arguing that people have a moral duty to disobey unjust laws (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark with source
Explain two religious beliefs about hate crimes. Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

Christians believe hate crimes are wrong because every person is made in the image of God (Genesis 1:27) (1 + 1 for source). Attacking someone because of their race, religion or sexuality treats them as less valuable than others, which denies the equal dignity God has given them (1).

Muslims believe hate crimes are wrong because the Qur'an teaches that God made humans into peoples and tribes "that you may know one another", and that the most noble is the most righteous (49:13) (1). Muhammad taught in his Farewell Sermon that no race is superior to another, so hatred based on race or religion goes against God's plan for humanity (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark evaluation
"People who commit crimes because they are poor should not be punished." Evaluate this statement. (12 + 3 SPaG)

**In support**: many Christians would agree, because poverty often leaves people with no real choice. A mother who steals food for her children is acting out of love, not greed, and Jesus showed special compassion for the poor and outcast. Christians believe society shares the blame when it allows poverty, so the right response is to tackle the cause, for example through food banks run with the Trussell Trust, rather than to punish the victim of poverty. Muslims might also agree: zakah exists precisely so that nobody is forced to steal, and Caliph Umar is reported to have suspended the punishment for theft during a famine, which shows that Islamic justice takes need into account. A utilitarian could add that punishing a desperate person causes more suffering and does not remove the reason they stole.

**Against**: however, the law must apply to everyone equally, and theft still harms the victim, who may also be poor. The Ten Commandments say "You shall not steal" (Exodus 20:15) with no exception, and Ephesians 4:28 tells the thief to stop and work honestly instead. Most Christians believe punishment can be fair and loving if it aims at reformation, and Paul taught that the authorities punish wrongdoers as God's servants (Romans 13:4). In Islam, humans have free will and are responsible for their choices; a poor person should ask for help from the mosque or community rather than take from others. If poverty were an automatic excuse, many people might claim it, and victims would lose trust in the law.

**Conclusion**: I disagree with the statement as it stands. Poverty should reduce the punishment and lead to help rather than harsh penalties, as Umar's example shows, but it does not make stealing right, because the victim's rights matter too. The fairest approach is a light, reforming punishment — such as community service with support — combined with tackling the poverty that caused the crime.
:::

"It is never right to break the law." Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should refer to religious arguments, may refer to non-religious arguments, and should reach a justified conclusion. (12)

---

::: watch
- Giving a **cause** of crime (greed, jealousy, poverty) when the question asks for a **type** of crime — the mark scheme refuses these.
- Treating "crime" and "sin" as the same: many sins are not crimes, and breaking an unjust law may be a crime but not a sin.
- Writing "Muslims chop hands off" without the context: say that the Qur'an (5:38) sets it, that strict conditions apply, that repentance is stressed (5:39), and that British Muslims keep UK law.
- In "contrasting" answers, giving two points that agree (e.g. two reasons why murder is wrong). Contrast obeying the law with breaking unjust laws, or original sin with fitrah.
- Forgetting the source in the 5-mark question. Romans 13:1, Acts 5:29, 1 Timothy 6:10, Qur'an 5:32 and 49:13 are easy to learn.
- 12-mark answers with no religion (capped at Level 2) or with only one side. Use Christian and Muslim teaching on both sides and finish with a reasoned judgement, not "it depends".
