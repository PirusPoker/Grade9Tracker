## What it is

This topic covers three of the key beliefs of Islam: **angels (malaikah)**, **predestination (al-Qadr) and human freedom**, and **Akhirah (life after death)**. In Sunni Islam belief in angels, in the Day of Judgement and in predestination are three of the **six articles of faith**. In Shi'a Islam, **Ma'ad** (the Day of Judgement and resurrection) and **Adalat** (God's justice) are two of the **five roots of Usul ad-Din** — and Adalat shapes how Shi'a Muslims understand predestination. Belief in angels is shared by both.

### Angels: their nature

| Feature | What Muslims believe | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Created from light** | Angels were made from light (nur), before humans, who were made from clay | hadith in Sahih Muslim |
| **No free will** | They cannot disobey God; they do exactly what they are commanded | Qur'an 66:6 (angels "do not disobey Allah in what He commands them") |
| **Genderless, do not eat or sleep** | They are not male or female, have no physical needs and never tire of praising God | Qur'an 21:19-20 |
| **Messengers with wings** | God made the angels messengers "having wings, two or three or four" | Qur'an 35:1 |
| **Usually unseen** | They can take human form when God wills, as when Jibril appeared as a man in white | the Hadith of Jibril (Sahih Muslim) |

Because angels have no free will, they are not tested and will not be judged. Humans, who do have free will, are the only creatures who can choose to obey God — which is why Muslims believe humans who choose goodness can rank even above angels.

### Angels: their roles

- **Jibril (Gabriel)** — the angel of revelation, the most important angel. He brought God's words to the prophets and revealed the **Qur'an** to Muhammad over about 23 years, beginning in the Cave of Hira in 610 CE with the command "Recite" (Qur'an 96:1-5). The Qur'an says he brought it down upon the Prophet's heart by God's permission (2:97). He also told Maryam she would give birth to Isa (Jesus) (19:16-21). In the Hadith of Jibril he tested the Prophet's companions on Islam, iman (faith) and ihsan (doing good as if you see God).
- **Mika'il (Michael)** — the angel of mercy and provision. He is responsible for bringing rain and nourishment to the earth and for rewarding good people in this life. He is named alongside Jibril in Qur'an 2:98.
- **Israfil** — will blow the trumpet to announce the Day of Judgement (the Qur'an refers to the trumpet being blown, e.g. 39:68; the angel's name comes from hadith).
- **Izra'il / Malak al-Mawt** — the Angel of Death, who takes each soul at the moment God has decreed (32:11).
- **Kiraman Katibin** — the "noble recorders", two angels on each person's shoulders who write down every good and bad deed (82:10-12; 50:17-18). This record is the book given to each person on the Day of Judgement.
- **Munkar and Nakir** — question the dead in the grave about their Lord, their religion and their prophet (hadith).
- Angels also guard the gates of heaven and hell and pray for believers.

**Influence on Muslims today**: knowing the Kiraman Katibin record everything encourages Muslims to think before they speak and act; many greet the recording angels by turning right and left at the end of salah. Belief in Jibril gives Muslims confidence that the Qur'an is God's own words, not Muhammad's. Belief in Mika'il reminds Muslims that everything they have is provided by God, so they should be thankful and share it.

### Predestination (al-Qadr) and human freedom

**Al-Qadr** is the belief that God has already decided everything that will happen. God is **omniscient** (knows everything, past, present and future) and **omnipotent**; nothing can happen that he has not willed.

- "Indeed, all things We created with predestination." (Qur'an 54:49)
- Everything is written in **al-Lawh al-Mahfuz**, the **Preserved Tablet** (85:21-22; also 57:22 — no disaster strikes except that it is written in a register before God brings it into being).
- "And you do not will except that Allah wills — Lord of the worlds." (81:29)
- This is why Muslims say **insha'Allah** ("if God wills") about the future (18:23-24).

But the Qur'an also teaches that humans are free and responsible:

- God created death and life "to test you [as to] which of you is best in deed" (67:2) — a test only makes sense if the choice is real.
- "Allah will not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves" (13:11).
- The truth has come from God, so "whoever wills — let him believe; and whoever wills — let him disbelieve" (18:29).

**How the two fit together — Sunni and Shi'a views**

| | Sunni Islam | Shi'a Islam |
|---|---|---|
| Place in belief | **Predestination (al-Qadr)** is the sixth article of faith | Not one of the five roots; understood through **Adalat** (God's justice), which is a root |
| Emphasis | God's **power and knowledge**: God wills and creates all things, including human actions | God's **justice**: a just God would not force someone to sin and then punish them |
| Human freedom | Many Sunni scholars (the Ash'ari school) teach that God creates each action but the person **chooses and "acquires"** it (kasb), so they are responsible for it | Humans have genuine free will; God knows what we will choose but does not **cause** our choices |
| Key idea | God **knows** what you will do because he is outside time — knowing is not forcing | God's decree sets the conditions of life (when we are born, die, what happens to us); our moral choices are our own |

A common illustration: a teacher may know which pupil will fail a test because they never revise, but the teacher's knowledge does not make the pupil fail. God's knowledge is perfect, yet the person still makes the choice.

**Relationship to the Day of Judgement**: if God decided everything, is it fair to judge people? Muslims answer that:
- humans are judged on what they **freely chose**, and on their **intentions (niyyah)** — a hadith teaches that actions are judged by intentions (Bukhari 1);
- God is completely **just** (Adalat), so no one is punished for something they could not help — people are judged only on what they were able to do (2:286: God does not burden a soul beyond what it can bear);
- God is also **merciful** and forgives those who repent.

**Influence**: belief in al-Qadr gives Muslims comfort and patience (sabr) in suffering — it is part of God's plan (2:155-156) — and stops them boasting in success. Belief in freedom makes them take responsibility for their actions rather than blaming fate.

### Akhirah (life after death)

**Akhirah** means "the hereafter". Muslims believe this life is a short test and the Akhirah is eternal.

1. **Death and barzakh** — the Angel of Death takes the soul. The soul waits in **barzakh**, a "barrier" state between death and resurrection (23:99-100). Munkar and Nakir question the person in the grave; the righteous rest peacefully, the wicked begin to suffer.
2. **The Last Day** — at a time only God knows, Israfil blows the trumpet, the world ends and all people are raised.
3. **Resurrection** — God will raise people **bodily**. When people doubted this, the Qur'an replied that God is able to put together even their fingertips (75:3-4). If God created humans from nothing, he can recreate them.
4. **Judgement** — every person stands before God with the book of their deeds recorded by the angels. Those given their book in their **right hand** are saved (69:19-24); those given it in their **left hand** will regret it (69:25-29). Deeds are weighed on the scales (mizan): heavy scales lead to a pleasant life, light scales to the fire (101:6-9). "Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it" (99:7-8).
5. **The bridge (as-Sirat)** — hadith describe a narrow bridge over hell that all must cross; the righteous cross it quickly, the wicked fall.
6. **Heaven or hell** — **Jannah (paradise)** is described as gardens beneath which rivers flow, with rivers of water, milk, wine that does not intoxicate and honey (47:15), and peace, with the greatest reward being closeness to God. **Jahannam (hell)** is a place of fire and torment for those who rejected God and did evil (e.g. 4:56).

**Human responsibility and accountability**: humans are God's **khalifah** (stewards) on earth (2:30). They are responsible for how they treat God's creation, other people and themselves, and they are **accountable** — they will have to answer for everything. Muslims believe God's mercy is greater than his anger, and that **Muhammad will intercede (shafa'ah)** for believers on that day; Shi'a Muslims also believe the Imams will intercede.

**Diversity of views**: many Muslims read the descriptions of Jannah and Jahannam **literally** as physical places; others see them as **symbolic** — the real reward is being with God and the real punishment is separation from him, described in images humans can understand. Most Muslims believe some believers may be punished for a time in hell before being admitted to paradise, while those who reject God entirely remain there. Sunni and Shi'a Muslims share these core beliefs; for Shi'a, **Ma'ad** is one of the five roots, stressing that resurrection shows God's justice.

**Influence on Muslims today**: belief in Akhirah affects everyday choices — praying, fasting, being honest, giving zakah, avoiding haram things such as alcohol and gambling — because every action will be weighed. It gives hope in suffering and a sense that injustice in this life will be put right. It also shapes funerals: Muslims are buried (not cremated), facing Makkah, as soon as possible, ready for resurrection.

::: terms
- **Malaikah**: angels — beings created by God from light, with no free will, who serve and obey God completely.
- **Jibril**: the angel of revelation, who brought the Qur'an to the Prophet Muhammad beginning in 610 CE.
- **Mika'il**: the angel of mercy, who brings rain and nourishment to the earth and rewards the righteous.
- **Kiraman Katibin**: the two "noble recorders", angels who write down every good and bad deed a person does.
- **Al-Qadr**: predestination — the belief that God has decided and knows everything that happens.
- **Al-Lawh al-Mahfuz**: the Preserved Tablet, on which God has written everything that will happen.
- **Adalat**: God's justice — one of the five roots of Usul ad-Din in Shi'a Islam.
- **Akhirah**: life after death — the eternal hereafter that follows the Day of Judgement.
- **Barzakh**: the barrier or waiting state between death and the Day of Resurrection.
- **Day of Judgement**: the day when all people will be resurrected and judged by God on their deeds and intentions.
- **Jannah**: paradise — the eternal reward for those who believed in God and did good.
- **Jahannam**: hell — the place of punishment for those who rejected God and did evil.
:::

::: method
Paper 1 (Islam) has two five-part questions: **Q1 on beliefs** (this topic) and **Q2 on practices**. Each is 1 + 2 + 4 + 5 + 12 = 24 marks. SPaG (3 marks) is assessed on the 12-mark question in the **beliefs** section.

1. **1 mark — multiple choice.** Four options A–D; choose one. Read every option — distractors are often other Arabic terms (e.g. Mika'il vs Israfil).
2. **2 marks — "Give two…"** Two short, separate, accurate points. No explanation needed. Only your **first two** answers are marked, so do not list five.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two ways in which belief in … influences Muslims today"** or **"Explain two contrasting beliefs…"**. Two points, each **developed**: 1 mark for a simple point + 1 for a detailed explanation. For **influence** questions the development must show what the Muslim actually **does or feels** because of the belief. For **contrasting** questions the two points must genuinely differ — e.g. the Sunni emphasis on God's power in al-Qadr vs the Shi'a emphasis on Adalat, or literal vs symbolic views of paradise.
4. **5 marks — "Explain two Muslim beliefs about … Refer to sacred writings or another source of Muslim belief and teaching in your answer."** Two developed beliefs (2 + 2) **plus 1 mark** for a relevant source: a Qur'an reference (e.g. 99:7-8, 54:49, 82:10-12), a hadith, or a scholar. Quote or paraphrase accurately — a vague "the Qur'an says be good" will not earn the mark.
5. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement."** Bullets: refer to Muslim teaching; give reasoned arguments **to support** the statement; give reasoned arguments **to support a different point of view**; reach a **justified conclusion**. Levels:
   - **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with a reason.
   - **Level 2 (4–6)**: one view explained with a logical chain, OR different views each with reasons. **Maximum Level 2 if there is no reference to religion.**
   - **Level 3 (7–9)**: different views each developed with logical chains, clear reference to Muslim teaching.
   - **Level 4 (10–12)**: a well-argued response — both sides developed and evaluated (say **why** one argument is stronger), accurate teaching and sources, and a conclusion that follows from what you wrote rather than just repeating one side.
   Structure: argument for (with teaching and a "because… this means…" chain), argument against (same), then weigh them and conclude. Use technical terms spelled correctly for SPaG.
:::

::: example 4-mark influence
Explain two ways in which belief in angels influences Muslims today. (4)

First, Muslims believe the Kiraman Katibin record every deed (1). This influences them to be careful about what they say and do, for example avoiding lies and gossip, because they know it will be in the book they are given on the Day of Judgement (1).

Second, Muslims believe Jibril brought the Qur'an to Muhammad (1). This means they treat the Qur'an as the exact words of God — they learn Arabic to recite it, wash before touching it and follow its teachings in their daily lives (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark with source
Explain two Muslim beliefs about the Day of Judgement. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Muslim belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

Muslims believe that on the Day of Judgement all people will be resurrected bodily (1). The Qur'an says God is able to put together even a person's fingertips (75:3-4), so no one can escape being raised to face judgement (1).

Muslims also believe everyone will be judged on their deeds, which are weighed on the scales (1). Nothing is too small to count: "whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it" (Qur'an 99:7-8) (1 for source). Those whose good deeds are heavy will go to Jannah and those whose scales are light will go to Jahannam (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark evaluation
"If God has already decided everything, humans cannot be responsible for what they do." Evaluate this statement. (12)

**For the statement**: Muslims believe in al-Qadr — the Qur'an says "all things We created with predestination" (54:49) and that humans "do not will except that Allah wills" (81:29). Everything is already written in the Preserved Tablet. If God is omnipotent and has decreed every event, then a person's choices seem to be part of that plan, so it could seem unfair to punish someone for doing what God decreed. Some people (including some atheists) argue that if God knows everything in advance, the future is fixed and free will is an illusion.

**Against the statement**: the Qur'an also stresses freedom — life is a test "which of you is best in deed" (67:2), and a test is meaningless unless the choice is real. Many Sunni scholars explain that God's knowledge is not the same as forcing: God, being outside time, knows what we will freely choose, just as a teacher can know a pupil will fail without making them fail. Shi'a Muslims stress Adalat: a just God would never force someone to sin and then punish them, so humans must be truly free. Judgement is also based on intention (niyyah), which is the person's own, and God does not burden a soul beyond what it can bear (2:286).

**Conclusion**: I disagree with the statement. Although al-Qadr shows that God knows and allows everything, the argument that knowing is not causing is stronger, because it keeps both God's power and his justice. If humans were not responsible, the Day of Judgement — a central belief — would be unjust, and Islam teaches that God is perfectly just. So Muslims hold that humans are responsible, even if exactly how this fits with predestination is ultimately a mystery known fully only to God.
:::

::: example 4-mark contrasting
Explain two contrasting Muslim beliefs about predestination. (4)

Many Sunni Muslims emphasise God's power: al-Qadr is an article of faith, and God wills and creates everything that happens, including human actions (1). Humans "acquire" their actions by choosing them, so they are responsible, but nothing happens outside God's will (1).

Shi'a Muslims emphasise Adalat (God's justice) instead (1). They believe God knows what people will choose but does not cause their choices, because it would be unjust to force someone to sin and then punish them on the Day of Judgement (1).
:::
