## What it is

Theme B asks how the universe began, why the world matters, and how humans should treat the environment and animals. You need **Christian** and **Muslim** teachings (with differences inside each), plus **non-religious** views (atheist and humanist), and the scientific ideas they respond to. **Animal experimentation** is one of the three Theme B issues on which you must be able to explain **contrasting beliefs**: the specification requires **Christianity and at least one other religious tradition** (here, Islam). Differences within Christianity and a non-religious view can be added, but a non-religious view can never be the only contrast.

### The origins of the universe

**The scientific view — the Big Bang.** About **13.8 billion years ago** the universe began from an extremely hot, dense point and has been expanding and cooling ever since. The evidence: light from distant galaxies is **red-shifted**, showing they are moving away (Edwin Hubble, 1929); and the **cosmic microwave background radiation**, the "afterglow" of the Big Bang, was detected in 1965. The theory was first proposed in 1927 by **Georges Lemaître**, a Belgian physicist who was also a **Catholic priest**. Science explains *how* the universe developed; it does not say *why* there is a universe at all, or what (if anything) caused the Big Bang.

**Christian teaching.** "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth" (Genesis 1:1). Genesis 1 describes God creating over six days by his word ("And God said, 'Let there be light'", Genesis 1:3), ending with humans made in his image, and the verdict that everything was "very good" (Genesis 1:31). John 1:1-3 adds that "through him [the Word] all things were made".

| Christian interpretation | Belief | Relationship with the Big Bang |
|---|---|---|
| **Literal / creationist** (young-earth) | Genesis is historical fact: six 24-hour days, a universe only thousands of years old | the Big Bang conflicts with scripture and is rejected; held by some evangelical and fundamentalist Protestants |
| **"Day-age" / old-earth** | the "days" are long ages — "with the Lord a day is like a thousand years" (2 Peter 3:8) | the order of Genesis broadly matches the long history science describes |
| **Non-literal / theistic** | Genesis is a symbolic story teaching religious truths: God is creator, creation is good and ordered, humans have a special place | the Big Bang is *how* God created; God caused it and sustains it. Held by the **Catholic Church**, the **Church of England** and most British Christians |

Many Christians argue the Big Bang *supports* belief in God: something must have caused it (the First Cause argument), and the fine-tuning of the universe for life suggests design. Pope Pius XII welcomed the theory in 1951.

**Muslim teaching.** Allah is the sole creator (**Al-Khaliq**). He creates simply by his command: "When He decrees a matter, He only says to it, 'Be,' and it is" (Qur'an 2:117; see also 36:82). The Qur'an says Allah created the heavens and the earth in **six days** (Qur'an 7:54), but most Muslims understand these as **periods** rather than 24-hour days, since "a day with your Lord is like a thousand years of what you count" (Qur'an 22:47).

- Many Muslims see the Qur'an as **compatible** with the Big Bang: Qur'an 21:30 says the heavens and the earth were "joined together" and then Allah "split them apart", and Qur'an 51:47 says Allah built the heaven and is "expanding it". They see this as the Qur'an revealing truths that science discovered centuries later.
- Some Muslims are cautious about tying the Qur'an to any scientific theory, which might change; others reject any account that leaves Allah out. All Muslims agree that the universe exists because **Allah willed it**, not by chance.

**Non-religious views.** Atheists and humanists accept the Big Bang because of the evidence. Many argue the universe needs no creator: physicists such as **Stephen Hawking** argued the laws of physics could explain its beginning without God. Humanists see the universe as unplanned, but still wonderful — they feel awe at it without seeing it as created.

### The value of the world and the duty to protect it

| Idea | Meaning | Christian source | Muslim source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dominion** | God has given humans authority or rule over the rest of creation | God tells humans to "fill the earth and subdue it" and rule over the fish, birds and animals (Genesis 1:26-28) | humans are honoured above much of creation and nature is made of service to them (Qur'an 45:13) |
| **Stewardship** | the duty to care for the world on God's behalf, as a manager looks after property for its owner | God put Adam in Eden "to work it and take care of it" (Genesis 2:15); "The earth is the Lord's, and everything in it" (Psalm 24:1) | humans are **khalifah** — trustees or vicegerents on earth (Qur'an 2:30; 6:165) |
| **Responsibility** | humans will answer to God for how they treated creation | the Parable of the Talents (Matthew 25:14-30) — people must use what God entrusts to them well; judgement | **akhirah** — on the Day of Judgement people account for their deeds, including how they used Allah's creation |
| **Awe and wonder** | a sense of amazement at the beauty and scale of nature, pointing to its creator | "The heavens declare the glory of God" (Psalm 19:1); Psalm 8 | nature is full of **ayat** (signs) of Allah for those who reflect (Qur'an 3:190) |

**How dominion is understood.** Most Christians today say dominion does **not** mean humans may exploit nature for themselves: it means ruling as God would, like a good king caring for his people. In the past some used Genesis 1:28 to justify using nature without limit. **Pope Francis**, in the encyclical ***Laudato Si'*** (2015), rejected that reading and called the earth "our common home", linking care for the environment with care for the poor who suffer most from its destruction.

**Islam: tawhid, khalifah and mizan.** Because of **tawhid** (the oneness of Allah) all creation belongs to Allah and forms a unity; humans are its trustees, not owners. Allah created everything in **balance** (**mizan**) and commands humans not to upset it (Qur'an 55:7-9). A Hadith records the Prophet saying that the world is sweet and green, and that Allah has made humans trustees in it to see how they act (Muslim). He also taught that planting a tree from which people or animals eat is a form of charity (Bukhari).

**Non-religious views.** Humanists have no religious duty, but argue that this is the only world we have, that future generations have a right to a healthy planet, and that reason and science show how urgently it must be protected. Many atheists share the same sense of **responsibility** without believing it is owed to God.

### Use and abuse of the environment

| Problem | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Use of natural resources** | non-renewable resources (oil, coal, gas, minerals) are running out; deforestation destroys habitats and releases carbon |
| **Pollution** | burning fossil fuels causes **climate change**; plastic pollutes the oceans; chemicals poison land and rivers; air pollution damages health |
| **Consumerism and waste** | richer countries use far more than their share, while poorer countries suffer the worst effects |

Religious and non-religious responses all support **sustainable development** — meeting present needs without harming future generations — through recycling, renewable energy, and reducing consumption.

- **Christian responses**: stewardship means not wasting God's gifts; many churches join **Eco Church**, run by the Christian conservation charity **A Rocha UK**; the Church of England has set a target of net zero carbon by 2030; **Christian Aid** campaigns on climate change because it harms the world's poorest. *Laudato Si'* calls for "ecological conversion".
- **Muslim responses**: the Qur'an warns that corruption has appeared on land and sea because of what people have done (Qur'an 30:41) and says "eat and drink, but be not excessive" (Qur'an 7:31). The Prophet taught against wasting water even when washing at a flowing river (Hadith, Ibn Majah). The **Islamic Foundation for Ecology and Environmental Sciences (IFEES)**, founded in Britain by Fazlun Khalid, runs conservation projects and teaches Islamic environmental ethics.
- **Non-religious responses**: humanists argue from science, from fairness to future generations and from the well-being of all living things.

### The use and abuse of animals

**Christian teaching.** Humans are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27) and have a higher status than animals, and after the Flood God gave animals for food (Genesis 9:3). But animals are God's creatures and he cares for them: not one sparrow falls "outside your Father's care" (Matthew 10:29), and "the righteous care for the needs of their animals" (Proverbs 12:10). The Catholic Church teaches that animals are God's creatures and may be used for food and clothing, but "it is contrary to human dignity to cause animals to suffer or die needlessly" (Catechism 2418).

**Muslim teaching.** Animals are Allah's creation and form "communities like you" (Qur'an 6:38). The Prophet taught kindness to animals: a man was forgiven for giving water to a thirsty dog, while a woman was punished for shutting up a cat until it starved (both Hadith, Bukhari). Humans are khalifah and will answer for cruelty on the Day of Judgement.

**Animals for food**

| View | Belief |
|---|---|
| **Most Christians** | eating meat is permitted (Genesis 9:3; in Acts 10 Peter's vision declares all foods clean), but factory farming that causes needless suffering is wrong; many choose free-range or reduce meat |
| **Some Christians** | are vegetarian or vegan, believing Eden was vegetarian (Genesis 1:29) and that stewardship means not killing when there is no need; **Seventh-day Adventists** encourage a vegetarian diet |
| **Muslims** | meat must be **halal** (permitted): the animal is killed quickly with a sharp knife, in the name of Allah (the tasmiyah), and should not see other animals slaughtered. Pork, blood and carrion are **haram** (Qur'an 2:173). Muslims may be vegetarian, but meat is not forbidden |
| **Non-religious** | views vary; many humanists and atheists eat meat, while others, such as the philosopher **Peter Singer**, argue that causing animals to suffer when we do not need to eat them is wrong |

**Animal experimentation** — using live animals in scientific research. In the UK it is regulated by the **Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986**; testing cosmetics on animals has been banned in the UK since **1998**. Scientists follow the **3Rs**: Replace animals where possible, Reduce the numbers, Refine methods to reduce suffering.

| Arguments for | Arguments against |
|---|---|
| it has led to vaccines and treatments (e.g. insulin, many modern medicines) that save millions of lives | animals feel pain and fear; it is cruel to cause suffering to creatures that cannot consent |
| it is legally regulated and the 3Rs limit suffering | animals react differently from humans, so results may be unreliable |
| human life is of greater value than animal life (for many religious people, because humans are made in God's image) | alternatives exist: cell cultures, computer models, human volunteers |
| testing new medicines safely protects human volunteers | cosmetics and other non-essential products do not justify any suffering |

**Contrasting beliefs on animal experimentation**

- **Catholic Church**: experimentation is morally acceptable "if it remains within reasonable limits" and contributes to caring for or saving human lives (Catechism 2417); needless suffering is wrong. Many Anglicans and other Protestants take a similar view.
- **Some Christians** (for example many who campaign for animal welfare) oppose all experiments because they cause suffering to God's creatures, and argue stewardship means protecting the vulnerable.
- **Islam**: experimentation is generally permitted when it is **necessary** for human health, the animal is treated as humanely as possible and suffering is minimised; unnecessary tests, such as for cosmetics, are cruel and wrong. The principle is that the benefit must be real and there must be no alternative.
- **Non-religious**: humanists usually judge by consequences — acceptable when it clearly saves lives and there is no alternative, wrong for trivial purposes. **Peter Singer** argues that favouring humans simply because they are human is **speciesism**, like racism, so most experiments are wrong.

::: terms
- **Big Bang theory**: the scientific theory that the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago from a hot, dense point and has been expanding ever since.
- **Creationism**: the belief that Genesis is literally true — God made the universe in six days, a few thousand years ago.
- **Dominion**: the God-given authority of humans to rule over the rest of creation (Genesis 1:26-28).
- **Stewardship**: the duty to look after the world responsibly on God's behalf, because it belongs to him.
- **Khalifah**: the Muslim belief that humans are trustees or vicegerents of Allah's creation, responsible for caring for it.
- **Awe and wonder**: a feeling of amazement and respect at the beauty and scale of nature, which for believers points to God.
- **Sustainable development**: meeting the needs of people today without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
- **Animal experimentation**: using live animals in scientific or medical research, for example to test new medicines.
- **Speciesism**: Peter Singer's term for treating the interests of other species as less important simply because they are not human.
- **Halal**: permitted in Islam; for meat, an animal killed quickly with a sharp knife in the name of Allah.
:::

::: method
**Paper 2A gives each theme one five-part question: 1, 2, 4, 5 and 12 marks (24 in all).** Allow about 25 minutes per theme.

1. **1 mark — multiple choice**: four options A–D; choose one. Watch for near-misses such as dominion and stewardship.
2. **2 marks — "Give two..."**: two short, correct, *different* points. Only the first two answers are marked. No explanation needed.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two contrasting (or similar) religious beliefs about..." — "In your answer you must refer to one or more religious traditions."** Two points, each developed: 1 mark for a simple explanation, 2 for a detailed one. For **animal experimentation** (a named "contrasting beliefs" issue in Theme B) the specification requires **Christianity and at least one other religious tradition**, so set a Christian view against a Muslim one — e.g. Christians who oppose all testing because it harms God's creatures against the Muslim view that it is permitted when necessary for human health. A contrast within Christianity (Catholic "within reasonable limits") or a humanist view can add depth, but must never replace the religious contrast. If the two beliefs are really the same, only one is credited. If the question says "similar", two contrasting beliefs lose marks the same way.
4. **4 marks — "Explain two ways..." / "Explain two reasons..."**: the same 2 + 2 pattern — say the point, then develop it with detail, an example or its effect on believers.
5. **5 marks — "Explain two... Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching in your answer."** Two developed points (4 marks) plus **1 mark for a relevant, accurate source**: Genesis 2:15, Psalm 24:1, Qur'an 2:30 or 6:38, the Catholic Catechism, *Laudato Si'*, a Hadith. A close paraphrase with the reference is fine; link it to your point.
6. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should give reasoned arguments in support of this statement; should give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; should refer to religious arguments; may refer to non-religious arguments; should reach a justified conclusion."** Plus **3 SPaG** marks (the best SPaG mark on the paper counts). Spell stewardship, environment, experimentation and khalifah correctly.

**How the 12-mark levels are reached** (the whole answer goes in one level):
- **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with reasons stated.
- **Level 2 (4–6)**: one point of view argued with a logical chain of reasoning, **or** different views recognised, each with reasons. **Capped at Level 2 with no reference to religion.**
- **Level 3 (7–9)**: reasoned consideration of **different** points of view, with logical chains that draw on knowledge, and **clear reference to religion**.
- **Level 4 (10–12)**: a well-argued response — different views weighed with logical chains **leading to a justified judgement**, and religion *applied* to the issue.

A reliable plan: two paragraphs for (point, explanation, teaching, evaluation of how strong it is), two against, then a conclusion that decides *which side is stronger and why* rather than repeating yourself. Use a non-religious view (humanist, Peter Singer) as one of your "different points of view" — it strengthens the answer but cannot replace religion.
:::

::: example 4-mark contrasting beliefs
Explain two contrasting religious beliefs about animal experimentation. In your answer you must refer to one or more religious traditions. (4)

Some Christians believe all animal experiments are wrong (1). They argue that animals are God's creatures — not one sparrow falls outside God's care (Matthew 10:29) — and that stewardship means protecting the vulnerable rather than causing pain, especially when alternatives such as computer models and cell cultures exist, so they support campaigns to end animal testing (1).

In contrast, many Muslims believe animal experiments are permitted when they are necessary for human health (1). Humans are khalifah with a higher status than animals, so testing a new medicine that could save lives can be justified, provided the animal is treated as humanely as possible and there is no alternative; testing cosmetics would be unnecessary cruelty and wrong (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark with a source
Explain two reasons why many religious people believe they have a duty to look after the world. Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

Christians believe that the world belongs to God, not to humans (1). Psalm 24:1 says "The earth is the Lord's, and everything in it", so humans are stewards who look after the earth on God's behalf, like tenants who must return a property in good condition, and they will have to answer to God for how they treated it (1) (source 1).

Muslims believe that Allah has made humans khalifah, trustees of his creation (1). Allah created the world in perfect balance (mizan), so pollution and waste upset what he designed; on the Day of Judgement people will be held accountable for this, which is why organisations such as IFEES encourage Muslims to conserve the environment (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark evaluation
"Religion and science can never agree about the origins of the universe." Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should give reasoned arguments in support of this statement; should give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; should refer to religious arguments; may refer to non-religious arguments; should reach a justified conclusion. (12)

Some people agree. Creationist Christians read Genesis 1 literally: God made everything in six 24-hour days only a few thousand years ago. The Big Bang theory says the universe is about 13.8 billion years old and developed over billions of years, so the two accounts cannot both be literally true. Creationists believe the Bible is the inspired word of God and cannot be wrong, so they reject the science where it disagrees. Some atheists agree from the other side: Stephen Hawking argued the laws of physics can explain the beginning of the universe without a creator, so science makes God unnecessary.

However, many religious people disagree. The Catholic Church and the Church of England read Genesis as a non-literal account teaching religious truths — that God is the creator and that creation is good. Science explains how the universe developed; religion explains why it exists at all. The Big Bang was first proposed by Georges Lemaître, who was a Catholic priest, which shows that a believer can hold both. Many Muslims go further and argue that the Qur'an agrees with the Big Bang: Qur'an 21:30 describes the heavens and earth being joined and then split apart, and Qur'an 51:47 describes Allah expanding the heaven. The Big Bang also leaves a question science cannot answer — what caused it — and believers say the answer is God.

In conclusion, I disagree. There is a real conflict between science and a literal reading of Genesis, so for creationists the statement is true. But "never" is too strong: most Christians and Muslims in Britain accept the Big Bang as the way God created, and science and religion answer different questions — how and why. Because the majority of believers find no contradiction, religion and science clearly can agree.

(Level 4: different views argued with logical chains, accurate Christian and Muslim teaching applied, non-religious view used, and a justified judgement.)
:::
