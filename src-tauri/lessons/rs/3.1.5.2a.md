## What it is

This topic covers the **duties** that shape a Muslim's life, and the first two in detail: **Shahadah** (declaring faith) and **salah** (prayer). Islam means "submission" to God, and Muslims believe these duties are how that submission is lived out every day. Sunni and Shi'a Muslims share most of the duties but organise them differently.

### The Five Pillars of Sunni Islam

A well-known hadith (in Bukhari and Muslim) says that Islam is built on five things. Like the pillars of a building, they hold up a Muslim's life.

| Pillar | Meaning | In brief |
|---|---|---|
| **Shahadah** | declaration of faith | "There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah" |
| **Salah** | prayer | praying five times a day at set times, facing Makkah |
| **Zakah** | almsgiving | giving 2.5% of savings above a minimum level each year to those in need |
| **Sawm** | fasting | fasting from dawn to sunset during the month of Ramadan |
| **Hajj** | pilgrimage | going to Makkah once in a lifetime, if healthy and able to afford it |

### The Ten Obligatory Acts of Shi'a Islam

Shi'a Muslims call their practical duties the **Furu ad-Din** ("branches of religion"). Their beliefs are the five roots (Usul ad-Din); the ten acts grow from those roots.

| Act | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Salah** | prayer |
| **Sawm** | fasting in Ramadan |
| **Zakah** | almsgiving |
| **Khums** | a 20% tax on surplus income, half for religious causes through Shi'a religious leaders and half for poor descendants of the Prophet |
| **Hajj** | pilgrimage to Makkah |
| **Jihad** | struggle in God's way — above all the inner struggle against evil; also defending Islam |
| **Amr-bil-Ma'ruf** | commanding what is good — encouraging others to do right |
| **Nahi Anil Munkar** | forbidding what is evil — discouraging wrongdoing |
| **Tawalla** | showing love for God, the Prophet, the Imams and those who follow them |
| **Tabarra** | turning away from the enemies of God and from evil |

**Key similarities and differences**

- Four pillars — **salah, sawm, zakah and hajj** — appear in both lists.
- **Shahadah** is not one of the ten acts, but it is not less important for Shi'a Muslims: its content (Tawhid and prophethood) is part of the five roots of belief, and Shi'a Muslims say it too.
- The Shi'a list adds **Khums**, the duties of **commanding good and forbidding evil**, and **tawalla and tabarra**.
- **Jihad** is one of the ten acts. Sunni Muslims do not list it as a pillar, but they too see jihad as a duty — the "greater jihad" of living a good life, and the "lesser jihad" of defending Islam under strict conditions. Some even call it an unofficial "sixth pillar". (Jihad is covered fully in a later topic.)
- Commanding good and forbidding evil are not Sunni pillars, but Sunni Muslims also hold them as duties, based on Qur'an 3:104 (let there be a community "inviting to [all that is] good, enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong").

### Shahadah: the declaration of faith

In Arabic: **"La ilaha illallah, Muhammadur rasulullah"** — "There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah."

- The first half expresses **Tawhid**, the oneness of God; the second, **Risalah** — accepting Muhammad as God's final messenger and so the Qur'an and his Sunnah as guidance. The Qur'an says, "know that there is no deity except Allah" (47:19).
- Many **Shi'a** Muslims add **"Aliyun waliyullah"** — "and Ali is the friend (wali) of God" — expressing their belief in the imamate. Most Shi'a scholars teach that this is recommended but not an obligatory part of the declaration.

**Its place in Muslim practice**

- **Becoming a Muslim**: a person becomes a Muslim by sincerely reciting the Shahadah, with understanding and belief, usually in front of Muslim witnesses.
- **Birth**: the father or another relative whispers the adhan (call to prayer), which contains the Shahadah, into a newborn baby's right ear, so these are the first words it hears.
- **Every day**: it is part of the **adhan** called five times a day, and is recited in every salah (in the tashahhud, the sitting part of the prayer).
- **Death**: Muslims hope that it will be the last words they say, and it is said to the dying.
- It is a statement to live by, not just words: rejecting **shirk** (associating anything with God) in everything a person does.

### Salah: prayer

**Why Muslims pray**

- It is **commanded by God**: "prayer has been decreed upon the believers a decree of specified times" (4:103). Muslims believe the number of five daily prayers was given to Muhammad during the **Night Journey** (al-Isra wal-Mi'raj).
- It keeps God at the centre of life, like a regular conversation with him, and is an act of **submission**.
- It makes people better: "Indeed, prayer prohibits immorality and wrongdoing" (29:45).
- It brings forgiveness: a hadith (Bukhari and Muslim) compares praying five times a day to washing in a river five times a day — no dirt would remain.
- It unites the **ummah** (worldwide Muslim community): Muslims everywhere face the same direction and use the same words in Arabic.
- A hadith (in Tirmidhi) says salah is the first deed a person will be asked about on the Day of Judgement.

**Times**

| Prayer | Time | Rak'ahs (obligatory) |
|---|---|---|
| **Fajr** | between dawn and sunrise | 2 |
| **Zuhr** | just after midday | 4 |
| **Asr** | afternoon | 4 |
| **Maghrib** | just after sunset | 3 |
| **Isha** | night | 4 |

Times move with the sun, so Muslims use a timetable or an app. The **adhan** (call to prayer) is given by the **mu'adhin** (muezzin), traditionally from the minaret. Missed prayers should be made up later (qada). Travellers may shorten four-rak'ah prayers to two (4:101).

**Direction**: Muslims face the **qiblah** — the Ka'aba in Makkah. At first Muslims faced Jerusalem, but God told Muhammad, "So turn your face toward al-Masjid al-Haram" (2:144). In a mosque, the **mihrab**, a niche in the wall, shows the direction; at home Muslims may use a compass or app.

**Preparation — wudu (ablution)**

The Qur'an commands: "When you rise to [perform] prayer, wash your faces and your forearms to the elbows and wipe over your heads and wash your feet to the ankles" (5:6). A typical order is:
1. make the **niyyah** (intention);
2. wash the hands, then rinse the mouth and nose;
3. wash the face;
4. wash the right arm then the left up to the elbow;
5. wipe the head with wet hands, and the ears;
6. wash the feet up to the ankles.

- **Shi'a** Muslims wash the face and arms (from elbow to fingertips) and then **wipe** the head and the tops of the feet with the water left on the hands, rather than washing the feet.
- If no clean water is available, Muslims may perform **tayammum** — dry purification with clean earth or dust (5:6).
- A full bath (**ghusl**) is required in some circumstances, e.g. after sex or menstruation.
- Wudu is physical and spiritual: it prepares the mind to stand before God. Muslims also pray in a clean place (often on a prayer mat), in clean clothes, with the body modestly covered, and remove shoes.

**The movements and recitations of a rak'ah**

A **rak'ah** is one complete sequence of set movements and words:

1. **Takbir** — standing facing the qiblah with the niyyah made, the worshipper raises the hands to the ears and says **"Allahu Akbar"** (God is the greatest), shutting out the world.
2. **Qiyam** (standing) — recites **Surah al-Fatihah** (Surah 1), which is said in every rak'ah, often followed by another passage of the Qur'an.
3. **Ruku** (bowing) — hands on knees, saying "Glory be to my Lord, the Most Great", showing respect.
4. Standing again — "God hears those who praise him".
5. **Sujud** (prostration) — forehead, nose, palms, knees and toes touching the ground, saying "Glory be to my Lord, the Most High". This is the position of greatest humility and closeness to God. It is done twice, with a short sitting between.
6. After every second rak'ah and at the end the worshipper **sits** to recite the **tashahhud**, which includes the Shahadah, and prayers for the Prophet.
7. **Taslim** — the prayer ends by turning the head to the right and to the left, saying "Peace be upon you and the mercy of God", greeting the angels and fellow worshippers.

After the set prayer, Muslims may offer **du'a** — personal prayers in their own words and language — and may use prayer beads (subhah) to recite the names of God.

**Where Muslims pray**

- **Mosque (masjid)**: worshippers stand shoulder to shoulder in straight rows behind the **imam**, which shows that all are equal before God. Men and women usually pray in separate rows or areas. A hadith (Bukhari) says praying in congregation is worth twenty-seven times praying alone.
- **Home**: salah can be prayed anywhere clean. Many Muslims pray at home, especially women, and families may pray together; homes often have a prayer mat and a compass or app for the qiblah.
- **Elsewhere**: in a school or workplace prayer room, at a station or in a park. A hadith says the earth has been made a place of prayer (a masjid) for Muslims.

**Jummah — Friday prayer**

- "O you who have believed, when [the adhan] is called for the prayer on the day of Jumu'ah, then proceed to the remembrance of Allah and leave trade" (62:9).
- Jummah is held at midday on Friday in place of Zuhr. It has **two rak'ahs** and a **khutbah** (sermon) from the imam, often about how to live as a Muslim or current issues.
- In Sunni Islam it is compulsory for adult men and optional for women. Some Shi'a scholars have taught that Friday prayer is not strictly compulsory while the Twelfth Imam is hidden, though many Shi'a attend it.
- In Britain many Muslims take a long lunch break or arrange with employers to attend; Friday is not a day of rest, and Muslims return to work afterwards.

**Key differences between Sunni and Shi'a practice**

| | Sunni | Shi'a |
|---|---|---|
| Prayer times | five separate times | may **combine** Zuhr with Asr and Maghrib with Isha, praying at **three** times of the day (still five prayers) |
| Hands | usually folded over the chest or stomach (though many Maliki Sunnis pray with arms at their sides) | arms by the sides |
| Prostration | forehead on the mat or floor | forehead on a **turbah** (mohr) — a small tablet of natural clay, often from Karbala — because prostration should be on natural earth |
| Wudu | feet washed | head and feet wiped |
| Adhan | standard wording | adds "Come to the best of deeds" and often a phrase affirming Ali as the wali of God |

Shi'a Muslims explain combining prayers with hadith that Muhammad sometimes combined prayers even when not travelling; Sunni Muslims mostly combine only when travelling or in special need.

**Different Muslim views about the importance of prayer**

- **The most important duty after Shahadah**: most Muslims see salah as essential and non-negotiable — a hadith (in Muslim) says that what stands between a person and disbelief is abandoning prayer.
- **Prayer and intention**: many emphasise that prayer said mechanically has little value; God looks at the heart (niyyah) and what matters is real concentration (khushu). The Qur'an warns against those who are heedless of their prayer (107:4-5).
- **Prayer and action**: some Muslims argue that living ethically — honesty, charity, helping others — is equally part of worship, and prayer must lead to good behaviour (29:45). A Muslim who prays but cheats others has missed the point.
- **Inner prayer**: Sufi Muslims especially value **dhikr** (continual remembrance of God) and personal du'a alongside the set prayers.
- **In modern Britain**: some Muslims find it hard to pray on time at school or work and may make up prayers later or combine them; others see keeping to the times, despite difficulty, as the real test of faith.

::: terms
- **Five Pillars**: the five key duties of Sunni Islam: Shahadah, salah, zakah, sawm and hajj.
- **Ten Obligatory Acts**: the practical duties (Furu ad-Din) of Shi'a Islam, including Khums, jihad, tawalla and tabarra.
- **Shahadah**: the declaration of faith: "There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah."
- **Salah**: the compulsory prayer performed five times a day at set times, facing Makkah.
- **Wudu**: ritual washing before prayer, as commanded in Qur'an 5:6, preparing body and mind.
- **Qiblah**: the direction of the Ka'aba in Makkah, which Muslims face when they pray.
- **Rak'ah**: one complete sequence of set movements and recitations in salah.
- **Jummah**: the Friday midday congregational prayer, with a sermon (khutbah), held in place of Zuhr.
- **Adhan**: the call to prayer, given by the mu'adhin five times a day, containing the Shahadah.
- **Turbah**: a small tablet of natural clay on which Shi'a Muslims place their forehead in prostration.
- **Du'a**: personal, informal prayer in a Muslim's own words, alongside the set salah.
:::

::: method
Paper 1 (Islam) has two five-part questions: **Q1 Beliefs** and **Q2 Practices** (this topic). Each is 1 + 2 + 4 + 5 + 12 = 24 marks. SPaG is assessed on the Beliefs 12-mark, **not** on this Practices 12-mark — but clear, accurate writing still helps you reach Level 4.

1. **1 mark — multiple choice.** Four options A–D; tick one. Typical: the name for Friday prayer (Jummah), the direction of prayer (qiblah), the number of rak'ahs, which act is not one of the Five Pillars.
2. **2 marks — "Give two…"** Two short, separate, accurate points (e.g. "Give two ways in which a Muslim prepares for salah" — wudu; facing the qiblah; making niyyah; using a clean prayer mat). Only your first two count.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two ways / reasons…" or "Explain two contrasting ways…"** Two points, each 1 mark for a simple explanation and 2 for a detailed one. Good contrasts here: **Sunni and Shi'a** practice of salah (combining prayers, the turbah, arms by the sides), praying in the **mosque vs at home**, or the **Five Pillars vs the Ten Obligatory Acts**. Two points that say the same thing earn only one set of marks.
4. **5 marks — "Explain two… Refer to sacred writings or another source of Muslim belief and teaching in your answer."** Two developed points (2 + 2) **plus 1** for an accurate source, e.g. 4:103 (prayer at fixed times), 29:45 (prayer prevents wrongdoing), 5:6 (wudu), 2:144 (qiblah), 62:9 (Jummah), 47:19 (no god but Allah), or the hadith of the river or of congregational prayer.
5. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should: refer to Muslim teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion."** Levels:
   - **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with a reason.
   - **Level 2 (4–6)**: one view with a logical chain of reasoning, OR different views each with a reason. **Maximum Level 2 if there is no reference to religion.**
   - **Level 3 (7–9)**: different points of view each developed with logical chains, with **clear reference to Muslim teaching**.
   - **Level 4 (10–12)**: a well-argued response — both sides developed, accurate teaching and practice, arguments **evaluated** (which is stronger and why) and a justified conclusion.
   Practice statements often ask about importance ("Salah is the most important pillar") or modern life ("It is too difficult to pray five times a day in Britain"). Use the different Muslim views about prayer, and Sunni/Shi'a differences, as your "different points of view".
:::

::: example 5-mark with source
Explain two reasons why salah is important to Muslims. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Muslim belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

Salah is a command from God, so praying is an act of obedience and submission (1). The Qur'an says, "prayer has been decreed upon the believers a decree of specified times" (4:103), so Muslims pray five times a day at set times, whatever else they are doing (1 + 1 for source).

Salah also helps Muslims live better lives (1). Remembering God five times a day keeps them conscious that he sees everything, which helps them avoid wrongdoing; the Qur'an teaches that "prayer prohibits immorality and wrongdoing" (29:45) (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark evaluation
"Praying at the mosque is better than praying at home." Evaluate this statement. (12)

**For the statement**: praying in congregation at the mosque has great value — a hadith in Bukhari says it is worth twenty-seven times praying alone. Standing shoulder to shoulder in straight rows behind the imam shows that all Muslims are equal before God, whatever their wealth or race, and strengthens the ummah. The Qur'an commands Muslims to leave trade and go to the Friday prayer (62:9), and Jummah, with its khutbah, can only be prayed in congregation, so the mosque is where Muslims learn and are encouraged in their faith. For many Muslims the mosque is also a place where they can concentrate, away from the distractions of home.

**Against the statement**: Muslims believe God is everywhere and hears every prayer; a hadith teaches that the whole earth has been made a place of prayer, so salah at home is fully valid if it is performed correctly with wudu and facing the qiblah. What matters most is sincerity and concentration (khushu) — God judges the intention (niyyah), and a distracted prayer in the mosque is worth less than a heartfelt prayer at home. Many women pray at home — some hadith encourage this, although Muhammad also told men not to stop women going to the mosque — and for them home prayer is not second best. Praying together as a family at home can also teach children the faith and bring the family closer. For many Muslims in Britain, a mosque is not close enough to reach five times a day, so home, school or work prayer rooms are essential.

**Conclusion**: I think the statement is true for men and for Jummah, because Islamic teaching clearly gives extra reward to congregational prayer and the Qur'an commands attendance on Friday. But as a general statement it is too strong: salah is valid anywhere clean, and its value depends on the sincerity of the person praying. The best view is that the mosque adds community and reward, while home prayer is equally accepted by God.
:::
