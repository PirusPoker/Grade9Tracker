## What it is

Islam teaches that there is **one God**, called **Allah** (Arabic for "the God" — Arabic-speaking Christians use the same word). Muslims are those who **submit** to God's will. There are two main branches: **Sunni** Muslims (the large majority worldwide, around 85–90%) and **Shi'a** Muslims (a significant minority, the majority in Iran and Iraq). Both share the core beliefs, but they summarise them differently.

### The six articles of faith (Sunni Islam)

Sunni Muslims summarise their beliefs (**iman**, faith) as **six articles of faith**. They come from the **Hadith of Jibril** (recorded in Sahih Muslim), in which the angel Jibril, appearing as a man in white, asked the Prophet Muhammad what faith is. He answered: to believe in Allah, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and in divine decree, both its good and its evil. The Qur'an lists most of them together: whoever disbelieves in Allah, His angels, His books, His messengers and the Last Day "has certainly gone far astray" (Qur'an 4:136; see also 2:177).

| Article | Arabic | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Belief in Allah** | Tawhid | God is one, unique and without partners |
| 2. **Belief in angels** | Malaikah | God's messengers, made of light, who obey him perfectly (e.g. Jibril, Mika'il) |
| 3. **Belief in the holy books** | Kutub | God revealed scriptures: the Qur'an (final and complete), the Torah (Tawrat), Psalms (Zabur), Gospel (Injil) and the Scrolls of Abraham |
| 4. **Belief in the prophets** | Risalah (Rusul) | God sent prophets to guide humanity, from Adam to Muhammad, the "Seal of the Prophets" (Qur'an 33:40) |
| 5. **Belief in the Day of Judgement** | Akhirah / Yawm ad-Din | Everyone will be raised, judged and sent to paradise or hell |
| 6. **Belief in predestination** | al-Qadr | God knows and has decreed everything that happens, yet humans are responsible for their choices |

### The five roots of Usul ad-Din (Shi'a Islam)

**Usul ad-Din** means "the roots (or foundations) of religion". Twelver Shi'a Muslims teach **five roots**. Just as a tree cannot stand without roots, faith cannot stand without these; the practices (the **Furu ad-Din**, "branches of religion") grow from them. Shi'a scholars teach that each Muslim should accept the roots through **personal understanding and conviction**, not simply by copying others.

| Root | Arabic | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **The Oneness of God** | Tawhid | God is one and unique — the same as the Sunni first article |
| 2. **Divine justice** | Adalat (Adl) | God is perfectly just and never acts wrongly or unfairly |
| 3. **Prophethood** | Nubuwwah | God sent prophets, ending with Muhammad, to guide humanity |
| 4. **Leadership (the Imamate)** | Imamah | After Muhammad, God appointed **twelve Imams**, beginning with his cousin and son-in-law **Ali**, as sinless, divinely guided leaders of the community |
| 5. **The Day of Judgement and resurrection** | Mi'ad (Qiyamah) | All will be raised and judged |

### Similarities and differences

**Key similarities**
- Both put **Tawhid first** — everything else depends on the oneness of God.
- Both believe in **prophethood**, with Muhammad as the final prophet and the **Qur'an** as God's final revelation.
- Both believe in the **Day of Judgement**, resurrection, paradise and hell.
- **Shi'a Muslims also believe in angels, the holy books and God's decree** — these are simply not listed as separate "roots"; angels and books are understood as part of prophethood, because prophets receive revelation through angels and bring the books.

**Key differences**
- **Adalat** is a separate root for Shi'a, but not a separate article for Sunnis (who still believe God is just — al-Adl is one of the 99 names of God).
- **Imamah** is a root for Shi'a — the belief that God appointed Ali and the Imams after him. Sunnis believe the community chose its leaders (the caliphs, beginning with Abu Bakr), and do not regard the Imams as infallible or divinely appointed.
- **Predestination (al-Qadr)** is a separate article for Sunnis. Shi'a thought, because of its stress on God's justice, gives **greater emphasis to human free will**: a just God would not punish people for actions he had forced them to do.
- The two lists reflect different emphases: the Sunni list stresses **God's power and decree**; the Shi'a list stresses **God's justice** and the need for **rightly guided leadership**.

### Tawhid — the oneness of God

**Tawhid** is the belief that God is **one** and **unique**. It is the most important belief in Islam — the first article and the first root — and the first half of the **Shahadah**: "There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the messenger of Allah."

**Surah 112 (al-Ikhlas, "Sincerity" or "Purity of Faith")** is the prescribed text:

> Say: He is Allah, the One and Only;
> Allah, the Eternal, Absolute;
> He begetteth not, nor is He begotten;
> And there is none like unto Him.
> (Qur'an 112:1–4, translated by Yusuf Ali)

What each line teaches:
- **"The One and Only"** — there is only one God; he cannot be divided into parts or persons.
- **"The Eternal, Absolute"** (Arabic *as-Samad*) — God has no beginning or end; everything depends on him, but he depends on nothing.
- **"He begetteth not, nor is He begotten"** — God has no parents and no children. Muslims therefore reject the idea that God has a son, which is why they do not accept the Christian doctrine of the Trinity, though they honour Jesus (Isa) as a great prophet.
- **"There is none like unto Him"** — nothing in creation can be compared with God (compare 42:11: "There is nothing like unto Him").

The Prophet taught that this short surah is **equal to a third of the Qur'an** (hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari), and Muslims recite it often, including in their daily prayers.

**Shirk** — associating partners with God — is the opposite of Tawhid and the **greatest sin** in Islam: "Indeed, Allah does not forgive association with Him, but He forgives what is less than that for whom He wills" (Qur'an 4:48) — though a person who repents of shirk before death can be forgiven. Shirk includes worshipping idols, believing any other being shares God's power, and, many scholars say, putting money, status or anything else in God's place, or worshipping to show off.

**How Tawhid influences Muslims today:**
- **Worship only God** — prayer and devotion are directed to Allah alone; Muslims do not pray to saints or statues.
- **No images of God** (or of the Prophet) — God cannot be pictured, so mosques are decorated with **Arabic calligraphy** (verses of the Qur'an, the names of God) and **geometric patterns**, which suggest God's infinite, perfect order.
- **One God, one creation, one humanity** — all people are created by the same God, which supports the unity of the **ummah** (worldwide Muslim community) and equality of all people.
- **The whole of life is under God** — no area of life is separate from religion: work, family, money and politics should all follow God's guidance.
- **Avoiding shirk** in daily life — not letting wealth, fame or desires become "gods".

### The nature of God

The Qur'an gives God many names — traditionally **the 99 Beautiful Names** (al-Asma al-Husna) — which describe his nature. Muslims believe God is beyond full human understanding, but these names reveal what he is like. Every surah of the Qur'an except one (Surah 9) begins with the **Bismillah**: "In the name of Allah, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful".

| Quality | Meaning | Names and sources | Influence on Muslims |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Omnipotence** | God is **all-powerful**; nothing is beyond him. He created everything and controls everything | **al-Qadir** (the All-Powerful). "When He intends a thing, He only says to it, 'Be,' and it is" (paraphrase of 36:82); God has power over all things (2:20). Ayat al-Kursi: neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes him (2:255) | Trust in God in hard times; humility — say "Allahu Akbar" (God is greatest) and "insha'Allah" (if God wills) |
| **Beneficence** | God is **all-good and generous**: he created the world for humans' benefit and provides everything they need | **ar-Rahman** (the Most Gracious / Beneficent). Surah 55 (ar-Rahman) lists God's gifts, asking again and again "Which of the favours of your Lord would you deny?" | Gratitude — thanking God in prayer and before meals; sharing wealth through zakah |
| **Mercy** | God is **compassionate and forgiving** to those who repent | **ar-Rahim** (the Most Merciful). "Do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins" (39:53). Hadith qudsi: "My mercy prevails over My wrath" (Bukhari and Muslim) | Repent and ask forgiveness; show mercy and forgiveness to others |
| **Fairness and justice** | God is **perfectly fair**; he judges everyone rightly, rewarding good and punishing evil | **al-Adl** (the Just), **al-Hakam** (the Judge). God does not wrong anyone even "as much as an atom's weight" (4:40); "Allah orders justice and good conduct" (16:90) | Act justly, treat others fairly; confidence that wrongs will be put right on the Day of Judgement |

### Adalat — God's justice in Shi'a Islam

For Shi'a Muslims, **Adalat** (divine justice) is one of the five roots, so it is given special emphasis:
- God is **just by his very nature**: he never does anything wrong, unfair or pointless, and never breaks his promises.
- Good and evil are real, and human **reason** can recognise justice — God commands what is good *because* it is good.
- Because God is just, humans must have **genuine free will**: God would not punish people for sins he forced them to commit. So people are fully **responsible** and **accountable** for their actions, and will be judged fairly.
- A just and caring God would not leave humanity without guidance — one reason Shi'a give for God sending prophets and appointing the **Imams**.
- Suffering in this life will be **balanced** on the Day of Judgement: those who suffered unjustly will be compensated.

Sunnis also believe God is just (al-Adl), but many Sunni theologians (following the **Ash'ari** school) emphasise that God's will is the **standard** of justice — something is good because God commands it — and place more weight on God's all-powerful decree (**al-Qadr**).

### Immanence and transcendence

Muslims hold two ideas together about God's relationship with the world:

| | **Transcendence** | **Immanence** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | God is **beyond** and **above** the universe — outside time and space, not limited by it, and beyond human understanding | God is **close** to and **involved** in the world and in each person's life |
| Qur'an | "Vision perceives Him not, but He perceives all vision" (6:103); "There is nothing like unto Him" (42:11); Surah 112:4 | "We are closer to him than [his] jugular vein" (50:16); "indeed I am near. I respond to the invocation of the supplicant when he calls upon Me" (2:186) |
| Effect on Muslims | Awe and humility; no images of God; God cannot be fully described | Personal prayer (du'a) in any language at any time; God knows every thought, so Muslims try to act rightly in private too |

- God is described in one verse as both "the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward" (Qur'an 57:3) — beyond all things, yet present within them.
- **Different emphases**: mainstream theologians of both Sunni and Shi'a traditions stress that God is **transcendent** — utterly distinct from creation — while **Sufi** Muslims (mystics within Islam) especially emphasise **immanence**, seeking a close, personal experience of God through dhikr (remembrance) and prayer. All agree that God is **both**: if God were only transcendent, he could not guide or answer prayer; if only immanent, he might be confused with creation, which would be shirk.

::: terms
- **Tawhid**: the oneness and uniqueness of God — the most important belief in Islam and the first part of the Shahadah.
- **Six articles of faith**: the Sunni summary of belief — Allah, angels, holy books, prophets, the Day of Judgement and predestination.
- **Usul ad-Din**: "roots of religion" — the five Shi'a foundations of faith: Tawhid, Adalat, Nubuwwah, Imamah and Mi'ad.
- **Adalat**: divine justice — the Shi'a root teaching that God is perfectly just and never acts unfairly.
- **Imamah**: the Shi'a belief that God appointed twelve sinless Imams, beginning with Ali, to lead the Muslim community after Muhammad.
- **Shirk**: associating partners with God, or putting anything in God's place — the greatest sin in Islam.
- **Omnipotence**: being all-powerful; God can do anything and controls everything.
- **Beneficence**: God's goodness and generosity in creating and providing for everything (ar-Rahman).
- **Mercy**: God's compassion and willingness to forgive those who repent (ar-Rahim).
- **Immanence**: God being close to and involved in the world and in people's lives (Qur'an 50:16).
- **Transcendence**: God being beyond and above creation, time and space, and beyond human understanding (Qur'an 6:103).
:::

::: method
Paper 1 (Islam) has two five-part questions: **Q1 on beliefs** (this topic) and **Q2 on practices**. Each is 1 + 2 + 4 + 5 + 12 = 24 marks. **SPaG (3 marks) is assessed on the 12-mark question in the beliefs section** — so this is the 12-mark where accurate spelling of Tawhid, Qur'an, omnipotence, transcendence and Shi'a counts.

1. **1 mark — multiple choice.** Four options A–D; choose one. Distractors are often other Arabic terms: Adalat vs Akhirah; Imamah vs Risalah; Tawhid vs Shirk.
2. **2 marks — "Give two…"** Two short, separate, accurate points ("belief in angels"; "belief in the holy books"). Only your **first two** are marked.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two ways in which belief in … influences Muslims today"** or **"Explain two contrasting beliefs…"**. Two points, each **developed**: 1 mark simple + 1 mark detail. For **influence** questions the development must show what a Muslim **does** because of the belief (e.g. Tawhid → no images of God, so mosques use calligraphy). For **contrasting** questions the points must genuinely differ — the classic here is **Sunni six articles vs Shi'a five roots** (e.g. Sunnis list al-Qadr, Shi'a list Adalat and Imamah), or **immanence vs transcendence**.
4. **5 marks — "Explain two Muslim beliefs about … Refer to sacred writings or another source of Muslim belief and teaching in your answer."** Two developed beliefs (2 + 2) **plus 1 mark** for a relevant source. The prescribed text is **Surah 112** — know it. Others: 50:16, 6:103, 42:11, 39:53, 4:48, 4:40, 4:136, the Hadith of Jibril. Link the source to the belief.
5. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement."** Bullets: *refer to Muslim teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion.* (+3 SPaG)

**How the 12-mark levels are reached** (the whole answer is placed in one level):
- **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with a reason stated.
- **Level 2 (4–6)**: one view argued with a logical chain of reasoning, **or** different views recognised, each with reasons. **Maximum Level 2 with no reference to religion.**
- **Level 3 (7–9)**: reasoned consideration of **different** points of view, with logical chains that draw on knowledge, and **clear reference to religion**.
- **Level 4 (10–12)**: a **well-argued** response — different views weighed with logical chains **leading to a justified judgement**, with Muslim teaching *applied* to the issue.

Typical statements: "Tawhid is the most important belief in Islam", "Muslims should think of God as close to them rather than beyond them", "Sunni and Shi'a Muslims believe the same things", "God's mercy is more important than his justice". The "different point of view" can come from **within Islam** — Sunni vs Shi'a emphasis, Sufi vs other theologians — which shows diversity and helps reach Level 3 and 4.
:::

::: example 4-mark — contrasting Sunni and Shi'a
Explain two contrasting beliefs about the foundations of faith in Sunni and Shi'a Islam. (4)

Sunni Muslims summarise their faith in six articles of faith, which include belief in al-Qadr, predestination (1). This comes from the Hadith of Jibril and emphasises God's power: God knows and has decreed everything that happens, though humans are still responsible for their choices (1). Shi'a Muslims instead follow the five roots of Usul ad-Din, which include Adalat (divine justice) and Imamah (1). Shi'a teach that because God is perfectly just he gives humans genuine free will, and that he appointed twelve Imams, starting with Ali, to guide the community after Muhammad — which Sunnis do not accept (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark — Tawhid
Explain two Muslim beliefs about Tawhid. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Muslim belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

First, Tawhid means that God is one and unique, with no partners or equals (1). Surah 112 says "Say: He is Allah, the One and Only... And there is none like unto Him" (source, 1), so Muslims believe nothing can be compared with God and he cannot be pictured, which is why mosques contain no images, only calligraphy and geometric patterns (1). Second, Muslims believe God has no parents or children (1). Surah 112 says "He begetteth not, nor is He begotten", so Muslims reject the idea that God could have a son, and they regard associating anything with God — shirk — as the greatest sin, which Qur'an 4:48 says God does not forgive if a person dies unrepentant (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark — immanence or transcendence
"God is too far beyond humans to be close to them." Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should: refer to Muslim teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion. (12 + 3 SPaG)

Some Muslims would see truth in this statement because Islam strongly emphasises God's transcendence. Surah 112 says "there is none like unto Him", and the Qur'an teaches that "Vision perceives Him not, but He perceives all vision" (6:103). God is eternal and all-powerful, outside time and space, and completely different from his creation. That is why Muslims never make pictures of God and why confusing God with any created thing counts as shirk, the greatest sin. If God is so far above human understanding, it could be argued that people cannot really know him or have a close relationship with him.

However, most Muslims would disagree with the statement, because the Qur'an also teaches that God is immanent. God says "We are closer to him than [his] jugular vein" (50:16), meaning God knows every thought and is present in every moment of a person's life. He also says "indeed I am near. I respond to the invocation of the supplicant when he calls upon Me" (2:186), which is why Muslims make personal du'a prayers in their own words at any time. God's names ar-Rahman and ar-Rahim show that he is merciful and caring, not distant. Sufi Muslims especially seek a close experience of God through dhikr, the constant remembrance of his names.

In conclusion, I disagree with the statement. It is true that God is beyond full human understanding, and Islam protects this through Tawhid. But the statement treats transcendence and immanence as opposites, whereas the Qur'an teaches both: God is "the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward" (57:3). Because he is all-powerful and not limited by space, he can be present everywhere at once, including close to every believer. So God's greatness is the very reason he can be close to humans, and the argument against the statement is stronger because it is based on the Qur'an's own balance.
:::
