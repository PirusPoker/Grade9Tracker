## What it is

Christianity is the main religious tradition in Great Britain, but it is diverse: **Catholic**, **Orthodox** and **Protestant** Christians (Anglicans, Methodists, Baptists, Pentecostals, Quakers and many more) share core beliefs about God but understand and express them in different ways. This topic covers what Christians believe **God is like**, the **Trinity**, the **problem of evil and suffering**, and **creation** — including the two prescribed texts, **Genesis 1:1-3** and **John 1:1-3**.

### The nature of God: omnipotent, loving and just

Christians are **monotheists**: they believe in one God. Three qualities of God are named in the specification.

| Quality | What it means | Where it comes from | How it affects Christians |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Omnipotent** | all-powerful; God can do anything that is logically possible | God creates the universe from nothing (Genesis 1); the angel tells Mary nothing is impossible with God (Luke 1:37); Jesus says "with God all things are possible" (Matthew 19:26) | trust that God is in control; prayer is worthwhile because God can act; awe and worship |
| **Loving** (omnibenevolent) | God is all-good and cares for every person | "God is love" (1 John 4:8); God gave his only Son (John 3:16) | Christians should love God and their neighbour; God's love is shown most fully in Jesus |
| **Just** | God is fair; he judges rightly, rewards good and punishes wrong | "I, the Lord, love justice" (Isaiah 61:8); the Parable of the Sheep and the Goats (Matthew 25:31-46) | Christians should work for justice and treat people fairly; everyone will be held accountable |

Other qualities Christians also name: **omniscient** (all-knowing), **eternal**, **creator**, **transcendent** (beyond the universe) and **immanent** (involved in it).

The qualities fit together: because God is **just** he will not ignore wrongdoing, but because he is **loving** he offers forgiveness. A loving God who was not just could not be trusted to put wrongs right; a just God who was not loving would leave humans with no hope. Christians see both brought together in Jesus' death on the cross.

### The problem of evil and suffering

**Evil** is anything morally wrong or harmful; **suffering** is the pain that results. Christians distinguish:

- **Moral evil** — caused by humans choosing to do wrong (murder, war, theft, abuse).
- **Natural evil** — caused by nature, not by human choice (earthquakes, floods, disease, famine).

**The problem**: if God is **omnipotent** he *could* stop suffering; if he is **loving** he would *want* to stop it; yet suffering exists. So either God is not all-powerful, or not all-loving, or does not exist. This "inconsistent triad" is one of the strongest arguments used by atheists, and it can challenge the faith of believers who experience suffering themselves.

**Christian responses** (Christians give different answers; many combine them):

| Response | The argument | Support |
|---|---|---|
| **Free will** | God gave humans free will so that they can freely love him; robots could not love. Free will means people can choose evil, so moral evil is the price of genuine freedom. | Genesis 3 — Adam and Eve freely disobey God |
| **The Fall** (Augustine) | Creation was made good ("God saw all that he had made, and it was very good", Genesis 1:31); evil entered through human sin, and suffering and death followed. | Genesis 3; Romans 5:12 |
| **Soul-making** (Irenaeus; developed by John Hick) | Suffering gives humans the chance to develop qualities such as courage, compassion and patience. A world with no pain would give no chance to grow. | Romans 5:3-4 — suffering produces perseverance, character and hope |
| **Test of faith / mystery** | Humans cannot fully understand God's plan. Job suffers terribly, questions God and is answered with God's greatness rather than a neat explanation (Job 38-42). | the Book of Job; Isaiah 55:8-9 (God's ways are higher than ours) |
| **God shares in suffering** | God does not stand at a distance: in Jesus he suffered and died. Many Christians find this more helpful than any theory. | the crucifixion; Jesus cries out "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34) |
| **Practical response** | The most important answer is to *act*: pray for those who suffer and help them. | Parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37); Matthew 25:31-46 |

Many Christians also point to **hope**: God will one day end suffering. Revelation 21:4 promises that God will wipe away every tear and there will be no more death or pain. Some also believe that "in all things God works for the good of those who love him" (Romans 8:28).

**Influence today**: suffering leads Christians to pray, to work as doctors, nurses and carers, to volunteer with charities such as **Christian Aid**, and to campaign against injustice that causes moral evil. It can also lead some Christians to doubt, or to lose faith.

### The oneness of God and the Trinity

Christians believe God is **one** — "Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4) — but that the one God exists as **three Persons**: **Father, Son and Holy Spirit**. This is the **Trinity**. It is *not* three gods, and it is *not* one God wearing three masks. Each Person is fully God; the Persons are distinct from each other; there is one God.

| Person | Role | Key reference |
|---|---|---|
| **God the Father** | creator and sustainer of all; a loving parent to his children | Jesus teaches his disciples to pray "Our Father" (Matthew 6:9) |
| **God the Son** | Jesus Christ, God incarnate (in human form); saviour | "The Word became flesh" (John 1:14) |
| **God the Holy Spirit** | God's presence and power in the world today; guides, strengthens and gives gifts to believers | the Spirit comes on the disciples at Pentecost (Acts 2) |

**Biblical basis**: the word "Trinity" is not in the Bible, but the idea is drawn from it. At **Jesus' baptism** all three Persons appear: the Son is baptised, the Spirit descends like a dove, and the Father's voice says "You are my Son, whom I love; with you I am well pleased" (Mark 1:10-11). Jesus tells his followers to baptise "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19).

**The Nicene Creed** (agreed in 325 and expanded in 381) states the belief: "We believe in one God, the Father, the Almighty, maker of heaven and earth", in "one Lord, Jesus Christ", "true God from true God", and in "the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the giver of life".

**Divergent view — the filioque**: Western churches (Catholic, Anglican and most Protestant) say the Holy Spirit proceeds "from the Father **and the Son**" (Latin *filioque*). The **Orthodox** Church rejects this addition, teaching that the Spirit proceeds from the Father. This was one of the causes of the split between East and West in 1054.

**Why it matters**: the Trinity shows that God is a **relationship of love** within himself, so love is at the heart of reality; it shapes worship (prayers are often made to the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit; services begin and baptisms are performed "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit"); and it helps Christians relate to God as creator, as saviour and as a presence within them.

### Creation: the role of Word and Spirit

**Genesis 1:1-3** (prescribed): "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth. Now the earth was formless and empty, darkness was over the surface of the deep, and the Spirit of God was hovering over the waters. And God said, 'Let there be light,' and there was light."

**John 1:1-3** (prescribed): "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was with God in the beginning. Through him all things were made; without him nothing was made that has been made."

What the two texts show:

- **God the Father** is the creator — God creates by his own will, out of nothing (*ex nihilo*).
- **The Spirit** is present at creation, "hovering over the waters", bringing order and life out of chaos.
- **The Word** creates: God *speaks* ("And God said...") and creation happens. John's Gospel identifies this Word (Greek *logos*) with **Jesus** — "The Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us" (John 1:14). So the Son was present and active at creation, not created later.
- So **all three Persons of the Trinity** were involved in creation. John 1:1 deliberately echoes Genesis 1:1 ("In the beginning...").

**Different Christian beliefs about creation**

| View | Belief | Who holds it |
|---|---|---|
| **Literal (creationist)** | Genesis is an accurate historical account: God made the world in six days, a few thousand years ago; the Big Bang and evolution are rejected where they contradict the Bible | some evangelical and fundamentalist Protestants |
| **Non-literal** | Genesis teaches religious truths — God is creator, creation is good, humans are made in God's image — not scientific facts; the "days" may be long ages or poetry. Science explains *how*, religion explains *why* | the Catholic Church, the Church of England and most mainstream Christians in Britain |
| **God behind the Big Bang** | the Big Bang (first proposed by Georges Lemaître, a Catholic priest and physicist) is the way God created; God started and sustains the process and guides evolution | many Christians who hold the non-literal view |

All these Christians agree on the key **religious** points: God created everything deliberately; creation was "very good" (Genesis 1:31); humans are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27) and have a duty of **stewardship** — caring for the world on God's behalf.

**Why creation matters**: it gives life purpose (the universe is intended, not an accident); it gives every human value; it calls for care of the environment; and it shows God's power and love.

::: terms
- **Omnipotent**: all-powerful — God is able to do anything that is logically possible.
- **Omnibenevolent**: all-loving and all-good — God cares for every person and wants what is best for them.
- **Just**: fair and right in judgement — God rewards good, punishes wrongdoing and treats everyone fairly.
- **Moral evil**: suffering caused by humans freely choosing to act wrongly, such as murder, war or theft.
- **Natural evil**: suffering caused by nature rather than human choice, such as earthquakes, floods and disease.
- **Free will**: the God-given ability of humans to make their own choices, including the choice to do wrong.
- **Trinity**: the belief that the one God exists as three Persons — Father, Son and Holy Spirit — each fully God.
- **Monotheism**: the belief that there is only one God.
- **The Word**: the Greek logos in John 1, God's creative word, identified by Christians with Jesus, the Son.
- **Creation ex nihilo**: the belief that God created the universe out of nothing, by his own will.
- **Filioque**: Latin for "and the Son" — the Western Creed's teaching that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son, rejected by the Orthodox.
- **Stewardship**: the duty given to humans by God to look after creation responsibly on his behalf.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 (Christianity) asks five parts per question: 1, 2, 4, 5 and 12 marks.** Spend about 25 minutes on each five-part question.

1. **1 mark — multiple choice**: four options, tick one. Read for "not" ("Which one of the following is **not** part of the Trinity?").
2. **2 marks — "Give two..." / "Name two..."**: two short, separate, correct points. Only your first two answers are marked, so do not list five and hope.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two ways..." / "Explain two Christian beliefs..." / "Explain two contrasting beliefs..."**: two points, each **developed** (1 mark simple + 1 mark detailed = 2 per point). Develop with an example, evidence, a different view or detailed information. For "contrasting" questions, the two points must genuinely differ — here, for example, a literal creationist view vs a non-literal view, or the Western vs Orthodox view of the Spirit. For "influence" questions, the second mark must show the *effect* on how Christians live.
4. **5 marks — "Explain two... Refer to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching in your answer"**: the same two developed points (4 marks) **plus 1 mark for a relevant, accurate source** — a Bible verse (a close paraphrase with the book is fine: "Genesis 1 says the Spirit hovered over the waters"), a creed, a Church teaching. Quote or paraphrase it *and* link it to the point.
5. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement."** On Paper 1 the bullets are: *refer to Christian teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion.* (On the themes paper the wording is "refer to religious arguments; may refer to non-religious arguments".) One 12-mark question in each religion carries **+3 SPaG** — spell key words (omnipotent, Trinity, omnibenevolent) correctly.

**How the 12-mark levels work** (the whole answer is placed in a level):
- **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with reasons stated.
- **Level 2 (4–6)**: one side argued with a logical chain of reasoning, **or** both sides recognised with reasons. **Capped at Level 2 if there is no reference to religion.**
- **Level 3 (7–9)**: reasoned consideration of **different** points of view, with logical chains drawing on knowledge, and **clear reference to religion**.
- **Level 4 (10–12)**: a **well-argued** response — different views weighed with logical chains **leading to a justified judgement**, with religion *applied* to the issue.

A strong structure: argument for (point, explanation, evidence, evaluation) ×2; argument against ×2; conclusion that says which side is stronger *and why*, rather than repeating what you said.
:::

::: example 4-mark contrasting beliefs
Explain two contrasting Christian beliefs about creation. (4)

Some Christians, such as some evangelical Protestants, take Genesis 1 literally: God made the world in six 24-hour days a few thousand years ago (1). They believe the Bible is the inspired word of God and cannot be wrong, so where science disagrees, for example over evolution, they believe Genesis rather than scientists (1).

Other Christians, including the Catholic Church and the Church of England, read Genesis as non-literal, teaching religious truths rather than science (1). They accept the Big Bang and evolution as the way God created, so science explains how the universe began, while Genesis explains why — God created deliberately and made it good (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark with a source
Explain two Christian teachings about the nature of God. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

Christians teach that God is omnipotent, meaning all-powerful (1). This means God could create the whole universe out of nothing simply by speaking — "And God said, 'Let there be light,' and there was light" (Genesis 1:3) — so Christians trust that God is in control of the world and pray to him for help (1) (source 1).

Christians also teach that God is loving (1). The First Letter of John says "God is love", and Christians believe God showed this by sending Jesus to die for humanity, so they try to show the same love to others, for example by helping those in need (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark evaluation
"The existence of evil and suffering proves that God is not loving." Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should: refer to Christian teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion. (12)

Some people agree. If God were loving he would want to stop suffering, and Christians teach that he is omnipotent, so he could stop it. Yet millions suffer from natural evils such as earthquakes and cancer, which no human caused. An atheist would argue that a loving parent with the power to stop their child's pain would do so, so the existence of such suffering suggests that God either does not exist or is not loving. Even some Christians, like Job in the Bible, cry out and question why God allows them to suffer.

However, most Christians disagree. They argue that God gave humans free will because love must be freely chosen; a world of robots could not love God. Free will means people can choose to hurt others, so moral evils such as war are caused by humans, not God. Irenaeus argued that suffering also allows humans to develop courage and compassion — "soul-making" — which would be impossible in a world without pain. Paul wrote that suffering produces perseverance, character and hope (Romans 5:3-4). Most importantly, Christians believe God does not stand at a distance: in Jesus he suffered and died on the cross, which shows that God is loving and shares human pain. Christians also believe God will one day "wipe every tear" from people's eyes (Revelation 21:4).

In conclusion, I disagree with the statement. The free will argument explains moral evil well, but natural evil is harder to explain, so suffering is a real challenge to belief in a loving God. However, "proves" is too strong: the crucifixion shows Christians a God who chooses to suffer alongside humanity, and suffering can lead to great acts of love, as when Christians respond by caring for the sick. The existence of suffering raises a serious question, but it does not prove that God is not loving.

(Level 4: both sides developed with logical chains, accurate Christian teaching applied, and a judgement that is justified rather than repeated.)
:::

::: q
"Christians should accept the Big Bang theory." Evaluate this statement. (12)
---
Levels as for all 12-mark questions (L4 10–12 well-argued, justified judgement; L3 7–9 different views with clear reference to religion; L2 4–6 one view reasoned or two views recognised; L1 1–3 a view with reasons; max L2 with no religion).
For: the evidence (expanding universe, background radiation) is strong; the theory was first proposed by Georges Lemaître, a Catholic priest; the Catholic Church and the Church of England accept it; Genesis can be read as non-literal religious truth — science says how, Genesis says why; God could have caused the Big Bang, so it does not remove the need for a creator.

::: watch
- **Mixing up the "omni" words**: omnipotent = all-powerful, omniscient = all-knowing, omnibenevolent = all-loving. One wrong word loses the mark and confuses an evaluation.
- **Explaining the Trinity as three gods**, or as one God simply playing three roles — both are rejected by Christians. Say "one God in three Persons".
- **Moral vs natural evil**: war and murder are moral; disease and earthquakes are natural. Candidates often muddle the examples.
- **Using the prescribed texts vaguely**: "the Bible says God created the world" rarely earns the source mark. Name Genesis 1:1-3 or John 1:1-3 and link it — Spirit hovering, Word through whom all things were made.
- **One creation view only**: "different Christian beliefs" needs literal *and* non-literal views, with who holds them.
- **12-mark answers with no Christian teaching** are capped at Level 2, however good the argument about suffering.
:::
