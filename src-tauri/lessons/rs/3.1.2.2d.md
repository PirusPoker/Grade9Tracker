## What it is

Christians believe that faith must be **shown in action**. The **Church** means both the building and, more importantly, the **community of believers** — the "body of Christ" (1 Corinthians 12:27). That body exists locally (the parish or congregation) and worldwide (over two billion Christians in almost every country). Three teachings drive everything in this topic:

- **Love your neighbour** — the second greatest commandment (Mark 12:31), illustrated by the **Parable of the Good Samaritan** (Luke 10:25–37): your neighbour is anyone in need.
- **The Sheep and the Goats** (Matthew 25:31–46): Jesus says "I was hungry and you gave me something to eat... I was a stranger and you invited me in", and whatever is done for "the least of these" is done for him.
- **Faith without deeds is dead**: "faith by itself, if it is not accompanied by action, is dead" (James 2:17); "let us not love with words or speech but with actions and in truth" (1 John 3:18).

### The Church in the local community

Churches are often at the centre of their neighbourhood, especially where other services have closed. Typical activities: parent and toddler groups, youth clubs, lunch clubs and visiting for older and lonely people, night shelters for homeless people, community cafés, "warm spaces" in winter, debt advice (for example through the Christian charity **Christians Against Poverty**), prison and hospital chaplaincy, and support for refugees.

**Food banks**
- A **food bank** gives emergency food parcels to people in crisis — for example after losing a job, a delay in benefit payments, illness or a large unexpected bill.
- The largest network in the UK is run by the **Trussell Trust** (now known simply as **Trussell**), a charity founded on Christian principles by **Paddy and Carol Henderson**; it opened its first UK food bank in **Salisbury in 2000**. Its network now operates from well over a thousand food bank centres, many based in church halls, and gave out around **2.9 million** emergency parcels in 2024–25.
- People are usually **referred** by professionals such as doctors, social workers or Citizens Advice, and receive about three days' nutritionally balanced food; volunteers also offer a hot drink, a listening ear and signposting to help with debt or benefits.
- Churches **collect donations** from congregations and supermarkets, **provide buildings** and **volunteers**. Many Christians also campaign for an end to the need for food banks — dealing with the causes of poverty, not just the symptoms.
- **Why**: Jesus fed the hungry (the feeding of the five thousand, Mark 6:30–44) and taught that feeding the hungry is serving him (Matthew 25:35).

**Street pastors**
- **Street Pastors** began in **Brixton, south London, in 2003**, founded by **Rev Les Isaac** through the Christian charity the **Ascension Trust**, as a church response to gun and gang crime and anti-social behaviour.
- Trained volunteers from local churches patrol town and city centres, usually on **Friday and Saturday nights**, working alongside the police, councils and door staff. They now operate in around 250 towns and cities in the UK.
- They **care, listen and help**: giving water to people who have drunk too much, flip-flops to people struggling in high heels, foil blankets, helping vulnerable people get home safely, sweeping up glass bottles that could be used as weapons, calming arguments. They do **not preach** on patrol unless asked — the aim is to show Christian love in action.
- Related schemes include School Pastors and Rail Pastors; some towns have similar "Street Angels" projects.
- **Why**: like the Good Samaritan, they help strangers in need; their presence has been linked by local police and councils to less crime and disorder in some areas.

### Mission, evangelism and Church growth

- **Mission** means being **sent out** by God to share the love of Christ in word and action — including serving the poor, working for justice and caring for creation, as well as preaching.
- **Evangelism** is the part of mission that is **spreading the Christian gospel** (the "good news" about Jesus) so that others may come to faith.
- **Church growth** is the increase in the number of believers and congregations — the result many Christians hope mission and evangelism will bring.

**Why Christians see mission as important:**
- The **Great Commission**: "go and make disciples of all nations, baptising them... and teaching them to obey everything I have commanded you" (Matthew 28:19–20).
- Jesus told the disciples "you will be my witnesses... to the ends of the earth" (Acts 1:8). After Pentecost "the Lord added to their number daily those who were being saved" (Acts 2:47).
- Christians believe the gospel brings salvation and eternal life, so sharing it is an act of love.
- They should be ready to explain their faith, but "with gentleness and respect" (1 Peter 3:15).

**How the Church does it today:**
- **Alpha courses** — an introduction to Christianity over a series of meals, talks and small-group discussion; developed at Holy Trinity Brompton, an Anglican church in London, and led for many years by Nicky Gumbel. It is now run by churches of many denominations around the world.
- **Fresh Expressions** — new forms of church for people who do not go to traditional services, e.g. café churches, **Messy Church** (creative, all-age church for families, begun in 2004), skate-park or online churches.
- **Missionaries** — Christians who go to other areas or countries to preach, teach, run schools and hospitals, or support local churches. Today many missionaries come **to** Britain from Africa, Asia and South America.
- Evangelists such as those of the **Church Army** (an Anglican mission society), street preaching, Christian radio, TV, websites and social media, Bible distribution, and simply **personal witness** — living in a way that shows the faith and talking about it with friends.
- Church planting — starting new congregations.

**Church growth in Britain and the world:**
- Across the world Christianity is **growing fast** in sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America and parts of Asia (for example through Pentecostal churches); most Christians now live in the Global South.
- In Britain overall church attendance has **declined**, and fewer than half of people in England and Wales described themselves as Christian in the 2021 census. But some churches are growing — especially **Pentecostal, Black-majority and migrant-led churches** (e.g. the Redeemed Christian Church of God, originally from Nigeria) and some evangelical churches.
- Different Christian views: evangelical Christians put strong emphasis on converting others; some liberal Christians focus more on social action and **interfaith dialogue**, respecting that people of other religions have their own paths to God; the Catholic Church speaks of the "**New Evangelisation**" — re-proposing the faith in countries where it has faded.

### The importance of the worldwide Church

Christians believe all believers form **one body** in Christ (1 Corinthians 12:12–27): "If one part suffers, every part suffers with it" (1 Corinthians 12:26), and in Christ there is neither Jew nor Gentile, slave nor free (Galatians 3:28). So the Church works across borders and denominations — for example through the **World Council of Churches** (founded 1948), which brings many Protestant, Anglican and Orthodox churches together, and through Catholic structures under the Pope.

### Working for reconciliation

**Reconciliation** means restoring friendship and harmony after conflict or wrongdoing. Christians believe Jesus's death **reconciled** humanity to God, and God has given Christians "the ministry of reconciliation" (2 Corinthians 5:18). Jesus said "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9) and taught that you should be reconciled with your brother or sister before offering your gift at the altar (Matthew 5:23–24).

Reconciliation happens at three levels:
1. **Between people and God** — through prayer, repentance and, for Catholics, the sacrament of reconciliation (confession).
2. **Between individuals** — forgiving others, as in the Lord's Prayer.
3. **Between groups and nations** — working for peace after conflict.

**Examples:**
- **Coventry Cathedral** was destroyed by German bombing on **14 November 1940**. Instead of calling for revenge, Provost Richard Howard had the words **"Father Forgive"** inscribed on the wall of the ruins. Crosses made from medieval nails found in the rubble became the **Cross of Nails**; the **Community of the Cross of Nails** now links churches and groups around the world working for reconciliation, including partners in Germany such as Dresden.
- **The Corrymeela Community** in Northern Ireland, founded in **1965** by **Ray Davey**, a Presbyterian chaplain, brings Catholics and Protestants together at its centre on the north coast to build trust and peace; church leaders also played a part in the Northern Ireland peace process.
- **Archbishop Desmond Tutu**, an Anglican, chaired South Africa's **Truth and Reconciliation Commission** after apartheid, based on truth-telling and forgiveness rather than revenge.
- Churches also work for reconciliation **between denominations** (ecumenism) and with **other faiths**, e.g. local interfaith forums.

### How Christian churches respond to persecution

**Persecution** is hostility and ill-treatment because of someone's beliefs — discrimination, imprisonment, violence or even death. The charity **Open Doors** estimates in its World Watch List that **more than 380 million Christians** face high levels of persecution and discrimination for their faith; countries such as **North Korea**, Somalia, Yemen, Libya, Sudan, Eritrea, Nigeria, Pakistan, Iran and Afghanistan have been near the top of its list.

**Christian teaching:**
- Jesus warned his followers they would be persecuted and said "Blessed are those who are persecuted because of righteousness, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 5:10).
- "Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44); "Bless those who persecute you" (Romans 12:14).
- "Remember those in prison as if you were together with them in prison" (Hebrews 13:3).
- The early Church was persecuted — Stephen was the first martyr (Acts 7) — yet it grew. The early Christian writer Tertullian said that the blood of the martyrs is the "seed" of the Church: persecution can strengthen faith.

**How churches respond:**
- **Prayer** — for persecuted Christians, and for the persecutors; many churches mark an annual day of prayer for the persecuted Church.
- **Practical support** — charities such as **Open Doors**, **Release International** and the Catholic **Aid to the Church in Need** supply Bibles, legal help, trauma care, rebuilding of homes and churches, and support for families of prisoners.
- **Campaigning** — raising awareness and lobbying governments and the United Nations to defend **freedom of religion** (a human right); in 2019 an independent review for the UK Foreign Office, led by the Bishop of Truro, examined how Britain supports persecuted Christians.
- **Forgiveness and non-violence** — following Jesus's example, persecuted Christians are encouraged not to take revenge.
- **Standing firm** — persecution is seen as sharing in Christ's sufferings; it can deepen faith and witness.

### The work of Christian Aid

The specification asks for **one** of three charities: **CAFOD** (the Catholic Agency for Overseas Development, founded 1962), **Christian Aid** or **Tearfund** (an evangelical charity founded 1968). This lesson studies **Christian Aid**.

| | Christian Aid |
|---|---|
| **Origins** | Founded in **1945** by British and Irish churches to help refugees in Europe after the Second World War; took the name **Christian Aid** in **1964** |
| **Who is behind it** | The official relief and development agency of **41 sponsoring churches** (Protestant and Orthodox) in Britain and Ireland |
| **Motto** | "**We believe in life before death**" — Christians should work for a full life for everyone now, not only hope for heaven |
| **Who it helps** | People in poverty in dozens of countries, **regardless of religion** — it helps people of all faiths and none |
| **How it works** | Through **local partner organisations** in the countries concerned, who know what communities need |

**What Christian Aid does:**
1. **Emergency relief** — responding to disasters such as earthquakes, floods, famine and war with food, clean water, shelter and medical help.
2. **Long-term development** — helping communities lift themselves out of poverty: improving farming and helping farmers adapt to **climate change**, clean water and sanitation, health care, education, loans for small businesses, and support for women's rights.
3. **Campaigning for justice** — tackling the causes of poverty: fair trade (Christian Aid was one of the founders of the **Fairtrade Foundation** in 1992), cancelling unjust debt, **tax justice** (making sure companies pay fair taxes in poor countries) and **climate justice**.
4. **Fundraising and education in Britain** — **Christian Aid Week** each May, held every year since **1957**, when volunteers deliver and collect envelopes house to house; church collections, sponsored events and resources for schools and churches.

**Why Christians support it:** the Parable of the Sheep and the Goats (Matthew 25:31–46); the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37); the prophets' demand for justice — "let justice roll on like a river" (Amos 5:24); the belief that every person is made in the image of God (Genesis 1:27) and deserves dignity; and the idea of **stewardship** — the world's resources belong to God and should be shared fairly.

::: terms
- **Food bank**: a place, often run from a church, that gives emergency food parcels to people in crisis who have been referred for help.
- **Street Pastors**: trained church volunteers who patrol town centres at night to care for, listen to and help vulnerable people.
- **Mission**: being sent by God to share the love of Christ with others through words and action.
- **Evangelism**: spreading the Christian gospel so that others may come to faith in Jesus.
- **Church growth**: an increase in the number of Christians and congregations, locally or worldwide.
- **Great Commission**: Jesus's command to "go and make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19–20).
- **Reconciliation**: restoring friendship and harmony between people, groups or nations, and between people and God, after conflict or sin.
- **Persecution**: hostility, discrimination or violence against people because of their beliefs.
- **Christian Aid**: a charity of British and Irish churches, founded in 1945, working for emergency relief, development and justice for people in poverty.
- **Ecumenism**: the movement to bring Christians of different denominations together in unity.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 (Christianity), Practices question: five parts worth 1, 2, 4, 5 and 12 marks.** Spend about 25 minutes on the whole question.

1. **1 mark — multiple choice**: four options, choose one. Know the definitions: mission vs evangelism; reconciliation vs persecution.
2. **2 marks — "Give two…"**: two brief, separate points ("run food banks"; "street pastors"). No explanation needed.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two ways…"**: e.g. "Explain two ways in which churches help the local community" or "Explain two ways Christians respond to persecution". Each point: **what** they do (1) + **detail or why** it helps / the teaching behind it (1). Use named examples — **Trussell Trust**, **Street Pastors**, **Coventry**, **Corrymeela**, **Open Doors**.
4. **5 marks — "Explain two… Refer to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching in your answer"**: two developed points (4 marks) **plus 1 mark for a relevant, accurate source**. The strongest sources here: Matthew 25:31–46 (Sheep and Goats), Luke 10:25–37 (Good Samaritan), James 2:17, Matthew 28:19–20 (Great Commission), Acts 1:8, 2 Corinthians 5:18, Matthew 5:9, 5:10, 5:44, Hebrews 13:3. Link the source to your point.
5. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement."** Paper 1 bullets: *refer to Christian teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion.* SPaG on Paper 1 goes on the Beliefs 12-marks, not this Practices one.

**How the 12-mark levels are reached** (the whole answer is placed in one level):
- **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with a reason stated.
- **Level 2 (4–6)**: one view argued with a logical chain of reasoning, **or** different views recognised, each with reasons. **Maximum Level 2 with no reference to religion.**
- **Level 3 (7–9)**: reasoned consideration of **different** points of view, with logical chains that draw on knowledge, and **clear reference to religion**.
- **Level 4 (10–12)**: a **well-argued** response — different views weighed with logical chains **leading to a justified judgement**, with religious teaching *applied* to the issue.

Typical statements: "Helping the local community is more important than evangelism", "The most important role of the Church is to help the poor", "Christians should do more to help persecuted Christians than anyone else", "Charity should begin at home". Weigh **words vs action**, **local vs worldwide**, and show that Christians **disagree** (evangelical emphasis on conversion vs emphasis on social justice). A conclusion that shows how the two sides connect ("action is itself a form of mission") often reaches Level 4.
:::

::: example 4-mark — the Church in the local community
Explain two ways in which the Church helps the local community. (4)

Many churches run or host food banks (1). They collect donated food and give emergency parcels to people referred by doctors or social workers, often in partnership with the Trussell Trust, so families in crisis do not go hungry and volunteers can direct them to help with debt or benefits (1). Churches also support Street Pastors (1): trained volunteers from local churches patrol the town centre on Friday and Saturday nights, giving water, flip-flops and a listening ear to vulnerable people and helping them get home safely, which has helped reduce disorder in some towns (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark — why mission and evangelism matter
Explain two reasons why evangelism is important to Christians. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

First, Jesus commanded it (1). In the Great Commission he told his disciples to "go and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19) (source, 1), so Christians see sharing the gospel as obedience to Jesus, and churches run Alpha courses to introduce people to the faith (1). Second, Christians believe the gospel brings salvation and eternal life (1). If they truly believe this, keeping it to themselves would be unloving, so they share their faith with friends and neighbours — though, as 1 Peter 3:15 teaches, with gentleness and respect for other people's beliefs (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark — helping the poor
"The most important thing the Church can do is to help the poor." Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should: refer to Christian teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion. (12)

Many Christians would agree. In the Parable of the Sheep and the Goats (Matthew 25:31–46) Jesus says that those who fed the hungry and welcomed strangers served him, and those who did not are sent away — so helping the poor seems to decide a person's eternal destiny. James teaches that faith without action is dead (James 2:17). Christian Aid's motto, "We believe in life before death", sums this up: the Church should make life better now through emergency relief, long-term development and campaigning for justice, as well as through local food banks. When the Church helps people regardless of their religion, it also shows what Christian love looks like, and this can attract people to the faith.

However, other Christians, especially evangelicals, would argue that the most important thing is to share the gospel. Jesus's final command was the Great Commission — "go and make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19). Christians believe salvation is eternal while poverty, however terrible, is temporary, so helping someone come to faith in Christ matters even more than feeding them. Others would say worship is the Church's first task, because the greatest commandment is to love God (Mark 12:30) and the second is to love your neighbour. The Church also has other vital work, such as reconciliation between enemies and supporting persecuted Christians.

In conclusion, I think the statement is partly right. Helping the poor cannot be separated from the Church's mission, because Jesus made care for the poor a sign of true faith. But it is not the *only* most important thing: Jesus gave both commands, to love God and neighbour, and to make disciples. The strongest view is that evangelism and service belong together — a food bank that offers a hot drink and a listening ear is itself a way of sharing the love of Christ — so helping the poor is one of the most important things, alongside worship and mission.
:::
