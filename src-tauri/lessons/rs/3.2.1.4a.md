## What it is

Theme D asks when, if ever, violence and war can be right. This first half covers four key ideas — **peace, justice, forgiveness and reconciliation** — then **violence**, **terrorism**, the **reasons for war**, the **just war theory**, **holy war** and **pacifism**. You need **Christian** and **Muslim** teaching (with differences inside each) and **non-religious** views. **Violence** and **pacifism** are two of the three Theme D issues on which you must explain **contrasting beliefs**: the specification requires **Christianity and at least one other religious tradition** (here, Islam). Differences within Christianity and a non-religious view can be added, but a non-religious view can never be the only contrast. (The third, weapons of mass destruction, is in the next lesson.)

### Peace, justice, forgiveness and reconciliation

| Idea | Meaning | Christian teaching | Muslim teaching |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Peace** | the absence of conflict; also harmony and well-being (Hebrew *shalom*, Arabic *salam*) | "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called children of God" (Matthew 5:9); Jesus is called the Prince of Peace (Isaiah 9:6) | the word *Islam* comes from the same root as *salam*; one of Allah's names is As-Salam, "the Source of Peace" (Qur'an 59:23); Muslims greet each other with "as-salamu alaykum" — peace be upon you |
| **Justice** | fairness; bringing about what is right, including a fair share of resources and fair treatment by the law | "let justice roll on like a river" (Amos 5:24); God requires people "to act justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly" (Micah 6:8) | "stand firm for justice, as witnesses to Allah, even if it be against yourselves" (Qur'an 4:135); Allah is Al-Adl, the Just |
| **Forgiveness** | pardoning someone for what they have done wrong and moving on without seeking revenge | forgive "seventy-seven times" (Matthew 18:21-22) — without limit; "forgive us our sins, as we forgive..." (Lord's Prayer, Matthew 6:12); on the cross Jesus prayed, "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34) | the reward for evil is an equal evil, "but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation, his reward is with Allah" (Qur'an 42:40); "let them pardon and overlook — would you not like Allah to forgive you?" (Qur'an 24:22); Allah is Al-Ghafur, the Forgiving |
| **Reconciliation** | restoring friendly relations after conflict — former enemies making up | be reconciled with your brother before offering your gift at the altar (Matthew 5:23-24); Christians are given "the ministry of reconciliation" (2 Corinthians 5:18) | "the believers are brothers, so make peace between your brothers" (Qur'an 49:10); if two groups of believers fight, make peace between them (Qur'an 49:9) |

**Why they matter**: religious believers see them as linked — lasting **peace** needs **justice** (injustice causes conflict), and after conflict **forgiveness** makes **reconciliation** possible. Forgiveness does not mean there should be no justice: a victim can forgive and still want a criminal punished.

**Examples**: **Gordon Wilson**, a Methodist whose daughter Marie was killed by the IRA bomb at Enniskillen in 1987, publicly forgave the bombers and later worked for peace in Northern Ireland. **Coventry Cathedral**, destroyed by bombing in 1940, has the words "Father forgive" in its ruins; its **Community of the Cross of Nails** works for reconciliation around the world, including with Dresden in Germany.

**Non-religious views**: humanists value peace, justice and reconciliation for the sake of human well-being; many see forgiveness as healthy for victims and society, but not something that can be demanded of them.

### Violence, including violent protest

**Violence** is the use of force to harm someone or damage property. **Protest** is expressing disagreement publicly; in the UK peaceful protest is a legal right, but violent protest — rioting, attacking people or property — is a crime.

**Contrasting beliefs about violence**

| View | Belief |
|---|---|
| **Most Christians** | violence is wrong: Jesus taught "turn the other cheek" (Matthew 5:39) and "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44); when Peter used a sword at Jesus' arrest, Jesus told him to put it away, "for all who draw the sword will die by the sword" (Matthew 26:52). **Martin Luther King Jr**, a Baptist minister, led the US civil rights movement by non-violent protest |
| **Some Christians** | violence may occasionally be justified as a last resort against great evil — Jesus overturned the tables of the money-changers in the Temple (Mark 11:15-17); the Lutheran pastor **Dietrich Bonhoeffer** became involved in the plot to assassinate Hitler |
| **Islam** | violence is wrong except in self-defence or to defend the oppressed, and then only within limits: "Fight in the way of Allah those who fight you, but do not transgress limits" (Qur'an 2:190). In Mecca the Prophet Muhammad and early Muslims endured persecution without fighting. Most Muslims support peaceful protest and condemn violent protest |
| **Non-religious** | most humanists oppose violence because it causes suffering; some accept it as a last resort, e.g. in self-defence |

### Terrorism

**Terrorism** is the unlawful use of violence, usually against civilians, to create fear and so achieve political or religious aims. Examples in Britain include the **7 July 2005** London bombings and the 2017 **Manchester Arena** bombing. Terrorists sometimes claim to act for a religion, but religious leaders overwhelmingly condemn terrorism.

- **Christians** condemn it as murder of the innocent — "You shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13) — and as the opposite of Jesus' teaching of love.
- **Muslims** condemn it: "whoever kills a soul... it is as if he had slain mankind entirely" (Qur'an 5:32); civilians, women and children must never be targeted; suicide is forbidden (Qur'an 4:29). In 2010 the scholar **Muhammad Tahir-ul-Qadri** issued a long fatwa declaring terrorism and suicide bombing un-Islamic, and the **Muslim Council of Britain** condemns terrorist attacks.
- **Non-religious** people condemn it as it kills innocent people; some point out that terrorism has also had purely political causes.

### Reasons for war

| Reason | Meaning | Religious response |
|---|---|---|
| **Greed** | fighting to gain land, wealth or resources such as oil (e.g. Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in 1990) | condemned — "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil" (1 Timothy 6:10); Islam forbids fighting for gain and teaches that wealth belongs to Allah |
| **Self-defence** | fighting to defend yourself, your country or allies from attack | accepted by most Christians and Muslims — "Permission to fight has been given to those who are being fought, because they were wronged" (Qur'an 22:39); for Christians it is a key part of the just war theory |
| **Retaliation** | fighting back in revenge for an attack (e.g. the US-led invasion of Afghanistan after the 11 September 2001 attacks) | "eye for eye" (Exodus 21:24) was meant to limit revenge; Jesus replaced it with "turn the other cheek" (Matthew 5:38-39). The Qur'an allows responding equally but says patience is better (Qur'an 16:126) |

Other reasons include defending **religion** or the freedom to practise it, helping an oppressed people, and removing a dictator.

### The just war theory

The **just war theory** was developed by **St Augustine** and **St Thomas Aquinas** to set out when Christians may fight. Most Christian denominations — Catholic, Anglican, Methodist and many others — accept it. The Catholic Church states the conditions in the Catechism (2309).

| Criterion | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Just cause** | fought in self-defence or to right a serious wrong (e.g. stopping genocide) |
| **Legitimate authority** | declared by a lawful government or ruler, not individuals or groups |
| **Right intention** | aimed at promoting good and restoring peace, not revenge or gain |
| **Last resort** | all peaceful ways — negotiation, sanctions, the United Nations — have been tried and failed |
| **Reasonable chance of success** | there must be a real chance of winning; a hopeless war wastes lives |
| **Proportionality** | the force used must be in proportion to the aim; the good achieved must outweigh the harm |
| **Discrimination** | civilians (non-combatants) must not be targeted |

**Islam** has similar conditions for the **lesser jihad** (military struggle): it must be declared by a legitimate leader; fought in self-defence, to defend the oppressed or to protect the freedom to worship; a last resort; with minimum force; and it must not harm civilians — women, children, the elderly — nor destroy crops, trees or animals (instructions of the first caliph, **Abu Bakr**). It must stop when the enemy seeks peace: "if they incline to peace, then incline to it also" (Qur'an 8:61). There must be "no compulsion in religion" (Qur'an 2:256), so it cannot be fought to force conversion.

**Non-religious views**: many atheists and humanists accept that some wars can be justified by similar conditions (the theory is also used in international law); others are pacifists.

### Holy war

A **holy war** is a war fought for a religious cause, believed to be approved by God and often declared by a religious leader, with spiritual rewards promised to those who fight.

- **Christianity**: the Old Testament describes wars fought at God's command, such as the conquest of Jericho (Joshua 6). The **Crusades**, called by **Pope Urban II** in 1095 to capture Jerusalem, were seen as holy wars. Today almost all Christians reject the idea: they believe Jesus' teaching of love rules out killing for religion, and the Catholic Church has apologised for violence committed in the name of faith.
- **Islam**: many Muslims reject the phrase "holy war", because **jihad** means "striving" in Allah's way. The **greater jihad** is the inner struggle to live as a good Muslim; the **lesser jihad** is military defence under the strict conditions above. Groups that use "jihad" to justify terrorism break those conditions, and most Muslims say their actions are not jihad at all.
- **Non-religious** people often argue that religion's claim to divine approval makes wars more extreme, and see holy wars as evidence that religion causes conflict.

### Pacifism

**Pacifism** is the belief that war and violence can never be justified. A **conscientious objector** refuses to fight on grounds of conscience; in both World Wars many did alternative service, e.g. as ambulance workers.

**Contrasting beliefs about pacifism**

| View | Belief |
|---|---|
| **Quakers** (Religious Society of Friends) | a pacifist church: their **Peace Testimony** (declared in 1660) rejects all war. They believe there is "that of God" in everyone, so no one should be killed; Quakers served in the Friends Ambulance Unit in both World Wars and work for peace and reconciliation today |
| **Other Christian pacifists** | follow Jesus' teaching — "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44), "put your sword back" (Matthew 26:52) — and point out that the early Christians refused to serve in the Roman army; groups include **Pax Christi** (Catholic) and the **Anglican Pacifist Fellowship** |
| **Most Christians** | are not absolute pacifists: they prefer peace but accept that war may be the "lesser of two evils" under the just war theory — e.g. to stop Nazi Germany. Many Christians serve in the armed forces, and chaplains serve with them |
| **Islam** | most Muslims are not pacifists: Islam is a religion of peace, but fighting in self-defence or to defend the oppressed is permitted and can be a duty (Qur'an 22:39). Peace is always to be sought (Qur'an 8:61). Some Muslims have led non-violent movements, e.g. **Abdul Ghaffar Khan**, who organised a non-violent Muslim movement against British rule in India |
| **Non-religious** | some are pacifists — the atheist philosopher **Bertrand Russell** was imprisoned in 1918 for opposing the First World War; others accept that war can sometimes be justified to protect human rights |

**Arguments for pacifism**: war kills innocent people and destroys lives and communities; violence breeds more violence; peaceful means (negotiation, sanctions, non-violent protest) can work, as Gandhi and Martin Luther King showed; sanctity of life. **Arguments against**: it can let aggressors and tyrants win (would pacifism have stopped Hitler?); governments have a duty to protect their people; the just war theory and Islamic teaching allow force to defend the innocent.

::: terms
- **Peace**: the absence of conflict, bringing harmony and well-being between people and nations.
- **Justice**: bringing about what is right and fair, according to the law or to a fair share for all.
- **Forgiveness**: pardoning someone for what they have done wrong and moving on without seeking revenge.
- **Reconciliation**: restoring friendly relationships after a conflict, so that former enemies are at peace.
- **Terrorism**: the unlawful use of violence, usually against civilians, to create fear and achieve political or religious aims.
- **Retaliation**: deliberately harming someone in return for harm they have caused — getting revenge.
- **Just war theory**: a set of criteria, from Augustine and Aquinas, that a war must meet to be morally justified.
- **Holy war**: a war fought for a religious cause, believed to be approved by God or declared by a religious leader.
- **Lesser jihad**: in Islam, military struggle to defend the faith or the oppressed, under strict conditions.
- **Pacifism**: the belief that war and violence can never be justified, so disputes must be settled peacefully.
- **Conscientious objector**: a person who refuses to fight in a war on grounds of conscience.
:::

::: method
**Paper 2A gives each theme one five-part question: 1, 2, 4, 5 and 12 marks (24 in all).** Allow about 25 minutes per theme.

1. **1 mark — multiple choice**: four options A–D. The 2023 paper asked which word best means accepting someone's apology (forgiveness) — know the four key-concept definitions exactly.
2. **2 marks — "Give two..."**: two short, correct, different points — e.g. two criteria of a just war, two reasons for war. Only the first two count.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two contrasting (or similar) religious beliefs about..." — "In your answer you must refer to one or more religious traditions."** Two beliefs, each 1 mark simple + 1 mark detailed. **Violence** and **pacifism** are named "contrasting beliefs" issues: the specification requires **Christianity and at least one other religious tradition**, so set a Christian view against a Muslim one — e.g. Quaker pacifism against the Muslim belief that fighting in self-defence is permitted (Qur'an 22:39). The contrast within Christianity (Quakers against the just war majority) or a non-religious view can be added, but never as the only contrast. If both beliefs say the same thing, only one is credited. If the question says **similar**, give two beliefs on the same side — contrasting ones lose marks the same way.
4. **4 marks — "Explain two ways / reasons..."**: 2 + 2 — the point, then detail, example or effect.
5. **5 marks — "Explain two... Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching in your answer."** Two developed points plus **1 mark for a relevant, accurate source**: Matthew 5:9, 5:44 or 26:52; Qur'an 2:190, 8:61, 22:39 or 42:40; the Catechism's just war conditions; the Quaker Peace Testimony. Link it to the point.
6. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should give reasoned arguments in support of this statement; should give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; should refer to religious arguments; may refer to non-religious arguments; should reach a justified conclusion."** Plus **3 SPaG** marks. Spell pacifism, reconciliation, conscientious and proportionality correctly.

**How the 12-mark levels are reached** (the whole answer goes in one level):
- **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with reasons stated.
- **Level 2 (4–6)**: one point of view argued with a logical chain, **or** different views recognised, each with reasons. **Capped at Level 2 with no reference to religion.**
- **Level 3 (7–9)**: reasoned consideration of **different** points of view, with logical chains that draw on knowledge, and **clear reference to religion**.
- **Level 4 (10–12)**: a well-argued response — different views weighed with logical chains **leading to a justified judgement**, religion *applied* to the issue.

For war questions, *apply* the just war criteria to a real case rather than just listing them, and show the range of views inside Christianity (Quaker, just war) and Islam (lesser jihad, peaceful resistance). A good conclusion weighs the ideal of peace against the duty to protect the innocent.
:::

::: example 4-mark contrasting beliefs
Explain two contrasting religious beliefs about pacifism. In your answer you must refer to one or more religious traditions. (4)

Quakers believe in pacifism: war and violence are always wrong (1). They believe there is "that of God" in every person, so killing anyone is killing someone God loves; this is why their Peace Testimony rejects all war and many Quakers were conscientious objectors who served in the Friends Ambulance Unit instead of fighting (1).

In contrast, most Muslims are not pacifists and believe fighting can be right in self-defence (1). The Qur'an gives permission to fight to those who are attacked and wronged (Qur'an 22:39), so the lesser jihad is allowed to defend the faith or the oppressed, though only as a last resort and without harming civilians (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark with a source
Explain two religious teachings about forgiveness. Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

Christians believe they must forgive others without limit (1). When Peter asked how many times he should forgive, Jesus said "seventy-seven times" (Matthew 18:22), meaning there should be no limit; Christians pray "forgive us our sins as we forgive those who sin against us", so they believe God's forgiveness is linked to their own (1) (source 1).

Muslims believe forgiveness is better than revenge (1). The Qur'an allows a wrong to be answered with something equal, but says whoever pardons and makes reconciliation will be rewarded by Allah (Qur'an 42:40), and Allah himself is Al-Ghafur, the Forgiving, so Muslims try to pardon others as they hope to be pardoned (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark evaluation
"There is no such thing as a just war." Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should give reasoned arguments in support of this statement; should give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; should refer to religious arguments; may refer to non-religious arguments; should reach a justified conclusion. (12)

Some people agree. Quakers and other Christian pacifists believe that Jesus taught "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44) and told Peter to put away his sword, so killing can never be what Jesus wants. They argue that no modern war can meet the criterion of discrimination, because bombing always kills civilians, so the just war theory is only used to make wars look respectable. War also often fails its own tests: its results are unpredictable, so "reasonable chance of success" and "proportionality" are guesses. Bertrand Russell, an atheist, opposed the First World War because millions died for no clear gain.

However, most Christians and Muslims disagree. The Catholic Church sets out the conditions of a just war in the Catechism, based on Augustine and Aquinas, because sometimes refusing to fight lets greater evil win. The Second World War is often given as an example: it had a just cause (stopping Nazi aggression and genocide), was declared by a legitimate authority and came after attempts at peace had failed. Islam also permits the lesser jihad in self-defence: "Fight in the way of Allah those who fight you, but do not transgress limits" (Qur'an 2:190), with rules that protect civilians and demand peace when the enemy seeks it (Qur'an 8:61). For these believers, love of neighbour sometimes means defending the innocent.

In conclusion, I disagree with the statement. It is true that many wars fail the just war criteria, especially discrimination, and the pacifist argument reminds us that war is always evil. But the fact that some wars are unjust does not prove that all are. When a nation is invaded or a people faces genocide, fighting may be the lesser evil, so a war can be just, although it can never be good.

(Level 4: pacifist and just war views argued in chains, Christian, Muslim and non-religious views applied, and a justified judgement.)
:::
