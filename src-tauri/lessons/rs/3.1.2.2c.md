## What it is

### Pilgrimage: its role and importance

A **pilgrimage** is a journey made for religious reasons to a **holy place** — somewhere linked with Jesus, a saint, a vision or a miracle. The journey itself is part of the experience: pilgrims leave ordinary life behind so they can focus on God. Christian pilgrimage has a long history: Jesus went up to Jerusalem with his family for Passover (Luke 2:41–42), and the Psalms speak of the joy of going "to the house of the Lord" (Psalm 122:1). Popular destinations include the **Holy Land** (Bethlehem, Jerusalem), **Rome**, **Santiago de Compostela** in Spain, **Walsingham** and **Canterbury** in England, **Taizé** in France, and the two named in the specification, **Lourdes** and **Iona**.

**Why Christians go on pilgrimage (its role):**
- To **grow closer to God** and strengthen their faith away from the distractions of daily life.
- To **pray** for something — healing, guidance, a sick relative — or to **give thanks**.
- To show **repentance** (penance) and seek forgiveness; in the Middle Ages pilgrimages were often set as a penance.
- To **feel connected** with Jesus, the saints and past believers by being where they were.
- To **meet other Christians** and experience the worldwide Church.
- To have time for **reflection**, often at a turning point in life, and come back **renewed**.
- To **serve**: many pilgrims go as volunteers to help sick or disabled pilgrims.

**Different Christian attitudes:**
- **Catholic and Orthodox** Christians often value pilgrimage highly as an act of devotion, linked to the saints, the Virgin Mary and the sacraments.
- Many **Protestants** see pilgrimage as helpful but **not necessary** — God is everywhere and can be worshipped anywhere. Jesus told the Samaritan woman that true worshippers will worship "in the Spirit and in truth", not only on a particular mountain or in Jerusalem (John 4:21–24).
- Some Christians see the **whole of life as a pilgrimage** — the "inner journey" towards God. Hebrews 11:13 describes believers as "foreigners and strangers on earth", and John Bunyan's *The Pilgrim's Progress* (1678) describes the Christian life as a journey to the Celestial City.
- Critics (including some Christians) say pilgrimages can be **expensive** and **commercialised** (souvenir shops), and the money could help the poor instead.

### Lourdes

| | Lourdes |
|---|---|
| **Where** | A town in the foothills of the Pyrenees in south-west France |
| **Why holy** | In **1858**, a fourteen-year-old local girl, **Bernadette Soubirous**, saw **eighteen visions** of the **Virgin Mary** in the **Grotto of Massabielle**, a cave by the river. Mary told her to drink from a spring and wash in it; Bernadette dug in the ground and a **spring** appeared. In one vision the lady said "I am the Immaculate Conception". The Catholic Church later accepted the visions as genuine, and Bernadette was made a saint in 1933. |
| **Who goes** | Mainly **Catholics** — several million visitors every year, including many sick and disabled people |
| **What pilgrims do** | Visit and pray at the **Grotto**; bathe in the **baths** filled with spring water or drink and collect the water; attend **Mass** in the basilicas; join the evening **torchlight (Marian) procession**, singing and praying the rosary; join the **Blessed Sacrament procession**, where the sick are blessed; walk the **Stations of the Cross**; go to confession; volunteer to help the sick |
| **Healing** | Many pilgrims go hoping for healing. The **Lourdes Medical Bureau** investigates claimed cures; only about seventy have been officially declared miracles by the Church after strict checks. Most pilgrims say the real "healing" is spiritual — peace, strength to cope and a deeper faith |

**Why Lourdes is important:** it strengthens faith in Mary's prayers for believers and in God's power to heal; the sick are placed **at the centre**, treated with dignity and cared for by volunteers (for example the UK charity HCPT takes groups of disabled and disadvantaged young people there); pilgrims experience a huge international Catholic community worshipping together.

### Iona

| | Iona |
|---|---|
| **Where** | A small island off the Isle of Mull in the Inner Hebrides, off the west coast of **Scotland** |
| **Why holy** | **St Columba** came from Ireland in **563 CE** and founded a monastery there; Iona became a centre for spreading Christianity through Scotland and northern England. Later a Benedictine abbey was built. |
| **The Iona Community** | Founded in **1938** by **Rev George MacLeod**, a Church of Scotland minister from Glasgow, who brought ministers and unemployed craftsmen together to rebuild the ruined abbey. It is an **ecumenical** community (open to Christians of all denominations) whose members follow a rule that includes **daily prayer and Bible reading** and **working for justice and peace** |
| **Who goes** | Christians of all denominations — often Protestant in style — and people exploring faith |
| **What pilgrims do** | Stay at the **abbey** for a week, **living in community** — sharing meals and chores; attend **daily worship** in the abbey (morning and evening, with simple, modern songs); join a **weekly pilgrimage walk around the island**, stopping for prayer and reflection at places such as St Columba's Bay; spend time in **quiet reflection** amid the natural beauty; take part in discussions on issues such as peace, poverty and the environment |

**Why Iona is important:** it offers **peace and quiet** to reflect and pray; it connects pilgrims to the roots of Christianity in Britain; living together teaches **community** and service; and its focus on **justice, peace and creation** sends pilgrims home to act on their faith in everyday life.

### Contrasting Lourdes and Iona

| | Lourdes | Iona |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Mary, healing, the sacraments | Reflection, community life, justice and peace |
| Atmosphere | Crowded, busy, large processions | Remote, quiet, small numbers |
| Tradition | Catholic | Ecumenical, often Protestant |
| Worship | Mass, rosary, processions, baths | Simple daily services, prayer walks, silence |
| What pilgrims hope for | Healing (physical and spiritual), Mary's prayers | Renewal, a closer relationship with God, inspiration to act |

### Christmas

**What it celebrates:** the **birth of Jesus** in Bethlehem — the **incarnation**, God becoming human (Luke 2:1–20; Matthew 1:18–2:12). "The Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us" (John 1:14).

**When:** **25 December** for Western churches. Many Orthodox Christians (for example in Russia) follow the older Julian calendar and celebrate on **7 January**.

**How it is celebrated:**
- **Advent** — four weeks of preparation beforehand: Advent wreaths with a candle lit each Sunday, Advent calendars, prayer and reflection on Jesus's coming (and second coming).
- **Midnight Mass** or a midnight communion service on Christmas Eve, and services on Christmas Day.
- **Carol services** — for example the Festival of Nine Lessons and Carols, broadcast from King's College, Cambridge; carol singing to raise money for charity.
- **Nativity plays** in schools and churches, and **cribs** (nativity scenes) showing Mary, Joseph, the baby Jesus, shepherds and wise men.
- **Christingle** services — an orange (the world) with a candle (Jesus, the light of the world), a red ribbon (his blood) and fruit or sweets on sticks (God's gifts and the four seasons), often raising money for The Children's Society.
- **Giving gifts** — remembering the gifts of the wise men and God's gift of Jesus; family meals.
- **Charity** — many churches serve Christmas meals to people who are homeless or alone, and the charity Crisis runs "Crisis at Christmas".
- The season ends with **Epiphany** (6 January), remembering the visit of the wise men.

**Importance for Christians in Great Britain today:**
- It celebrates God's love in sending Jesus — a message of **hope, peace and light**.
- It is a time when **many people who rarely go to church attend**, so it is an opportunity to share the Christian message.
- Christmas Day and Boxing Day are **public holidays**, and many traditions (carols, nativities, cards) remain part of British culture.
- However, many Christians worry that Christmas has become **commercialised** and **secular** — focused on shopping, Father Christmas and parties rather than Jesus — and try to "keep Christ in Christmas".

### Easter

**What it celebrates:** the **resurrection of Jesus** three days after his crucifixion. It is the **most important** Christian festival, because the resurrection shows that Jesus is the Son of God, has defeated sin and death, and that believers will also rise to eternal life. Paul wrote that "if Christ has not been raised, our preaching is useless and so is your faith" (1 Corinthians 15:14).

**When:** a Sunday in spring (the date varies each year). Orthodox Easter (**Pascha**) often falls on a later date because it is calculated differently.

**How it is celebrated:**
- **Lent** — forty days of preparation, beginning on **Ash Wednesday** (ashes are marked on the forehead as a sign of repentance), with prayer, fasting or giving something up, and charity. **Shrove Tuesday** (Pancake Day) comes just before.
- **Holy Week:**
  - **Palm Sunday** — Jesus's entry into Jerusalem; palm crosses are given out and there may be processions.
  - **Maundy Thursday** — the Last Supper; some churches hold a foot-washing ceremony, following Jesus's example of service (John 13). In Britain the monarch gives out **Maundy money** to older people.
  - **Good Friday** — the crucifixion; solemn services, often from noon to 3pm, with readings of the Passion, the **Stations of the Cross**, bare altars, and **processions of witness** carrying a cross through town centres; hot cross buns are eaten.
- **Easter Vigil** (Catholic and many Anglican churches, on Saturday night) — a new fire is lit and the **Paschal candle** carried into the dark church ("The light of Christ"); new members are often baptised.
- **Easter Sunday** — joyful services, some at **sunrise** outdoors; churches are filled with flowers; the greeting "Christ is risen! He is risen indeed!"; **Easter eggs** symbolise new life and the empty tomb.
- **Orthodox** Christians hold a midnight service: the church is in darkness, then candles are lit from one flame and people process round the church proclaiming "Christ is risen!"; red-dyed eggs represent Christ's blood and new life.

**Importance for Christians in Great Britain today:**
- It is the **heart of the faith**: without the resurrection, Christianity would lose its meaning.
- It gives **hope** of eternal life and of good overcoming evil.
- Lent and Holy Week give Christians time to **reflect, repent** and renew their commitment.
- Good Friday and Easter Monday are **bank holidays**, and Easter remains a major time for church attendance.
- Some Christians feel its meaning is lost when it becomes mainly about chocolate eggs and bunnies.

::: terms
- **Pilgrimage**: a journey made for religious reasons to a holy place, such as Lourdes, Iona or the Holy Land.
- **Pilgrim**: a person who makes a pilgrimage to grow closer to God, pray, give thanks or seek healing.
- **Lourdes**: a town in south-west France where Bernadette Soubirous saw visions of the Virgin Mary in 1858; a major Catholic place of pilgrimage and healing.
- **Iona**: a small Scottish island where St Columba founded a monastery in 563 CE; home of the ecumenical Iona Community.
- **Ecumenical**: bringing together Christians of different denominations, as the Iona Community does.
- **Incarnation**: the belief that God became human in Jesus, celebrated at Christmas.
- **Advent**: the four weeks of preparation before Christmas, looking forward to Jesus's birth and his second coming.
- **Resurrection**: Jesus rising from the dead on the third day after the crucifixion, celebrated at Easter.
- **Lent**: the forty days of prayer, fasting and repentance before Easter, beginning on Ash Wednesday.
- **Holy Week**: the week before Easter Sunday — Palm Sunday, Maundy Thursday and Good Friday — remembering the last days of Jesus.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 (Christianity), Practices question: five parts worth 1, 2, 4, 5 and 12 marks.** Spend about 25 minutes on the whole question.

1. **1 mark — multiple choice**: four options, choose one. Know which place goes with which person (Lourdes — Bernadette; Iona — Columba, George MacLeod) and which festival goes with which belief (Christmas — incarnation; Easter — resurrection).
2. **2 marks — "Give two…"**: two brief, separate facts ("bathing in the spring water"; "the torchlight procession"). No explanation needed.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two ways…" / "Explain two contrasting…"**: two points, each **developed** (simple point + detail/example/effect on the Christian). "Explain two contrasting examples of Christian pilgrimage" is a favourite: describe Lourdes and Iona and **make the contrast explicit** — healing and Mary vs quiet reflection and community.
4. **5 marks — "Explain two… Refer to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching in your answer"**: two developed points (4 marks) **plus 1 mark for a relevant, accurate source**. Useful sources: Psalm 122:1 and Luke 2:41–42 (pilgrimage); John 4:21–24 (God can be worshipped anywhere); John 1:14 and Luke 2 (Christmas); 1 Corinthians 15:14 and Mark 16 (Easter). Link the source to your point.
5. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement."** Paper 1 bullets: *refer to Christian teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion.* SPaG on Paper 1 goes on the Beliefs 12-marks, not this Practices one.

**How the 12-mark levels are reached** (the whole answer is placed in one level):
- **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with a reason stated.
- **Level 2 (4–6)**: one view argued with a logical chain of reasoning, **or** different views recognised, each with reasons. **Maximum Level 2 with no reference to religion.**
- **Level 3 (7–9)**: reasoned consideration of **different** points of view, with logical chains that draw on knowledge, and **clear reference to religion**.
- **Level 4 (10–12)**: a **well-argued** response — different views weighed with logical chains **leading to a justified judgement**, with religious teaching *applied* to the issue.

Typical statements: "Pilgrimage is not necessary for Christians", "Easter is more important than Christmas", "Christmas has lost its meaning in Britain today". Use the **beliefs behind the practice** (incarnation, resurrection, God everywhere) as your evidence, show **diversity** (Catholic vs Protestant attitudes to pilgrimage) and end with a conclusion that weighs which argument is stronger and why.
:::

::: example 4-mark — contrasting pilgrimages
Explain two contrasting examples of Christian pilgrimage. (4)

Lourdes in France is a place where Catholics go to seek healing (1): Bernadette saw visions of the Virgin Mary there in 1858 and a spring appeared, so pilgrims bathe in the water, pray at the Grotto and join the torchlight procession, hoping for physical or spiritual healing (1). In contrast, Iona in Scotland is a quiet island where pilgrims go for reflection and community (1): they stay at the abbey run by the ecumenical Iona Community, share chores and daily worship and walk round the island praying, focusing on peace and justice rather than miracles (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark — why Easter matters
Explain two reasons why Easter is important for Christians. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

First, Easter celebrates Jesus's resurrection, which is the centre of the Christian faith (1). It proves that Jesus is the Son of God and has defeated sin and death; Paul wrote that "if Christ has not been raised, our preaching is useless and so is your faith" (1 Corinthians 15:14) (source, 1), so without Easter there would be no Christianity (1). Second, Easter gives Christians hope of eternal life (1). Because Jesus rose, they believe they too will be raised, which comforts them when people die and is why many are baptised or renew their baptismal promises at the Easter Vigil, as the Paschal candle is lit to show Christ's light overcoming darkness (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark — is pilgrimage necessary?
"Pilgrimage is not important for Christians today." Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should: refer to Christian teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion. (12)

Some Christians, especially many Protestants, would agree. They believe God is present everywhere, so there is no need to travel to a particular place to meet him. Jesus told the Samaritan woman that true worshippers would worship "in the Spirit and in truth", not only on a mountain or in Jerusalem (John 4:21–24). Pilgrimage is not one of the sacraments and is not commanded by Jesus, so it is optional. Some also argue that pilgrimages are expensive and commercialised — Lourdes is full of souvenir shops — and that the money would be better spent helping the poor, which Jesus clearly commanded (Matthew 25:35–40).

On the other hand, many Christians, particularly Catholics and Orthodox, see pilgrimage as very important. Going to Lourdes allows sick pilgrims to pray for healing where Mary appeared to Bernadette, and even if they are not cured, many find strength and peace to cope with illness; the volunteers who care for them put Christian love into action. Pilgrimage has deep roots in the Bible: Jesus went to Jerusalem for Passover (Luke 2:41–42). Iona shows it need not be about miracles: pilgrims live in community, pray and reflect on justice and peace, then return home renewed to serve others. In a busy modern world, a pilgrimage may be one of the few chances Christians get to step away from distractions and focus on God.

In conclusion, I disagree with the statement. It is true that pilgrimage is not essential for salvation, and no Christian is required to go. But "not important" goes too far: millions still go, and the effect on their faith and on the way they live afterwards is real. The argument that God is everywhere is strong, yet it does not stop a special place from helping people notice God — just as a church building does. So pilgrimage is not necessary, but it remains important for many Christians today.
:::

::: q
"Easter is more important than Christmas." Evaluate this statement. (12)
---
For: the resurrection proves Jesus is the Son of God and has defeated death — "if Christ has not been raised... your faith is futile" (1 Corinthians 15:14–17); it is the basis of the hope of eternal life; the Church has always treated Easter as the greatest feast, with Lent and Holy Week leading up to it; salvation comes through the death and resurrection.
Against: without the incarnation there could be no crucifixion or resurrection — Christmas is where salvation begins (John 1:14); Christmas attracts more people to church in Britain and is a key chance to share the faith; the two belong together as one story.
Justified conclusion, e.g. Easter is theologically more important, though the two cannot be separated. Levels as in the method box.
