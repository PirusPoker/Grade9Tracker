## What it is

This topic is the first half of **Theme A: Relationships and families**. You need religious teachings and religious, philosophical and ethical arguments about: **human sexuality** (heterosexual and homosexual relationships); **sexual relationships before and outside marriage**; **contraception and family planning**; the **nature and purpose of marriage**; **same-sex marriage and cohabitation**; **divorce** (reasons, and remarrying); and **ethical arguments about divorce**, including the **sanctity of marriage vows** and **compassion**. You must know **contrasting perspectives in contemporary British society**: Christian (the main religious tradition in Britain) and Muslim views, with their internal diversity, and non-religious views (humanist and atheist).

Three issues are named in the spec as **"contrasting beliefs"** issues: **contraception**, **sexual relationships before marriage** and **homosexual relationships**. For these you must be able to contrast beliefs with reference to **Christianity and one or more other religious traditions** (here Islam). Non-religious views can be added but cannot replace the religious contrast.

### The law in Britain (context)

- Sex between men was partly decriminalised in England and Wales in **1967**. **Civil partnerships** for same-sex couples came in with the Civil Partnership Act **2004**; the **Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013** allowed same-sex marriage in England and Wales from **March 2014**, with protection so that no religious organisation can be forced to conduct one (the Church of England is barred unless it changes its own rules). Opposite-sex civil partnerships have been allowed since 2019.
- The **age of consent** is 16. Adultery is not a crime but has been a legal basis for divorce.
- The **Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020** brought in **"no-fault" divorce** from April 2022: one or both spouses state that the marriage has broken down irretrievably, and no one has to be blamed.

### Sexual relationships before and outside marriage

**Before marriage (pre-marital sex)**

| | View |
|---|---|
| **Christian — traditional** | Sex before marriage is wrong: sex expresses a lifelong commitment that only exists in marriage. "Flee from sexual immorality" (1 Corinthians 6:18); marriage should be honoured and the marriage bed kept pure (Hebrews 13:4). The Catholic Church teaches that fornication is gravely contrary to human dignity (Catechism 2353). Many young Christians choose to wait. |
| **Christian — liberal** | Many Christians accept sex in a **loving, committed, faithful** relationship, especially where the couple intend to marry; love and commitment matter more than a ceremony. |
| **Islam** | Sex before marriage is **zina** and is forbidden: "do not approach unlawful sexual intercourse. Indeed, it is ever an immorality and is evil as a way" (Qur'an 17:32). Muslims are told to dress and behave modestly (24:30-31) and to avoid situations that could lead to temptation. Families often help young people to marry, sometimes through arranged (never forced) marriages. |
| **Humanist / atheist** | Acceptable between consenting adults who act responsibly (e.g. using contraception) and honestly; it is a personal choice. |

**Outside marriage (adultery)**: almost everyone — Christian, Muslim and non-religious — condemns adultery. "You shall not commit adultery" is one of the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:14); Jesus taught that even looking at someone lustfully is adultery in the heart (Matthew 5:27-28), yet he forgave the woman caught in adultery and told her to sin no more (John 8:1-11). In Islam adultery is zina (17:32) and a very serious sin. Humanists oppose it because it breaks **trust and promises** and harms the partner and children.

### Contraception and family planning

**Contraception** means preventing pregnancy: **artificial** methods (condom, pill, coil, implant, sterilisation) or **natural** methods (natural family planning — having sex only at the infertile times of the woman's cycle). **Family planning** means choosing how many children to have and when.

| | View | Reasons / teaching |
|---|---|---|
| **Catholic** | **Artificial** contraception is wrong; **natural family planning** is allowed for serious reasons such as spacing births. | *Humanae Vitae* (Pope Paul VI, 1968) teaches that every sexual act must remain open to the transmission of life; this follows **natural law** — the natural purpose of sex includes procreation. "Be fruitful and multiply" (Genesis 1:28). Catechism 2370. Some methods (e.g. the morning-after pill) may be seen as causing an early abortion. |
| **Church of England and most Protestant churches** | Contraception is a matter of **individual conscience** for a married couple; responsible family planning is good. | The 1930 Lambeth Conference first accepted contraception in some circumstances. Sex is also for love and unity, not only children; planning helps a couple care properly for the children they have and protects the mother's health. |
| **Some Christians** | Accept contraception but not methods that may work after fertilisation. | Sanctity of life from conception |
| **Islam** | Most Muslims accept contraception within marriage, for good reasons such as **spacing births**, protecting the **mother's health** or avoiding serious hardship, as long as **both** spouses agree and it is not used to avoid having children altogether. Many prefer natural or reversible methods; **permanent** methods (sterilisation) are generally not allowed unless for health reasons; methods that work after conception are opposed by many. | Some companions practised withdrawal (**'azl**) in the Prophet's lifetime and it was not forbidden (hadith, Bukhari and Muslim). Some scholars cite "Allah wants to lighten your burden" (4:28). Some Muslims oppose contraception, citing "do not kill your children for fear of poverty" (17:31) and trusting God to provide. |
| **Humanist / atheist** | Contraception is responsible and good. | Prevents unwanted pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections; lets couples plan; allows women control over their lives and careers; helps limit population growth. |

### The nature and purpose of marriage

**Marriage** is a legal union between two people, and for religious believers a commitment made before God.

**Christianity**
- Marriage is a **gift from God**: "a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24). Jesus repeated this and added "What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate" (Mark 10:9).
- For Catholics and Orthodox Christians marriage is a **sacrament** — a sign of God's grace; for most Protestants it is a holy **covenant** (a binding agreement before God). Paul compares the love of husband and wife to Christ's love for the Church (Ephesians 5:25).
- **Purposes**, as the Church of England wedding service puts them: to be a lifelong union of mutual love, help and comfort; to be the right place for sexual union; and to provide a stable place for **having and raising children**.
- Vows are made in front of God and witnesses: to love and cherish "for better, for worse, for richer, for poorer, in sickness and in health… till death us do part". Rings symbolise never-ending love.

**Islam**
- Marriage (**nikah**) is a **contract** before God and witnesses, strongly encouraged as the **sunnah** of the Prophet; a well-known hadith reports that marriage is part of his sunnah and whoever turns away from it is not of him. The Qur'an tells the community to marry off the unmarried among them (24:32).
- **Purposes**: companionship, love and mercy — "He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy" (Qur'an 30:21); the right place for sex; having and bringing up children as Muslims; and uniting families.
- Both partners must **freely consent** (forced marriage is forbidden), there must be witnesses, and the husband gives the wife a gift called the **mahr** (4:4), which is hers to keep. A Muslim man may marry a Christian or Jewish woman (5:5); traditionally a Muslim woman marries only a Muslim man. In Britain a nikah must also be legally registered to count as a legal marriage.

**Non-religious**: many atheists and humanists marry because it expresses **public, lifelong commitment**, gives legal and financial security, and provides stability for children — but they see it as a human institution, not a religious duty. **Humanist weddings** have been legally recognised in **Scotland since 2005** and **Northern Ireland since 2018**; in England and Wales the government has announced it will recognise them, but for now couples still need a separate civil ceremony for their marriage to be legal.

### Same-sex marriage and cohabitation

**Same-sex marriage** (legal in England and Wales since 2014):
- **Catholic Church**: marriage can only be between a man and a woman, open to children, so same-sex marriage is not possible. In 2023 the Vatican document *Fiducia Supplicans* allowed priests to give short, informal blessings to couples in "irregular" situations, including same-sex couples, while making clear this does not approve of the relationship or equal a wedding.
- **Church of England**: does not marry same-sex couples, but allows prayers of blessing (Prayers of Love and Faith, 2023).
- **Quakers** (who decided to celebrate same-sex marriages in 2009), the **Methodist Church** (vote in 2021), the **United Reformed Church** and the **Scottish Episcopal Church** allow same-sex weddings — because love and commitment are what make a marriage.
- **Islam**: most Muslims do not accept same-sex marriage, because nikah is between a man and a woman and homosexual acts are forbidden; Muslims in Britain accept that it is legal for others.
- **Humanists**: strongly support it — equal love deserves equal rights.

**Cohabitation** — an unmarried couple living together in a sexual relationship. It is now very common in Britain, and many couples cohabit before marrying.
- **Catholic** and many traditional Christians: wrong, because sex belongs within marriage and cohabitation lacks the public, lifelong commitment made before God.
- **Church of England** and liberal Christians: generally disapprove in principle but recognise that many cohabiting couples are faithful and committed, and may see it as a step towards marriage; churches welcome cohabiting couples who want to marry.
- **Islam**: not allowed, since sex outside nikah is zina.
- **Humanists / atheists**: a personal choice; commitment and love matter more than a certificate. Some point out that cohabiting partners have fewer legal rights than married ones if they split up.

### Divorce and remarriage

**Divorce** is the legal ending of a marriage. **Reasons** people divorce include: adultery; domestic abuse; falling out of love or growing apart; addiction; money problems or stress; one partner changing their faith or values; a marriage entered too young or too quickly.

| | Divorce | Remarriage |
|---|---|---|
| **Catholic** | A valid sacramental marriage **cannot be dissolved** by anyone but death (Mark 10:9; Catechism 2382). A civil divorce may be tolerated to protect a spouse or children (e.g. from abuse), but the couple are still married in God's eyes. An **annulment** is a declaration that a valid marriage never existed (e.g. one partner did not freely consent). | Not allowed in church while the former spouse is alive, since Jesus said that whoever divorces and marries another commits adultery (Mark 10:11-12). |
| **Church of England** | Marriage is meant to be lifelong, but divorce is accepted as sometimes the lesser of two evils when a marriage has truly broken down. | Since 2002 a divorced person may remarry in church at the discretion of the priest; otherwise a service of blessing after a civil wedding. |
| **Orthodox** | Divorce may be granted in some circumstances (e.g. adultery). | Permitted — up to three marriages; later weddings have a more penitential tone. |
| **Other Protestants** (e.g. Methodists) | Accept divorce where the marriage has broken down; reconciliation is encouraged first. | Generally allowed. Some point to Jesus' exception for sexual immorality (Matthew 19:9). |
| **Islam** | Divorce is **permitted but disliked**: a hadith (Abu Dawud) reports that of all lawful things, the most hated by God is divorce. **Reconciliation** must be tried first, with an arbitrator from each family (4:35). A husband pronounces **talaq**; then comes a waiting period (**'iddah**) of three monthly cycles (2:228) in which the couple can reconcile. Divorce can be revoked twice; after the third it is final — "divorce is twice; then either keep [her] in an acceptable manner or release [her] with good treatment" (2:229). A wife can seek divorce by **khul'** (returning the mahr) or through a judge or **Shari'ah council**. Shi'a Islam requires the talaq to be said in set words before two just witnesses (65:2), and does not count three talaqs said at once as more than one. | **Allowed** and often encouraged, so no one is left alone or without support; the Prophet himself married widows and a divorcee. |
| **Humanist / atheist** | Divorce is often the most **compassionate** and rational choice when a marriage has failed; staying in an unhappy or abusive marriage harms both partners and children. Couples should think carefully about children's welfare and try to separate fairly. | Allowed — a second chance at happiness. |

### Ethical arguments related to divorce

**The sanctity of marriage vows** (argument **against** divorce):
- Marriage vows are **sacred promises** made before God — "till death us do part". Breaking them breaks a promise to God as well as to the partner.
- Jesus taught "What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate" (Mark 10:9); for Catholics the sacrament creates an unbreakable bond.
- Keeping promises is important for society: if vows can be broken easily, trust and stability (especially for children) are weakened. Couples should work at reconciliation, counselling and forgiveness ("seventy times seven", Matthew 18:22).
- Some non-religious people also stress the importance of keeping solemn promises.

**Compassion** (argument **for** allowing divorce):
- Jesus showed **compassion and forgiveness** (e.g. the woman caught in adultery, John 8:1-11); the Church should not force someone to stay trapped, especially in an **abusive** marriage.
- Christian **situation ethics** (Joseph Fletcher) says do the most loving thing in each situation; sometimes divorce is the loving outcome for everyone.
- **Utilitarian** reasoning: divorce may bring the greatest happiness for the greatest number, including children who suffer from constant conflict.
- Islam allows divorce as a merciful provision when a marriage has failed; the Qur'an says to part with kindness (2:229).
- Humanists emphasise the **wellbeing** of those involved over tradition.

A common Christian and Muslim position combines both: marriage should be for life and every effort should be made to save it, but when it has genuinely broken down, divorce may be the lesser evil.

::: terms
- **Heterosexual**: sexually attracted to people of the opposite sex.
- **Homosexual**: sexually attracted to people of the same sex.
- **Cohabitation**: an unmarried couple living together in a sexual relationship.
- **Contraception**: methods used to prevent a pregnancy, either artificial (e.g. the pill) or natural (family planning by the woman's cycle).
- **Family planning**: choosing how many children to have and when, often using contraception.
- **Adultery**: sexual activity between a married person and someone who is not their spouse.
- **Sanctity of marriage vows**: the belief that marriage promises are sacred because they are made before God and should never be broken.
- **Annulment**: a Catholic declaration that a marriage was never valid, so the couple were never truly married.
- **Nikah**: the Muslim marriage contract, agreed freely by both partners before witnesses.
- **Talaq**: divorce in Islam, pronounced by the husband and followed by a waiting period ('iddah).
- **Humanae Vitae**: the 1968 letter of Pope Paul VI teaching that artificial contraception is wrong.
:::

::: method
Theme A is one five-part question on Paper 2: 1 + 2 + 4 + 5 + 12 = 24 marks, with **SPaG (3 marks)** on the 12-mark question. Spend about 25 minutes on the theme.

2. **2 marks — "Give two…"** (e.g. "Give two reasons why many religious believers accept remarriage"). Two short, separate points; only the first two count.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two contrasting beliefs…" / "Explain two similar beliefs…" / "Explain two ways…"** Two points, each 1 mark simple + 1 mark detailed. For **contraception, sex before marriage and homosexual relationships** the question will say: *"In your answer you should refer to the main religious tradition of Great Britain and one or more other religious traditions."* So you **must** use Christianity **plus** at least one other religion (e.g. Islam) — a non-religious view may be added but cannot be your only contrast. Contrasts can also be within Christianity (Catholic vs Church of England). If your two beliefs are really the same, only one is credited.
4. **5 marks — "Explain two religious beliefs about… Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching in your answer."** Two developed beliefs (2 + 2) plus 1 mark for a relevant, accurate source — e.g. Mark 10:9, Genesis 2:24, Humanae Vitae, Catechism 2357-2359, 1 Corinthians 6:18, Qur'an 30:21, 17:32, 2:229, 4:35. 5. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Levels:
   - **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with reason(s) stated.
   - **Level 2 (4–6)**: one view with a logical chain of reasoning, OR different views each supported by reasons. **Maximum Level 2 if there is no reference to religion.**
   - **Level 3 (7–9)**: reasoned consideration of different points of view, logical chains of reasoning, **clear reference to religion**.
   - **Level 4 (10–12)**: a well-argued response — different views developed **and evaluated**, knowledge used as evidence, a judgement that follows from the argument.

::: example 4-mark contrasting (contraception)
Explain two contrasting beliefs in contemporary British society about contraception. In your answer you should refer to the main religious tradition of Great Britain and one or more other religious traditions. (4)

Catholic Christians believe artificial contraception is wrong (1). Humanae Vitae (1968) teaches that every sexual act must remain open to new life, following natural law and God's command to "be fruitful and multiply", so they only use natural family planning (1).

In contrast, most Muslims accept contraception within marriage for good reasons (1). If both husband and wife agree, they may use it to space out their children or protect the mother's health, since withdrawal was practised in the Prophet's time and not forbidden — as long as they do not refuse to have children altogether (1).

::: example 5-mark with source (divorce)
Explain two religious beliefs about divorce. Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

Catholics believe a valid marriage cannot be ended by divorce (1). Marriage is a sacrament that creates a lifelong bond, and Jesus taught "What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate" (Mark 10:9), so divorced Catholics cannot remarry in church while their spouse is alive, although an annulment may be granted if the marriage was never valid (1) (1 for source).

Muslims believe divorce is allowed but should be a last resort (1). The couple must first try to reconcile with the help of an arbitrator from each family, and there is a waiting period ('iddah) so they have time to change their minds; only if this fails is the divorce made final (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark evaluation
"Couples should never get divorced." Evaluate this statement. (12 + 3 SPaG)

Many Christians would agree. Catholics believe marriage is a sacrament that creates a permanent bond, and Jesus said "What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate" (Mark 10:9). The vows "till death us do part" are made before God, so to break them is to break a promise to God himself — the argument from the sanctity of marriage vows. This also protects children, who need the stability of parents who stay together, and encourages couples to work through difficulties with forgiveness and counselling rather than giving up.

However, most Christians in Britain, such as Anglicans and Methodists, accept that a marriage can truly break down. They argue that Jesus showed compassion to people whose relationships had failed, like the woman caught in adultery, and that it cannot be God's will for someone to stay in an abusive marriage. Islam also allows divorce: though a hadith calls it the most hated of lawful things, the Qur'an permits it after reconciliation has failed, telling the husband to release his wife "with good treatment" (2:229). Humanists would add, using utilitarian reasoning, that constant conflict harms children more than a careful separation.

In my judgement the statement is too absolute. The argument from the sanctity of vows is strong — marriage should be entered seriously and every effort made to save it — but the word "never" ignores cases such as domestic abuse, where compassion must come first. Even many who believe marriage is for life, including Muslims and most Protestant churches, accept divorce as the lesser of two evils. So couples should try everything to stay together, but divorce is sometimes the right choice.
:::
