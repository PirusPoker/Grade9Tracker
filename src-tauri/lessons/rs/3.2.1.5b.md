## What it is

This is the second half of **Theme E: Religion, crime and punishment**. You need to know: the **aims of punishment** (retribution, deterrence and reformation); the **treatment of criminals** — **prison, corporal punishment and community service**; **forgiveness**; the **death penalty**; and the **ethical arguments** about the death penalty, including the **principle of utility** and the **sanctity of life**.

Three of these are the theme's set **"contrasting beliefs" issues**: **corporal punishment, the death penalty and forgiveness**. For these you must be able to explain contrasting beliefs **with reference to Christianity (the main religious tradition in Britain) and one or more other religious traditions** — so learn the Christian and the Muslim views side by side, including the differences *within* each religion.

### Why punish at all?

Punishment is what the state does to someone who has broken the law. Christians and Muslims agree that punishment is needed for **justice**: victims deserve to see wrongdoing dealt with, and society needs laws that mean something. Paul taught that the ruler is "God's servant… to bring punishment on the wrongdoer" (**Romans 13:4**); the Qur'an commands justice "even if it be against yourselves" (**4:135**). Humanists agree that punishment is necessary to protect people, but judge each form of it by its results.

### The aims of punishment

| Aim | Meaning | Religious and non-religious views |
|---|---|---|
| **Retribution** | Getting even: the criminal "pays" for what they did, and the punishment matches the crime. | "Life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth" (**Exodus 21:23–25**) was originally a *limit* on revenge. Jesus rejected personal revenge: "turn to them the other cheek" (**Matthew 5:38–39**); "Do not take revenge… 'It is mine to avenge; I will repay,' says the Lord" (**Romans 12:19**). Islam allows equal retribution — "life for a life, eye for an eye" — but adds that whoever gives up that right as charity, it is an expiation for him (**Qur'an 5:45**). |
| **Deterrence** | Putting people off crime: the offender (individual deterrence) and everyone else (general deterrence). | The Qur'an describes the punishment for theft as "a deterrent from Allah" (**5:38**). Christians accept deterrence as part of protecting society. Utilitarians favour it if it prevents more suffering than it causes. Critics say many crimes are unplanned, so offenders do not think about punishment. |
| **Reformation** | Changing the offender so that they will not reoffend — education, training, therapy, faith. | The aim most Christians see as the most important: Jesus came "to seek and to save the lost" (**Luke 19:10**), and Zacchaeus changed completely (**Luke 19:1–10**). In Islam, "whoever repents after his wrongdoing and reforms, indeed, Allah will turn to him in forgiveness" (**Qur'an 5:39**). Humanists stress reform because it reduces future victims. |

Other aims you can use: **protection** (keeping dangerous people away from the public), **reparation** (making up for the harm, e.g. paying compensation) and **vindication** (showing that the law must be respected).

### The treatment of criminals

**Prison**
- Takes away freedom; used for serious crimes. It **protects** the public, can **deter** and provides **retribution**, and prisons offer education, training and drug treatment to **reform**.
- Criticisms: overcrowding; drugs and violence inside; many prisoners reoffend soon after release; prison can be "a school for crime"; families (especially children) suffer too.
- **Christianity**: most Christians accept prison as necessary, but insist prisoners are treated with dignity. Jesus said, "I was in prison and you came to visit me" (**Matthew 25:36**); "Continue to remember those in prison as if you were together with them in prison" (**Hebrews 13:3**). The Quaker **Elizabeth Fry** campaigned in the 1800s to improve conditions for women in Newgate prison. Today there are Christian **prison chaplains** and groups such as **Prison Fellowship**, which support prisoners and their families.
- **Islam**: prison is an acceptable punishment; prisoners must be treated humanely — the righteous "give food… to the needy, the orphan, and the captive" (**Qur'an 76:8**). **Muslim chaplains (imams)** work in British prisons, leading prayers and helping prisoners reform.
- **Humanists**: support prison where needed to protect the public, but want it to focus on rehabilitation, and prefer alternatives for minor offences.

**Corporal punishment** (contrasting-beliefs issue)
- Punishing a person by causing physical pain — **caning, whipping, flogging**. Judicial corporal punishment (birching) was **abolished in Britain in 1948**; it was banned in state schools in 1987 and in all schools by the early 2000s. It is still used in some countries, such as **Singapore** (caning) and **Saudi Arabia** and **Iran** (flogging). Smacking children is banned in **Scotland** and **Wales**; in England, parents may use only "reasonable punishment".
- Arguments **for**: quick and cheap; a strong deterrent; the offender keeps their job and family, unlike prison.
- Arguments **against**: it is **degrading** and breaches human rights (it is banned by the European Convention on Human Rights as interpreted by its court); it does not reform — it may teach that violence solves problems; it can cause lasting physical and psychological harm; it is irreversible if the person was innocent.

| Christianity | Islam |
|---|---|
| **Most Christians oppose** corporal punishment: it does not reform, it harms the dignity of a person made in the image of God (**Genesis 1:27**), and Jesus taught love and mercy, not violence (**Matthew 5:38–39**). | **Many Muslims support** corporal punishment in principle, because it is set by God in the Qur'an for certain crimes — e.g. flogging for sexual immorality (**24:2**) — and so is part of Shari'ah. |
| **A minority of Christians** support physical discipline, especially of children: "Whoever spares the rod hates their children" (**Proverbs 13:24**). Others say this is about discipline in general, not literal beating. | These **hudud** punishments are meant to be a strong deterrent, but require very strict proof (e.g. four witnesses or a confession), and scholars taught that judges should avoid them when there is any doubt. |
| Churches in Britain supported ending corporal punishment in schools and generally back non-violent discipline. | **Many British Muslims** do not want these punishments used in Britain: they may only be carried out by a proper Islamic court in an Islamic state, and Muslims living in Britain keep UK law. Some Muslim scholars (e.g. Tariq Ramadan) have called for a moratorium on hudud punishments worldwide. |

**Humanists** oppose corporal punishment: it is cruel, degrading and ineffective, and there is evidence that physical punishment of children increases aggression.

**Community service**
- A **community order** in England and Wales may include **unpaid work** (between 40 and 300 hours, known as Community Payback), such as cleaning graffiti or clearing wasteland, often with a curfew, electronic tag, drug treatment or supervision. Used mainly for less serious crimes and first-time offenders.
- **For**: the offender **repays** the community (reparation); can keep their job and family; avoids learning crime in prison; cheaper than prison; can help reform by building skills and responsibility.
- **Against**: may seem a **"soft option"** that does not punish enough; the public may feel unsafe; offenders may not complete it.
- **Christians** generally support it because it aims at **reformation** and gives a chance to put things right — like Zacchaeus paying back those he had cheated (**Luke 19:8**). **Muslims** also support it for minor crimes, since it allows repentance and reparation, and helps the offender stay part of the community. **Humanists** support it because it tends to reduce reoffending better than short prison sentences.

### Forgiveness (contrasting-beliefs issue)

**Forgiveness** means showing grace and mercy and pardoning someone for what they have done wrong. It does **not** necessarily mean that there is no punishment: many believers forgive the person while still thinking justice must be done. **Restorative justice** brings offenders and victims together (when the victim wants it) so that the offender understands the harm done — this can lead to forgiveness and reform. **The Forgiveness Project**, a UK charity, shares stories of victims and offenders to show that forgiveness is possible.

**Christianity**
- Forgiveness is at the heart of the faith: God forgives sins through Jesus' death, so Christians must forgive others. The Lord's Prayer: "Forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors" (**Matthew 6:12**); if you do not forgive others, God will not forgive you (**Matthew 6:14–15**).
- Peter asked if he should forgive seven times; Jesus replied "seventy-seven times" (or "seventy times seven") — i.e. without limit (**Matthew 18:21–22**).
- On the cross Jesus prayed, "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing" (**Luke 23:34**).
- The woman caught in adultery: "Let any one of you who is without sin be the first to throw a stone at her" — Jesus did not condemn her but told her to leave her life of sin (**John 8:1–11**).
- Examples: **Pope John Paul II** visited and forgave **Mehmet Ali Ağca**, who had shot him in 1981. **Gee Walker**, whose son Anthony was murdered in a racist attack in Liverpool in 2005, publicly forgave his killers because of her Christian faith.
- **Differences**: most Christians believe forgiveness should be unconditional, but some think it requires **repentance** first (**Luke 17:3–4**). Catholics believe God's forgiveness is received in the sacrament of **reconciliation** (confession). Many Christians say forgiveness does not cancel punishment: the offender should still be punished, but with the aim of reform, not revenge.

**Islam**
- God is **Al-Ghafur** (the Forgiving) and **Ar-Rahman** (the Most Merciful); every surah but one begins by calling God "the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful".
- Muslims should forgive, as they hope to be forgiven: "let them pardon and overlook. Would you not like that Allah should forgive you?" (**Qur'an 24:22**). "The retribution for an evil act is an evil one like it, but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation — his reward is from Allah" (**42:40**).
- **Muhammad** forgave the people of Makkah, who had persecuted him, when he entered the city in 630 CE.
- But forgiveness does not remove **justice**: Shari'ah sets punishments, and forgiveness from God requires sincere **repentance (tawbah)**. In cases of murder or injury, the **victim's family** can choose to forgive, accept **diyah** (compensation) instead of **qisas** (equal retribution) — "whoever is pardoned by his brother… then there should be a suitable follow-up and payment" (**2:178**).
- Example: **Azim Khamisa**, a Muslim whose son Tariq was shot dead in the USA in 1995, forgave the teenage killer and set up the Tariq Khamisa Foundation with the killer's grandfather to stop youth violence.

| Christianity | Islam |
|---|---|
| Forgiveness should have **no limit** (Matthew 18:21–22) and many say it need not wait for repentance, following Jesus on the cross. | Forgiving is highly rewarded, but it is the **victim's (or family's) right** to choose between retribution, compensation and pardon (2:178; 42:40). |
| Most emphasise **reform** as the aim alongside forgiveness. | Forgiveness from God needs **tawbah**, and some crimes (hudud) are offences against God that a victim cannot simply pardon once they reach the court. |

**Non-religious views**: many humanists value forgiveness because it helps victims move on and gives offenders a chance to change, but believe it is a personal choice — no one should be pressured to forgive, and forgiveness does not replace the need for the law to protect people.

### The death penalty (contrasting-beliefs issue)

**Capital punishment** — the legal execution of a person for a crime. In Britain the death penalty for murder was suspended in **1965** and abolished permanently in **1969**; it was removed for all crimes in **1998**. The last executions in the UK were in **1964**. Cases that influenced abolition include **Timothy Evans**, hanged in 1950 for murders committed by John Christie and later pardoned, and **Derek Bentley**, hanged in 1953, whose conviction was quashed in 1998. It is still used in countries including **China, Iran, Saudi Arabia** and some **US states**.

**Christianity**
- **Most Christians today oppose** it. The **Catholic Church** teaches (Catechism 2267, revised by Pope Francis in 2018) that "the death penalty is inadmissible because it is an attack on the inviolability and dignity of the person". The **Church of England**, **Methodist Church** and **Quakers** also oppose it. Reasons: sanctity of life — only God should take life; everyone can be reformed and forgiven; Jesus rejected "eye for eye" (**Matthew 5:38–39**) and saved the woman who faced stoning (**John 8:1–11**); innocent people have been executed.
- **Some Christians support** it, particularly for murder: "Whoever sheds human blood, by humans shall their blood be shed; for in the image of God has God made mankind" (**Genesis 9:6**); the Old Testament commands death for murder (**Exodus 21:12**); the state "does not bear the sword for no reason" (**Romans 13:4**). **Thomas Aquinas** taught it could be used to protect the common good. Many of these supporters are in the USA.

**Islam**
- **Many Muslims support** the death penalty for certain crimes, because the Qur'an allows it: killing is forbidden "except by right" (**17:33**), and 5:32 makes an exception for killing in return for a life or for "corruption in the land". Under Shari'ah it may be used for **murder** and for serious crimes that threaten society; some interpretations also include other offences.
- It requires very strict proof and a just Islamic court, and the **victim's family may choose mercy** — forgiveness or compensation (**2:178**) — so the killer may be spared.
- **Some Muslims oppose** it in practice: they argue that fair trials cannot be guaranteed, that the conditions of an Islamic state are not met, or that the Qur'an's praise of forgiveness (42:40) should come first. British Muslims keep UK law, which does not allow it.

| Christianity | Islam |
|---|---|
| Most denominations (Catholic, Church of England, Methodist, Quaker) **oppose** it: life is sacred, everyone can be reformed, and Jesus taught mercy. | Many Muslims **accept** it for murder and "corruption in the land" because the Qur'an permits taking life "by right" (17:33; 5:32). |
| A minority **support** it, using Genesis 9:6 and Romans 13:4. | The victim's family can **forgive** and accept diyah instead (2:178), and some Muslims oppose its use today. |

**Humanists** (e.g. **Humanists UK**) oppose the death penalty: it is irreversible, innocent people have been executed, it is not a proven deterrent, and it denies any chance of reform. Some non-religious people support it for the worst crimes as justice for victims and protection for the public.

### Ethical arguments about the death penalty

**The principle of utility** (utilitarianism, Jeremy Bentham): the right action produces the greatest happiness (or least suffering) for the greatest number.
- **For**: if executing a murderer **deters** others and protects many potential victims, it brings the greatest good; it permanently removes a dangerous person; it may bring a sense of justice to victims' families.
- **Against**: there is little evidence it deters more than life imprisonment; executing an innocent person causes huge suffering; it brutalises society; a utilitarian can argue life imprisonment protects the public equally without these risks.

**The sanctity of life**: the belief that all life is holy because it is created by God and belongs to him — "The LORD gave and the LORD has taken away" (**Job 1:21**); humans are made in God's image (**Genesis 1:27**); in Islam, life is a trust from God (5:32).
- **Against the death penalty**: if life is sacred, the state has no right to end even a murderer's life; only God should decide when life ends; every person can still repent and be redeemed.
- **For the death penalty**: some believers use the same principle the other way — because the *victim's* life was sacred, the murderer has forfeited their own right to life (**Genesis 9:6** gives the image of God as the reason for execution; Qur'an 5:32 permits taking life for a life).
- Humanists do not accept a religious sanctity of life but speak of the **value** and **quality** of each human life — which many use to argue against execution.

::: terms
- **Punishment**: a penalty given by the state to someone who has committed a crime.
- **Retribution**: an aim of punishment — getting even, so the criminal pays for what they have done.
- **Deterrence**: an aim of punishment — putting the offender and others off committing the crime.
- **Reformation**: an aim of punishment — changing the criminal's behaviour for the better so they do not reoffend.
- **Corporal punishment**: punishing an offender by causing them physical pain, such as caning or flogging.
- **Community service**: a punishment of unpaid work in the local community, part of a community order.
- **Forgiveness**: showing grace and mercy and pardoning someone for what they have done wrong.
- **Death penalty**: capital punishment; the legal execution of a person as punishment for a crime.
- **Principle of utility**: the idea that the right action is the one that brings the greatest good for the greatest number.
- **Sanctity of life**: the belief that all life is holy because it is created by God and belongs to him.
- **Qisas and diyah**: in Shari'ah, equal retribution for murder or injury, and the compensation a victim's family may accept instead.
:::

Paper 2 (Thematic studies) has **one five-part question per theme**: 1 + 2 + 4 + 5 + 12 = 24 marks. SPaG (up to **3 marks**) is assessed on the 12-mark question of every theme, and the best of your SPaG marks counts.

Two genuinely **different** beliefs, each simple (1) or detailed (2).    - **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with reason(s) stated.

::: example 12-mark evaluation
"The death penalty is never right." Evaluate this statement. (12 + 3 SPaG)

**In support**: most Christians agree because of the sanctity of life. Humans are made in the image of God (Genesis 1:27) and only God should decide when a life ends; executing a murderer takes away any chance for them to repent and be reformed, which Christians believe is always possible because God forgives. Jesus rejected "eye for eye" (Matthew 5:38–39) and saved the woman who was about to be stoned (John 8:1–11). The Catholic Church now teaches that the death penalty is "inadmissible". There is also a strong non-religious case from the principle of utility: innocent people such as Timothy Evans have been executed, which causes enormous suffering and cannot be undone, and there is little evidence that it deters murderers more than life imprisonment.

**Against**: however, many Muslims believe the death penalty can be right because the Qur'an allows life to be taken "by right" (17:33) — for example for murder — and it is part of Shari'ah. Some Christians agree, pointing to Genesis 9:6, which says that because humans are made in God's image, whoever sheds human blood should have their own blood shed: the victim's sacred life demands justice. A utilitarian could argue that if it deters even a few murders, or permanently removes a serial killer, it produces the greatest good for the greatest number.

**Conclusion**: I agree with the statement. The strongest argument against it is that it is irreversible: no justice system is perfect, and an innocent person executed can never be compensated. Even in Islam, the Qur'an strongly encourages the victim's family to forgive (2:178), which suggests mercy is better than execution. Life imprisonment protects the public just as well while leaving room for repentance, so the death penalty is not needed.
:::
