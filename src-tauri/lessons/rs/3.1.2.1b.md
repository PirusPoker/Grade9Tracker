## What it is

Christians believe that death is not the end. Because God raised Jesus from the dead, Christians hope that they too will live again with God. The spec asks you to know **different Christian beliefs about the afterlife and why they matter**: resurrection and life after death, judgement, heaven and hell. The Nicene Creed sums up the shared belief: Jesus "will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead", and Christians "look for the resurrection of the dead, and the life of the world to come". The Apostles' Creed ends with "the resurrection of the body, and the life everlasting".

### Resurrection and life after death

- **Jesus' resurrection is the foundation.** St Paul argues that if Christ has not been raised, Christian faith is useless (1 Corinthians 15:14–17), but because he has been raised, he is the "firstfruits" of all who will rise (1 Corinthians 15:20). Jesus tells Martha, "I am the resurrection and the life" — whoever believes in him will live, even though they die (John 11:25).
- **Bodily resurrection.** Most Christians believe the whole person will be raised, body and soul, not just a ghost-like spirit. But it will not be the same perishable body: Paul says it is "sown perishable" and "raised imperishable", sown "a natural body" and raised "a spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:42–44). The risen Jesus could be touched and could eat (Luke 24:39–43) yet also appeared in locked rooms (John 20:19), which suggests a transformed body.
- **The soul.** Many Christians believe that at death the soul — the non-physical, eternal part of a person made in God's image — leaves the body and goes to be with God straight away. Jesus told the criminal crucified beside him, "Truly I tell you, today you will be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43).
- **Two stages for many Christians.** The soul lives on after death (and is judged), and then at the end of time, at the **Second Coming** (Parousia) of Christ, the dead are raised with new bodies (1 Thessalonians 4:16–17).
- **Different emphases.** Some Protestants think the dead "sleep" until the resurrection at the end of time, so the soul does not go to heaven straight away. Some liberal Christians see resurrection as spiritual rather than physical — a new life with God that words like "body" only point towards. Jesus warns that in the resurrection people "will neither marry nor be given in marriage" but will be like the angels (Matthew 22:30), so the next life is not simply a copy of this one.

### Judgement

Christians believe God is **just** (see the nature of God) and so everyone will be held accountable for how they lived.

- **Particular judgement**: Catholics teach that each soul is judged immediately at death.
- **Final (Last) Judgement**: at the end of time Christ returns to judge everyone — "the living and the dead" (Creeds).
- **The Parable of the Sheep and the Goats** (Matthew 25:31–46): the Son of Man separates people as a shepherd separates sheep from goats. Those who fed the hungry, gave drink to the thirsty, welcomed the stranger, clothed the naked and visited the sick and those in prison are welcomed into the kingdom, because whatever they did for "the least of these" they did for Jesus (25:40). Those who did not "will go away to eternal punishment, but the righteous to eternal life" (25:46).
- **Faith or works?** Protestants stress that people are saved by **faith** in Jesus and God's grace, not by earning it (Ephesians 2:8–9); good works are the evidence of real faith. Catholics and Orthodox teach that faith must be lived out in love and good works, and that God's grace works through the sacraments. All agree that judgement is carried out by a God who is both just and merciful.

### Heaven

- **A state or a place?** Some Christians picture heaven as a real place of paradise; Jesus says, "My Father's house has many rooms" (John 14:2) and that he goes to prepare a place. Many others think heaven is a **state of being** in the presence of God, beyond space and time, which the Bible describes in picture language.
- **What it is like.** Eternal peace and joy with God; God "will wipe every tear from their eyes. There will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain" (Revelation 21:4). Revelation 21 describes a "new heaven and a new earth". Catholics speak of the **beatific vision** — seeing God face to face.
- **Who goes?** Traditionally, those who have faith in Christ and are forgiven. Some Christians believe only Christians will be saved (Jesus: "No one comes to the Father except through me", John 14:6); others believe people of other faiths or none who have lived good lives may be saved through Christ without knowing it; **universalists** believe a loving God will in the end save everyone.

### Purgatory (Catholic belief)

The **Catholic Church** teaches that those who die in God's friendship but are not yet completely purified go through **purgatory**, a state of cleansing, before entering heaven (Catechism of the Catholic Church 1030–1031). It is not a second chance for the wicked; everyone in purgatory will reach heaven. Catholics pray for the dead and offer Masses for them, citing 2 Maccabees 12:46. **Protestants reject purgatory** because they say it is not in the Bible (they do not accept 2 Maccabees as scripture) and because Christ's death was sufficient to take away all sin. The **Orthodox** Church prays for the dead but does not accept the Catholic doctrine of purgatory as such.

### Hell

| View | What it says | Who holds it |
|---|---|---|
| **Eternal punishment** | Hell is a real place or state of everlasting suffering for those who reject God. Jesus uses images of fire and "weeping and gnashing of teeth" (Matthew 13:42); the rich man suffers in Hades (Luke 16:19–31). | Traditional view; many evangelical and conservative Christians. |
| **Separation from God** | Hell is not fire but the pain of being cut off from God, which people choose for themselves. The Catholic Catechism calls it a "state of definitive self-exclusion from communion with God" (CCC 1033). | Catholic teaching; many Anglicans and others. The fire is taken as a symbol. |
| **Annihilation** | The unrepentant are not tormented for ever; they simply cease to exist. A Church of England Doctrine Commission report (*The Mystery of Salvation*, 1995) described the final end of rejecting God as total non-being rather than endless torment. | Some Anglicans and evangelicals. |
| **Universalism** | A loving God would not let anyone suffer for ever; eventually all will be reconciled to God, so hell is empty or temporary. | A minority of liberal Christians. |

Many Christians ask how a loving God could send anyone to hell; the usual answer is that God respects **free will** — people who reject God are given what they chose.

### Why these beliefs matter (their importance)

- **Hope and comfort**: death is not the end, so Christians can face their own death with hope and are comforted when loved ones die. Funerals speak of "sure and certain hope of the resurrection to eternal life" (Anglican funeral service).
- **Meaning and purpose**: life on earth is a preparation for eternal life with God.
- **How to live**: judgement motivates Christians to follow Jesus' teaching, love their neighbour and help those in need (Sheep and the Goats) — for example by working with the poor or visiting prisoners.
- **Justice**: evil that goes unpunished in this life will be dealt with by God; the suffering will be put right.
- **Value of the body**: belief in bodily resurrection is one reason many Christians treat the body with respect; historically many preferred burial to cremation, although most Churches now accept cremation.
- **Mission**: if salvation matters eternally, many Christians feel they must share the gospel.
- **Prayer for the dead**: Catholics and Orthodox pray for those who have died; Protestants usually pray for comfort for the mourners instead.

::: terms
- **Resurrection**: rising from the dead; for Christians, God raising the whole person to new, transformed life, as he raised Jesus.
- **Afterlife**: life after death — for Christians, eternal life with God (or separated from him).
- **Soul**: the non-physical, eternal part of a person, made in God's image, which many Christians believe lives on after death.
- **Judgement**: God's assessment of each person's life and faith, at death and at the Last Judgement when Christ returns.
- **Parousia**: the Second Coming of Christ at the end of time, when the dead will be raised and judged.
- **Heaven**: the eternal state (or place) of joy and peace in the presence of God.
- **Hell**: the state (or place) of separation from God, understood as eternal punishment, self-exclusion or annihilation.
- **Purgatory**: in Catholic teaching, a state of cleansing after death for those who die in God's grace but are not yet fully purified.
- **Eternal life**: life with God that never ends, which Christians believe begins through faith in Jesus.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 (Christianity), Beliefs question: five parts worth 1, 2, 4, 5 and 12 marks.** Spend about 25 minutes on the whole question.

1. **1 mark — multiple choice**: four options, tick one. Watch for "not" and look-alike terms (purgatory vs Parousia; resurrection vs reincarnation).
2. **2 marks — "Give two…"**: two short, separate, correct points ("the soul lives on"; "God will judge everyone"). Only the first two answers are marked.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two ways…" / "Explain two contrasting beliefs…"**: two points, each **developed** — 1 mark simple + 1 mark detailed. Develop with an example, a teaching, or the effect on a believer. For **contrasting** questions the two beliefs must genuinely differ — e.g. hell as eternal punishment vs hell as separation from God; bodily vs spiritual resurrection; Catholic belief in purgatory vs Protestant rejection of it. For **influence** questions ("Explain two ways in which belief in judgement influences Christians today") the second mark must show what a Christian *does* because of the belief.
4. **5 marks — "Explain two… Refer to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching in your answer"**: two developed points (4 marks) **plus 1 mark for a relevant, accurate source** — a Bible passage, a creed, the Catechism. Quote briefly or paraphrase with the reference ("In the Parable of the Sheep and the Goats, Matthew 25, Jesus says…") and **link it to your point**.
5. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement."** Paper 1 bullets: *refer to Christian teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion.* (On the themes paper they read "refer to religious arguments; may refer to non-religious arguments".) The 12-mark in the Beliefs section of each religion carries **+3 SPaG** — spell resurrection, judgement, purgatory and Catholic correctly.

**How the 12-mark levels are reached** (the whole answer is placed in one level):
- **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with a reason stated.
- **Level 2 (4–6)**: one view argued with a logical chain of reasoning, **or** different views recognised, each with reasons. **Maximum Level 2 with no reference to religion.**
- **Level 3 (7–9)**: reasoned consideration of **different** points of view, with logical chains that draw on knowledge, and **clear reference to religion**.
- **Level 4 (10–12)**: a **well-argued** response — different views weighed with logical chains **leading to a justified judgement**, with religious teaching *applied* to the issue.

Structure: two arguments for (point → explain → evidence → evaluate), two arguments against, then a conclusion that says which side is stronger **and why** — for example because one side rests on scripture shared by all Christians while the other depends on one denomination's reading. Where the statement allows, show diversity (Catholic vs Protestant, traditional vs liberal) — that is "different points of view" too.
:::

::: example 5-mark — teachings about life after death
Explain two Christian teachings about life after death. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

Christians teach that the dead will be resurrected (1). This does not mean the same decaying body comes back: St Paul says the body is "sown perishable" and "raised imperishable", a "spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:42–44), so Christians believe they will be raised as whole people, body and soul, transformed like the risen Jesus (1 + 1 source). Christians also teach that after death everyone is judged by God (1). In the Parable of the Sheep and the Goats (Matthew 25:31–46) those who helped the hungry, strangers and prisoners go to eternal life, while those who ignored them go to eternal punishment, so Christians believe how they treat others now affects their eternal future (1).
:::

::: example 4-mark — contrasting beliefs about hell
Explain two contrasting Christian beliefs about hell. (4)

Some Christians, particularly more traditional evangelicals, believe hell is a real place of eternal punishment (1), because Jesus described it with images of fire and "weeping and gnashing of teeth", so those who reject God suffer for ever (1). In contrast, the Catholic Church and many other Christians believe hell is not literally fire but a state of separation from God (1): the Catechism calls it "self-exclusion" from communion with God, so people who freely reject God's love are given what they chose, and the pain is the loss of God himself (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark — heaven and hell
"Heaven and hell are not real places." Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should: refer to Christian teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion. (12 + 3 SPaG)

Many Christians would agree. God is spirit and beyond space and time, so being "with God" cannot be a location on a map; heaven is better understood as a **state** of perfect relationship with God. The Catholic Church describes hell as a "state of definitive self-exclusion from communion with God" (CCC 1033), so the fire Jesus speaks of is a symbol of the pain of losing God, not a furnace somewhere underground. Since the Bible uses picture language — a city of gold, many rooms — reading these literally misses their point, which is to describe joy and loss that humans cannot fully imagine.

On the other hand, many Christians believe heaven and hell are real in a fuller sense. Jesus said, "My Father's house has many rooms… I am going there to prepare a place for you" (John 14:2), and the Creeds teach "the resurrection of the body". If people are raised with bodies, even transformed ones, it seems they must exist *somewhere*, just as the risen Jesus ascended rather than simply vanishing. Traditional Christians also argue that Jesus warned seriously about hell (Matthew 25:46) and that calling it "not real" risks making the warning meaningless.

Non-religious people, such as humanists, would go further and say neither heaven nor hell exists, because there is no evidence of life after death; but this does not address what Christians mean by them.

In conclusion, the statement is partly true. Most Christians agree heaven and hell are **real** — they are not just metaphors for how we feel in this life — but many accept that they are not "places" in the ordinary physical sense. The stronger view is that they are real states of being with or without God which the Bible describes in images, because this fits both the Bible's picture language and the belief that God is beyond space.
:::

::: q
Give two Christian beliefs about heaven. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): it is eternal life in the presence of God; there is no more suffering, death or tears (Revelation 21:4); it may be a real place ("many rooms", John 14:2) or a state of being with God; Catholics speak of seeing God face to face (the beatific vision); it is reached through faith in Jesus / God's grace.
:::

::: q
What does the Catholic Church teach about purgatory, and why do Protestants disagree? (4)
---
Catholic: purgatory is a state of cleansing after death for those who die in God's friendship but are not yet completely pure (1); everyone in purgatory will eventually reach heaven, and the living can help by praying and offering Masses for the dead (1). Protestants disagree because they find no clear basis for it in the Bible — the passage Catholics cite, 2 Maccabees 12:46, is not in the Protestant Bible (1) — and because Jesus' death on the cross was enough to deal with all sin, so no further cleansing is needed (1).
:::

::: q
Explain the difference between the particular judgement and the Last Judgement. (2)
---
The particular judgement happens to each individual soul immediately at death (Catholic teaching) (1); the Last (Final) Judgement happens to everyone together at the end of time when Christ returns — "he will come again to judge the living and the dead" (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two ways in which belief in resurrection influences Christians today. (4)
---
Any two, each developed (1 + 1). It gives hope when facing death (1), so Christian funerals, though sad, speak of the "sure and certain hope of the resurrection" and mourners are comforted that they will see the dead again (1). It gives life meaning (1), so Christians try to live faithfully now as preparation for eternal life with God (1). It leads to respect for the body (1), for example care over funerals and, for some, a preference for burial (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what the Parable of the Sheep and the Goats teaches about judgement. (3)
---
Jesus (the Son of Man) will judge all people at the end of time, separating them as a shepherd separates sheep from goats (Matthew 25:31–33) (1). People are judged on whether they helped those in need — the hungry, thirsty, strangers, the naked, the sick and prisoners — because helping them is helping Jesus himself (25:40) (1). The righteous go to eternal life and the others to eternal punishment (25:46), so how Christians treat others has eternal consequences (1).
:::

::: q
Explain why some Christians believe in universalism and why others reject it. (4)
---
For: God is all-loving and wants everyone to be saved, so eternal suffering seems incompatible with a loving and merciful God (1); a loving parent would never give up on a child, so God will in the end reconcile everyone (1). Against: Jesus clearly warns of eternal punishment (Matthew 25:46) and says "No one comes to the Father except through me" (John 14:6) (1); God gave humans free will, and if some freely reject God, forcing them into heaven would not respect that choice (1).
:::

::: q
"Belief in judgement is the most important Christian belief about the afterlife." Give one argument for and one argument against. (4)
---
For: without judgement there would be no justice — the wicked who escaped punishment in life would never be held to account, and God is described as just (1); it also motivates Christians to live well, as in the Sheep and the Goats (1). Against: resurrection is more important, because without Jesus' resurrection there is no Christian hope at all — Paul says faith would be useless (1 Corinthians 15:14) (1); and many Christians stress God's grace and forgiveness over judgement, so what matters most is the promise of eternal life (John 3:16) (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Contrasting** questions need two genuinely **different** beliefs — "heaven is peaceful" and "heaven is joyful" are the same belief. Use eternal punishment vs separation, bodily vs spiritual resurrection, or Catholic vs Protestant on purgatory.
- Do not confuse **resurrection** (raised to new life) with **reincarnation** (reborn in another body, which Christians reject) or **purgatory** with **Parousia**.
- Purgatory is **Catholic** teaching — do not say "all Christians believe in purgatory".
- On 5-mark questions the source must be **linked** to the point it supports, not just dropped at the end.
- **Importance** questions need the effect on a believer's life — hope, comfort, motivation to help others — not just a description of heaven.
- 12-mark answers without Christian teaching are capped at Level 2; show diversity within Christianity to reach "different points of view".
:::
