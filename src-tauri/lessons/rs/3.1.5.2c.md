## What it is

**Hajj** is the pilgrimage to **Makkah** in Saudi Arabia. It is the **fifth of the Five Pillars** of Sunni Islam and one of the **Ten Obligatory Acts** (Furu ad-Din) of Shi'a Islam, so Sunni and Shi'a Muslims share it and perform it together. Every adult Muslim who is **physically and financially able** must perform it **once in their lifetime** (Qur'an 3:97 says pilgrimage to the House is a duty owed to God by all who are able to make their way there). It takes place between the **8th and 12th (or 13th) of Dhul-Hijjah**, the last month of the Islamic lunar year. In recent years up to around two million pilgrims have attended each Hajj.

The spec asks for: the **role and significance** of Hajj, its **origins**, **how it is performed**, and the actions at the **Ka'aba in Makkah, Mina, Arafat and Muzdalifah** and their significance.

### Origins

| Event | What happened | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Ibrahim and Hajar** | God told Ibrahim (Abraham) to leave his wife **Hajar** and baby son **Isma'il** in the barren valley of Makkah. When their water ran out, Hajar ran seven times between the hills of **Safa and Marwah** looking for water. God caused the spring of **Zamzam** to gush out at the baby's feet. | Qur'an 14:37 (Ibrahim's prayer for the family he settled in an uncultivated valley); hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari |
| **Building the Ka'aba** | Ibrahim and Isma'il raised the foundations of the **Ka'aba**, the House of God, and prayed for it to be accepted. Muslims believe it was the first house of worship built for humankind (Qur'an 3:96); many believe Adam first built it. | Qur'an 2:127; 3:96 |
| **The call to pilgrimage** | God told Ibrahim to proclaim the pilgrimage to all people, so that they would come on foot and on every kind of camel from distant places. | Qur'an 22:27 |
| **Ibrahim's test** | Ibrahim was willing to sacrifice his son at God's command; Shaytan tried to tempt him to disobey, and Ibrahim drove him away by throwing stones. God provided a ram instead. | Qur'an 37:102-107; tradition about the stoning |
| **Muhammad restores Hajj** | Over the centuries the Ka'aba had been filled with idols. When Muhammad returned to Makkah in **630 CE** he removed the idols and rededicated the Ka'aba to the one God. In **632 CE** he led the **Farewell Pilgrimage**, the model every Muslim now follows, and preached his Farewell Sermon at Arafat. | hadith and sirah (biography of the Prophet) |

So Hajj **re-enacts** the faith and obedience of Ibrahim, Hajar and Isma'il, and follows exactly the example (sunnah) of the Prophet.

### Before Hajj: ihram

Before reaching the boundary points around Makkah (the **miqat**), pilgrims enter **ihram**, a state of holiness and purity:
- they perform a full wash (ghusl) and state their intention (**niyyah**);
- **men** wear two plain, unsewn white sheets; **women** wear plain, modest clothing, usually white, with the face and hands uncovered;
- they must not use perfume, cut hair or nails, have sexual relations, hunt, argue or fight (Qur'an 2:197 forbids sexual relations, disobedience and disputing during Hajj);
- they repeatedly recite the **Talbiyah**: "Labbayk Allahumma labbayk" — "Here I am, O God, here I am."

**Significance**: everyone looks the same, so rich and poor, king and worker, stand **equal before God** — it also reminds pilgrims of the white shroud in which they will be buried and of standing before God on the Day of Judgement.

### How Hajj is performed

| Day (Dhul-Hijjah) | Place | Action | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrival (often the 7th or 8th) | **Ka'aba, Makkah** | **Tawaf**: walking seven times anticlockwise around the Ka'aba, starting at the **Black Stone** (al-Hajar al-Aswad), which pilgrims try to touch or kiss, or point to if the crowd is too great | God is the centre of a Muslim's life; the ummah moves as one in worship. The Prophet kissed the Black Stone, so pilgrims follow his example. |
| Arrival | **Safa and Marwah** (now inside the Grand Mosque) | **Sa'y**: walking or running seven times between the two hills; drinking **Zamzam** water | Remembers Hajar's trust in God and her perseverance; Qur'an 2:158 calls Safa and Marwah among the symbols of God. God provides for those who trust him. |
| 8th | **Mina** | Pilgrims travel to Mina, a huge tent city a few miles from Makkah, and spend the day and night in prayer and reading the Qur'an | Preparation for the most important day; simple living, focus on God. |
| 9th | **Arafat** | **Wuquf** ("standing"): from midday to sunset pilgrims stand on the plain of Arafat, near the **Mount of Mercy** (Jabal al-Rahmah), praying, reading the Qur'an and asking forgiveness | The **most important part** of Hajj: the Prophet taught "Hajj is Arafat" (hadith, Tirmidhi) — without it Hajj is invalid. It is where Muhammad gave his Farewell Sermon, and tradition says Adam and Hawwa (Eve) were reunited and forgiven there. It is a rehearsal for standing before God on the Day of Judgement. |
| Night of 9th–10th | **Muzdalifah** | Pilgrims pray the evening prayers together, sleep under the open sky, and collect small pebbles for the stoning | Humility and equality — everyone sleeps on the ground; prepares them to reject evil. |
| 10th | **Mina** | **Ramy al-jamarat**: throwing seven pebbles at the largest pillar (Jamarat al-Aqabah); then the **sacrifice** of an animal (the start of **Id-ul-Adha**); men shave or cut their hair, women cut a lock | Stoning remembers Ibrahim rejecting Shaytan and is a pilgrim's own rejection of temptation and evil. The sacrifice remembers Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice his son; the meat is shared with the poor. Cutting hair shows a new start, and pilgrims leave most ihram restrictions. |
| 10th (or after) | **Ka'aba, Makkah** | **Tawaf al-Ifadah** and sa'y | Completes the essential rites of Hajj. |
| 11th–12th (or 13th) | **Mina** | Pilgrims stay in Mina and stone all **three** pillars each day | Repeated rejection of evil and of Shaytan's temptations. |
| Departure | **Ka'aba, Makkah** | **Farewell tawaf** (Tawaf al-Wada) | A last act of worship before returning home. |

Many pilgrims also visit **Madinah** to pray at the Prophet's Mosque, but this is not part of Hajj. A shorter pilgrimage to Makkah, **Umrah**, can be made at any time of year (it includes tawaf and sa'y), but it does not replace Hajj.

**Sunni and Shi'a**: both perform the same rites at the same time. Shi'a pilgrims may pray with their arms by their sides and prostrate on a small tablet of earth, as they do in salah at home. Shi'a Muslims also make **ziyarah** (visits) to shrines such as **Karbala** and **Najaf** in Iraq — important, but never a substitute for Hajj.

### Role and significance of Hajj

- **Obedience to God**: it fulfils a pillar/obligatory act commanded in the Qur'an (3:97; 22:27).
- **Forgiveness and a fresh start**: the Prophet taught that whoever performs Hajj without obscenity or wrongdoing returns as free of sin as the day their mother gave birth to them (hadith, Bukhari).
- **Unity of the ummah**: Muslims of every nationality, colour, language and school of thought worship together in one place.
- **Equality**: ihram clothing removes signs of wealth and status. In his Farewell Sermon Muhammad taught that no Arab is superior to a non-Arab and no white person to a black person, except by piety and good action.
- **Following the prophets**: pilgrims walk where Ibrahim, Hajar, Isma'il and Muhammad walked.
- **Preparation for the Akhirah**: the white cloth, the crowds on the plain of Arafat and the plea for forgiveness all echo the Day of Judgement.
- **Lasting change**: many return with the title **Hajji** (man) or **Hajjah** (woman) and a new commitment to prayer, charity and patience.

### Different views and challenges today

- **Who must go**: only those who are **able** — healthy, adult, sane, and able to afford it without leaving their family in debt or hardship. Someone too ill may send a proxy to perform it for them (hajj badal).
- **Cost and access**: from Britain a Hajj package can cost several thousand pounds. Saudi Arabia sets quotas for each country, so many British Muslims wait years or travel through licensed operators; fraudulent operators are a known problem.
- **Safety**: huge crowds and extreme heat bring danger — a crush at Mina in 2015 killed many hundreds of pilgrims, and many pilgrims died in extreme heat in 2024. Saudi authorities have since expanded the Jamarat bridge, cooling and crowd control.
- **Is it the best use of money?** Some Muslims argue that giving the money to the poor is also pleasing to God; most answer that Hajj is an obligation for those who can afford it, and charity is a separate duty (zakah). A person who cannot go is not blamed — God judges intentions (niyyah).
- **Inner and outer**: many Muslims stress that the physical journey means little without the inner change it is meant to bring — Qur'an 22:37 teaches that what reaches God is people's piety (taqwa), not the meat or blood of sacrifices.

::: terms
- **Hajj**: the pilgrimage to Makkah in the month of Dhul-Hijjah, a duty once in a lifetime for every Muslim who is able.
- **Ka'aba**: the cube-shaped House of God in the Grand Mosque at Makkah, built by Ibrahim and Isma'il, which all Muslims face in prayer.
- **Ihram**: the state of purity pilgrims enter before Hajj, including simple white clothing and restrictions on behaviour.
- **Tawaf**: walking seven times anticlockwise around the Ka'aba as an act of worship.
- **Sa'y**: walking or running seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwah, remembering Hajar's search for water.
- **Zamzam**: the spring God provided for Hajar and Isma'il in Makkah, whose water pilgrims drink.
- **Wuquf**: the "standing" on the plain of Arafat from midday to sunset on 9 Dhul-Hijjah, the most important rite of Hajj.
- **Muzdalifah**: the open ground between Arafat and Mina where pilgrims pray, sleep outdoors and gather pebbles.
- **Ramy al-jamarat**: the stoning of the pillars at Mina, which represents rejecting Shaytan and temptation.
- **Umrah**: the lesser pilgrimage to Makkah, which can be made at any time of year and does not replace Hajj.
:::

::: method
Paper 1 (Islam) has two five-part questions: **Q1 on beliefs** and **Q2 on practices** (this topic). Each is 1 + 2 + 4 + 5 + 12 = 24 marks. On this paper SPaG (3 marks) is assessed on the 12-mark question in the **beliefs** section, so the practices 12-mark carries no SPaG — but write accurately anyway, because clarity helps your level.

1. **1 mark — multiple choice.** Four options A–D; choose one. Distractors are often other places or festivals (Mina vs Muzdalifah; Hajj vs Ashura).
2. **2 marks — "Give two…"** (e.g. "Give two actions pilgrims perform at Makkah"). Two short, accurate, separate points. Only your first two are marked.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two ways…" / "Explain two reasons…" / "Explain two contrasting…"** Two points, each **developed**: 1 mark for a simple point, 2 for a detailed one. Develop by saying **what the action means** or **why it matters** — e.g. "Pilgrims stand at Arafat (1) because it is where Muhammad gave his last sermon and it prepares them for the Day of Judgement, so they spend the day asking forgiveness (2)." For "contrasting" questions, make the two points genuinely different (e.g. Hajj vs Umrah; Sunni and Shi'a practice; performing Hajj in person vs by proxy).
4. **5 marks — "Explain two… Refer to sacred writings or another source of Muslim belief and teaching in your answer."** Two developed points (2 + 2) **plus 1 mark** for an accurate source: Qur'an 3:97 (duty for those able), 22:27 (call to pilgrimage), 2:125-127 (the Ka'aba), 2:158 (Safa and Marwah), 2:197 (conduct in ihram), 22:37 (piety reaches God), or the hadith "Hajj is Arafat" or the hadith on returning free of sin.
5. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement."** Bullets: refer to Muslim teaching; give reasoned arguments to support the statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion. Levels:
   - **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with a reason stated.
   - **Level 2 (4–6)**: one view explained with a logical chain of reasoning, OR different views each supported by reasons. **Maximum Level 2 if there is no reference to religion.**
   - **Level 3 (7–9)**: different points of view, each developed with logical chains of reasoning, with clear reference to Muslim teaching.
   - **Level 4 (10–12)**: a well-argued response — both sides developed and **evaluated** (say why one argument is stronger), accurate teaching and sources, and a conclusion that follows from your argument rather than just repeating one side.
   Structure: argument for (teaching + "because… this means…" chain), argument against (same), weigh them, then conclude.
:::

::: example 4-mark significance
Explain two reasons why the Day of Arafat is important for pilgrims on Hajj. (4)

First, it is the most important rite of Hajj (1). The Prophet taught "Hajj is Arafat", so a pilgrim who misses the standing from midday to sunset on 9 Dhul-Hijjah has not completed Hajj at all (1).

Second, it is a time of forgiveness and preparation for judgement (1). Pilgrims stand together on the plain in simple white clothes asking God to forgive their sins, which reminds them of standing before God on the Day of Judgement, and it is where Muhammad gave his Farewell Sermon (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark with source
Explain two actions pilgrims perform at Makkah during Hajj. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Muslim belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

First, pilgrims perform tawaf, walking seven times anticlockwise around the Ka'aba (1). The Ka'aba was built by Ibrahim and Isma'il as the first house of worship to the one God (Qur'an 2:127), so circling it shows that God must be at the centre of a Muslim's life, and the whole ummah moving together expresses unity (1).

Second, pilgrims perform sa'y, walking seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwah (1). This re-enacts Hajar's desperate search for water for Isma'il, which ended when God provided the Zamzam spring, so pilgrims learn to trust God and persevere; they also drink Zamzam water (1). The Qur'an says that Safa and Marwah are among the symbols of God (2:158) (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark evaluation
"Hajj is the most important of the Five Pillars." Evaluate this statement. (12)

Some Muslims would agree. Hajj brings together all the other pillars: pilgrims declare their faith in the Talbiyah, pray at the Ka'aba which they face in every salah, fast if they cannot offer a sacrifice, and give meat to the poor. Because the Prophet taught that a pilgrim whose Hajj is accepted returns as free of sin as a newborn, Hajj offers a complete fresh start that no other single act offers. It also shows the unity and equality of the ummah more powerfully than anything else, as two million people from every nation stand dressed alike on the plain of Arafat.

However, others would argue the Shahadah is the most important pillar, because without sincerely believing that there is no god but God and Muhammad is his messenger, none of the other pillars have any value — it is the gateway into Islam. Many would also argue that salah is more important in practice, because it is performed five times every day, whereas Hajj is required only once and only of those who are able (Qur'an 3:97). A poor or ill Muslim who never goes on Hajj is not a lesser Muslim, since God judges intentions. Salah is also the first thing a person will be asked about on the Day of Judgement, according to hadith.

Overall, the argument against the statement is stronger. Hajj is a remarkable experience and the high point of many Muslims' lives, but because it is conditional on health and wealth it cannot be the foundation of Islam. The Shahadah is the foundation and salah the daily practice that keeps a Muslim close to God; Hajj is the summit that builds on them. Most Muslims would say the pillars work together as a whole, like the pillars of a building, rather than one being most important.
:::

---
After sunset on 9 Dhul-Hijjah pilgrims travel from Arafat to Muzdalifah, pray together and sleep under the open sky (1). They collect pebbles for the stoning at Mina; sleeping outdoors shows humility and equality before God (1).

::: q
What does Qur'an 22:37 teach about the sacrifice at the end of Hajj, and why does it matter? (2)
---
It teaches that the meat and blood of the sacrifice do not reach God — what reaches him is the pilgrim's piety (taqwa) (1). So Hajj is about inner devotion and obedience, not simply performing outward rituals; the meat is shared with the poor (1).
