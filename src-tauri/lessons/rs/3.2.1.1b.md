## What it is

This is the second half of **Theme A: Relationships and families**. The spec asks for religious teachings and arguments about: the **nature of families** (the role of parents and children; extended and nuclear families); the **purpose of families** (procreation, stability and the protection of children, educating children in a faith); **contemporary family issues** (same-sex parents, polygamy); the **roles of men and women**; **gender equality**; and **gender prejudice and discrimination, including examples**. You need Christian and Muslim views (with their diversity) and non-religious views, set in **contemporary British society**, where the main religious tradition is Christian but beliefs are diverse.

### The nature of families

| Type | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Nuclear family** | Two parents and their children living together. Traditionally seen by many Christians as the ideal. |
| **Extended family** | Parents, children and other relatives — grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins — living together or close by and supporting each other. Common in many Muslim and South Asian families in Britain. |
| **Single-parent family** | One parent raising children, after divorce, separation, death or by choice. |
| **Reconstituted (blended) family** | A step-family formed when divorced or widowed parents remarry or live with a new partner and bring their children together. |
| **Same-sex parent family** | Two parents of the same sex raising children (by adoption, fostering, surrogacy, IVF or from a previous relationship). |
| **Multi-generational family** | Children, parents and grandparents under one roof; increasingly common because of housing costs and the cost of care. |

**Christianity** sees the family as the basic unit of society, created by God (Genesis 2:24) — the Catholic Church calls it the "domestic church", where faith is first lived and learned. **Islam** sees the family as the foundation of the ummah; family ties are sacred, and the Qur'an commands good treatment of parents and relatives (4:36). Muslims often keep strong **extended family** links: grandparents may help raise children, and adult children look after elderly parents at home rather than in a care home. **Humanists** value families of every kind as the place where people learn love, support and responsibility; what matters is the quality of relationships, not the structure.

### The role of parents and children

**Parents** — to love, feed, clothe and protect their children; to teach right from wrong; to give them an education; and, for religious parents, to bring them up in the faith.
- **Christianity**: "Train up a child in the way he should go; even when he is old he will not depart from it" (Proverbs 22:6). "Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" (Ephesians 6:4). The Catechism teaches that parents are the first educators of their children in the faith.
- **Islam**: children are a gift and a trust (amanah) from God. Parents should give them a good name, love, education and religious teaching, and treat sons and daughters fairly. The adhan is whispered into a newborn's ear so that the first word they hear is about God; many families hold an **aqiqah** celebration (sacrifice and naming) about seven days after birth.

**Children** — to respect, obey and later care for their parents.
- **Christianity**: "Honour your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12 — one of the Ten Commandments); "Children, obey your parents in everything, for this pleases the Lord" (Colossians 3:20). This includes caring for them in old age.
- **Islam**: the Qur'an links kindness to parents with worship of God: be good to them, and if one or both reach old age, do not even say "uff" (a word of impatience) to them, but speak to them kindly and pray for God's mercy on them (17:23-24). The mother carried the child "in weakness upon weakness" (31:14). A hadith teaches that paradise lies at the feet of mothers; another says that when asked who most deserves good treatment, the Prophet answered "your mother" three times, then "your father" (Bukhari and Muslim).
- **Non-religious**: humanists stress mutual respect: parents should help children think for themselves; children should care for parents out of love and gratitude, not duty to God.

### The purpose of families

| Purpose | Christianity | Islam | Non-religious |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Procreation** (having children) | God commanded "Be fruitful and multiply" (Genesis 1:28); children are a blessing (Psalm 127:3). For Catholics every marriage should be open to children. | God made spouses for people and from them children and grandchildren (16:72); having children continues the ummah. | Many choose to have children as a source of joy and to continue the family, but it is a personal choice, not a duty. |
| **Stability and protection of children** | A committed, loving family gives children security, food, shelter and love, and protects them from harm; Jesus welcomed children (Mark 10:14). | Parents are responsible for children's physical, moral and spiritual wellbeing; extended families give extra support and protection. | Families provide the safe, stable environment children need to grow up happy and healthy; research links stability with wellbeing. |
| **Educating children in a faith** | Infant **baptism**, godparents' promises, Sunday school, church schools, family prayer, **confirmation**. | Learning prayer and Arabic, attending **madrasah** (mosque school) to learn to read the Qur'an, fasting and celebrating Id as a family. | Humanists believe children should learn about many beliefs and make up their own minds, rather than being brought up in one faith. |

### Contemporary family issues

**Same-sex parents**. Same-sex couples in England and Wales have been able to adopt jointly since the Adoption and Children Act 2002 came into force (2005).
- **Catholic Church** and many traditional Christians: a child has a right to a mother and a father, reflecting God's design of male and female (Genesis 1:27); marriage between a man and a woman is the proper setting for raising children. But they stress that all children must be loved.
- **Liberal Christians** (e.g. many Anglicans, Methodists, Quakers): what children need is **love, stability and care**, which same-sex parents can give as well as anyone; "love your neighbour".
- **Islam**: most Muslims do not support same-sex parenting, because Islam teaches that children should be raised by a married mother and father, and homosexual relationships are not accepted. They accept it is legal in Britain.
- **Humanists**: fully support same-sex parents; research shows children of same-sex couples do just as well as others, and many children in care need loving homes.

**Polygamy** — being married to more than one person at the same time (in practice, **polygyny**, one man with several wives). It is **illegal** in the UK: marrying while already married is the crime of bigamy.
- **Christianity**: marriage is between **one man and one woman** — "the two shall become one flesh" (Mark 10:8); the Church opposes polygamy. Some Old Testament figures such as Abraham, Jacob and Solomon had more than one wife, but Christians see monogamy as God's intention from creation, and the New Testament asks church leaders to be "the husband of one wife" (1 Timothy 3:2).
- **Islam**: the Qur'an **permits** a man up to **four** wives, but only if he can treat them all **justly** — "if you fear that you will not be just, then [marry only] one" (4:3). It also says men will never be able to be completely fair between wives (4:129), so many Muslims believe **monogamy is the ideal** and polygamy is only for special circumstances (e.g. after war, when many women were widowed — it was revealed after the Battle of Uhud). A wife may not have more than one husband. **British Muslims must obey the law of the land**, so they do not practise legal polygamy; some Muslim-majority countries, such as Tunisia and Turkey, ban it.
- **Humanists**: oppose polygamy as usually unequal and harmful to women and children.

### The roles of men and women

| View | Belief | Teaching |
|---|---|---|
| **Christian — traditional / complementarian** | Men and women are **equal in value but have different roles**: the husband leads and provides, the wife cares for the home and children. | "Wives, submit to your own husbands, as to the Lord" (Ephesians 5:22); but husbands must love their wives as Christ loved the Church (5:25). |
| **Christian — egalitarian** (most British Christians today) | Men and women are **equal** and can share any role at home, at work and in the Church; old roles reflect the culture of the time. | Both made in God's image (Genesis 1:27); "there is neither Jew nor Greek… there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28); Jesus treated women with respect and Mary Magdalene was the first witness to the resurrection (John 20). |
| **Islam — traditional** | Men and women are **spiritually equal** but have **complementary** roles: the man is responsible for providing for and protecting the family; the woman has a special role as mother and home-maker, which is highly honoured. | "Men are in charge of women by [right of] what Allah has given one over the other and what they spend [for maintenance] from their wealth" (4:34) — read by many as a duty of care and financial responsibility, not superiority. |
| **Islam — modern** | Many Muslim women work, run businesses and lead organisations; many couples share roles. They point to the Prophet's example: he helped with housework, and his first wife **Khadijah** was a successful businesswoman who employed him. | Equal reward for good deeds (3:195; 33:35). |
| **Humanist / atheist** | Roles should be based on **individual choice and ability**, not gender; both partners share work, childcare and decisions. | Reason, equality and human rights |

### Gender equality

**Gender equality** means men and women having the same rights, status and opportunities. In Britain it is protected by law: the **Equal Pay Act 1970**, the **Sex Discrimination Act 1975**, and the **Equality Act 2010**, which makes sex one of nine protected characteristics.

**Christianity**
- All people are created **equal in God's image** (Genesis 1:27); Galatians 3:28.
- Women led and served in the early Church, e.g. **Phoebe** (described as a deacon or servant of the church, Romans 16:1) and **Priscilla**.
- **Women in leadership** divides churches:
  - **Catholic** and **Orthodox**: only men can be ordained priests, because Jesus chose twelve male apostles and the priest represents Christ at Mass; Pope John Paul II declared this could not be changed (*Ordinatio Sacerdotalis*, 1994). They stress that this is not about women being less valuable — Mary, the mother of Jesus, is honoured above all saints — and women serve in many other roles.
  - **Church of England**: the first women priests were ordained in **1994** and the first woman bishop, **Libby Lane**, was consecrated in **2015**. In 2026 **Sarah Mullally** was installed as the first woman **Archbishop of Canterbury**.
  - **Methodists**, the **Salvation Army** (co-founded by **Catherine Booth**) and **Quakers** have long treated men and women equally in ministry.
  - Some evangelical churches teach that only men should lead congregations, citing 1 Timothy 2:12.

**Islam**
- Men and women are **equal before God** and will be judged by the same standard — the Qur'an addresses believing men and believing women equally (33:35) and promises that the good work of no one, male or female, will be lost (3:195). All humans come from one pair and the most honoured is the most righteous (49:13).
- Islam gave women rights to **own property**, to **inherit** (4:7), to **consent to marriage**, to **seek divorce**, to keep their own **mahr** and earnings, and to be **educated** — the Prophet's wife **Aisha** was a leading scholar.
- Some differences remain: a son inherits twice a daughter's share (4:11) — explained by many as fair because men must financially support the family while a woman's wealth is her own; men and women usually pray in **separate** areas of the mosque (to avoid distraction during prostration); **imams** leading mixed congregations are men, though women can lead women and many are scholars and teachers.
- Many Muslims argue that injustices faced by some Muslim women (e.g. forced marriage, denial of education) come from **culture, not Islam**. Groups such as the **Muslim Women's Network UK** campaign for Muslim women's rights.

**Humanists** support complete equality and campaign against discrimination in religion, law and work; they argue that traditional roles based on scripture should not limit people's choices.

### Gender prejudice and discrimination, with examples

- **Prejudice** is an unfair opinion or attitude formed without good reason (e.g. "women are too emotional to lead"). **Discrimination** is **acting** on prejudice — treating someone unfairly because of their sex (e.g. not promoting a woman because she might have children).
- **Sexism** is prejudice or discrimination based on sex or gender.

**Examples**:
- The **gender pay gap**: on average women in the UK still earn less per hour than men, partly because of fewer women in senior, highly paid roles (the "glass ceiling").
- Women being passed over for promotion or losing work because of **pregnancy** or childcare.
- **Sexual harassment** at work or in public (highlighted by the #MeToo movement).
- **Stereotyping** jobs and subjects — e.g. assuming engineering is for boys or nursing for girls; men who stay at home to raise children being mocked.
- Around the world: girls denied education (e.g. under the Taliban in Afghanistan), forced and child marriage, and **female genital mutilation (FGM)**, which is illegal in the UK and condemned by Christian and Muslim leaders alike.
- Within religion: some argue that refusing to ordain women is discrimination; the churches concerned reply that it reflects different roles, not unequal worth.

**Religious responses**: Christians and Muslims teach that sexism is wrong because all are made by God and equal in his sight. The Anglican **Mothers' Union** campaigns worldwide against gender-based violence; **Christian Aid** and **Islamic Relief** run projects to educate girls and support women's livelihoods in developing countries.

::: terms
- **Nuclear family**: a family of two parents and their children living together.
- **Extended family**: a family that includes grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins as well as parents and children.
- **Procreation**: having children — one of the main purposes of marriage and the family in religious teaching.
- **Polygamy**: being married to more than one person at the same time; illegal in the UK.
- **Same-sex parents**: two people of the same sex who are raising children together.
- **Gender equality**: men and women having the same rights, status and opportunities.
- **Gender prejudice**: an unfair opinion about someone formed only because of their sex or gender.
- **Gender discrimination**: acting on prejudice by treating someone unfairly because of their sex or gender.
- **Complementarian**: the view that men and women are equal in value but have different, complementary roles.
- **Madrasah**: a mosque school where Muslim children learn to read the Qur'an and about their faith.
:::

::: method
Theme A is one five-part question on Paper 2: 1 + 2 + 4 + 5 + 12 = 24 marks, with **SPaG (3 marks)** on the 12-mark question. About 25 minutes per theme.

1. **1 mark — multiple choice.** Choose one of A–D (e.g. "Which one of the following means having more than one wife or husband at the same time?").
2. **2 marks — "Give two…"** (e.g. "Give two purposes of the family"). Two brief separate points; only the first two count.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two contrasting / two similar religious beliefs…" or "Explain two ways…"** Two points, 1 simple + 1 detailed each. The June 2023 paper asked "Explain two contrasting religious beliefs about gender equality. In your answer you must refer to one or more religious traditions." Contrasts can be **within** a religion (Catholic vs Church of England on women priests; traditional vs modern Muslim roles) or **between** religions; a non-religious view can be one side only if the question does not say otherwise, but at least one religion must be used. Two beliefs that say the same thing earn only one point.
4. **5 marks — "Explain two religious beliefs about… Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching in your answer."** June 2023: "Explain two religious beliefs about the role of children within the family." Two developed beliefs (2 + 2) plus 1 for an accurate source: Exodus 20:12, Colossians 3:20, Ephesians 6:4, Proverbs 22:6, Genesis 1:27-28, Galatians 3:28, Qur'an 17:23-24, 31:14, 4:3, 4:34, 49:13, a hadith, or the Catechism.
5. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement."** You should give reasoned arguments in support; reasoned arguments for a different view; refer to religious arguments; you may refer to non-religious arguments; reach a justified conclusion. Levels:
   - **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with reason(s).
   - **Level 2 (4–6)**: one view with a logical chain, OR different views with reasons. **Maximum Level 2 with no reference to religion.**
   - **Level 3 (7–9)**: different views, each with logical chains, clear reference to religion.
   - **Level 4 (10–12)**: well-argued, views developed **and evaluated**, evidence used, a judgement that follows from the reasoning.
   Plan: for (religious teaching + source + consequence), against (another denomination/religion/humanist), weigh, conclude. Check spelling of key terms for SPaG.
:::

::: example 4-mark contrasting (gender equality)
Explain two contrasting religious beliefs about gender equality. In your answer you must refer to one or more religious traditions. (4)

The Church of England believes men and women are equal in every role, including leadership in the Church (1). Since all people are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27) and "there is no male and female… in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28), it has ordained women priests since 1994 and women bishops since 2015 (1).

In contrast, the Catholic Church believes men and women are equal in worth but that only men can be ordained priests (1). This is because Jesus chose twelve male apostles and the priest represents Christ at Mass, so women serve the Church in other ways, following the example of Mary (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark with source (role of children)
Explain two religious beliefs about the role of children within the family. Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

Christians believe children should respect and obey their parents (1). The Ten Commandments say "Honour your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12), so children should listen to their parents' guidance and, when they are adults, continue to show respect by caring for them in old age (1) (1 for source).

Muslims believe children have a duty to be kind to and care for their parents, especially their mother (1). The Qur'an teaches that when parents reach old age children must not even say "uff" to them but speak to them with honour (17:23-24), so many Muslim families care for elderly parents at home rather than in a care home (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark evaluation
"The main purpose of the family is to bring up children in a faith." Evaluate this statement. (12 + 3 SPaG)

Many religious believers would agree. Christians are told to "train up a child in the way he should go" (Proverbs 22:6), and at infant baptism parents and godparents promise to bring the child up as a Christian; the Catholic Church calls the family the "domestic church" because it is where faith is first learned. Muslims whisper the adhan into a newborn baby's ear so that the first words the child hears are about God, and send children to madrasah to learn the Qur'an. For believers, eternal life matters more than anything, so passing on faith is the greatest gift a family can give.

However, others would say the main purpose is procreation or the protection of children. God's first command was to "be fruitful and multiply" (Genesis 1:28), and without children there is no one to educate at all. Even religious parents would put their child's safety, food and love first — a child cannot learn about God if they are neglected. Humanists argue that the purpose of a family is to give children stability and love, and that children should learn about different beliefs and decide for themselves, rather than being raised in one faith; many British families today are not religious and still clearly fulfil the purpose of a family.

Weighing these views, I think the statement is too narrow. For religious families educating children in the faith is a vital purpose, and it is certainly one of the three the religions stress. But it depends on the other two: a family must first bring children into the world and keep them safe and loved. So the main purpose is to provide a stable, loving home, within which religious families will also pass on their faith.
:::
