## What it is

This topic has two parts. First, **jihad**: the different understandings of jihad — the meaning and significance of **greater** and **lesser jihad**, and the **origins, influence and conditions** for declaring lesser jihad. Jihad is one of the **Ten Obligatory Acts** of Shi'a Islam; the spec asks you to study it in both Sunni and Shi'a Islam. Second, the **festivals and commemorations** of **Id-ul-Adha**, **Id-ul-Fitr** and **Ashura**: their origins, meanings and importance for Muslims in Great Britain today.

### Jihad: what the word means

**Jihad** means **"striving"** or **"struggle"** — in full, *jihad fi sabilillah*, "striving in the way of God". It is **not** simply "holy war", and most Muslims regard that translation as misleading. The Qur'an tells Muslims to "strive for Allah with the striving due to Him" (22:78) and promises that those who strive for God will be guided to his ways (29:69).

| | Greater jihad (al-jihad al-akbar) | Lesser jihad (al-jihad al-asghar) |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | The inner, personal struggle of every Muslim to live as God wants and to overcome selfish desires (the nafs) | The outward struggle to defend Islam and Muslims — including, under strict conditions, the use of military force |
| **Examples** | Keeping the Five Pillars (getting up for Fajr prayer, fasting in Ramadan); learning the Qur'an; resisting temptations such as anger, greed, lying, gossip, alcohol; working for justice in society; being patient in hardship | Defending a Muslim community that is attacked; protecting the freedom to worship; resisting oppression; also, for many Muslims, non-violent striving such as speaking out against injustice |
| **Who** | Every Muslim, every day, for life | Only when the conditions are met, and only declared by a proper authority |
| **Significance** | Most Muslims see it as the more important jihad: a lifelong test of faith | A last resort to protect Islam and the innocent, never to spread the faith by force |

A saying often quoted (though many scholars regard its chain of narration as weak) reports that the Prophet, returning from a battle, said the Muslims had come back from the lesser jihad to the greater jihad — the struggle with the self. Whether or not the saying is authentic, the idea that the inner struggle comes first is widely accepted in both Sunni and Shi'a Islam.

**Sunni and Shi'a**: jihad is not one of the Five Pillars of Sunni Islam, though it is a duty and some have called it an informal "sixth pillar". In Shi'a Islam it is one of the Ten Obligatory Acts, alongside the related duties of **"commanding what is good"** (amr bil-ma'ruf) and **"forbidding what is evil"** (nahi 'anil-munkar). Many Shi'a scholars teach that in the absence of the Twelfth Imam (in occultation), only **defensive** lesser jihad is permitted.

### Lesser jihad: origins

- In **Makkah** (610–622 CE) the early Muslims were persecuted — insulted, boycotted, tortured and some killed — but they were told to be patient and not fight back.
- After the **hijrah** to **Madinah** in 622 CE, the Makkans continued to attack them. The Qur'an then gave **permission to fight** to those who were being fought against because they had been wronged and driven from their homes only for saying "Our Lord is Allah" (22:39-40).
- The first major battle was **Badr** (624 CE). The key verse limits fighting: "Fight in the way of Allah those who fight you but do not transgress. Indeed, Allah does not like transgressors" (2:190).

### Conditions for declaring lesser jihad

Drawn from the Qur'an, the Prophet's example and the rules taught by scholars (and by the first caliph **Abu Bakr**, who told his army not to kill women, children or the elderly, or to cut down fruit trees or harm animals needlessly):

1. It must be **declared by a legitimate authority** — a recognised Muslim leader or the religious authorities, not any individual or group.
2. There must be a **just cause**: self-defence, defending Muslims who are attacked or oppressed, or protecting the freedom to practise Islam.
3. It must be a **last resort**, after peaceful ways have failed.
4. It must **not** be fought to force people to convert — "There shall be no compulsion in [acceptance of] the religion" (2:256) — or to gain land, wealth or power.
5. **Innocent civilians** (women, children, the elderly, the sick), crops, animals and places of worship must not be harmed; prisoners must be treated fairly.
6. Force must be **proportionate** and must **stop** as soon as the enemy wants peace: "if they incline to peace, then incline to it [also]" (8:61).

Qur'an 5:32 teaches that killing one innocent soul is like killing all humankind, and saving one life is like saving all humankind.

### Influence of jihad today

- **Everyday life**: for most British Muslims jihad means the greater jihad — keeping their faith in a secular society, fasting at school or work, resisting peer pressure, controlling anger, studying and working hard, and campaigning for justice.
- **Misuse**: some extremist groups have used the word "jihad" to justify terrorism. The vast majority of Muslims and scholars reject this, because terrorism breaks every condition: no legitimate authority, targets innocent people, and spreads fear rather than defending anyone. For example, the scholar **Muhammad Tahir-ul-Qadri** issued a long fatwa (ruling) in London in 2010 condemning terrorism and suicide bombing as completely against Islam, and the **Amman Message** (2004), backed by hundreds of scholars, rejected extremism.
- **Consequences**: misuse of the word has fed Islamophobia, so many British Muslims work to explain the true meaning of jihad to others.

### Festivals and commemorations

| | **Id-ul-Fitr** | **Id-ul-Adha** | **Ashura** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **When** | 1 Shawwal — the day after Ramadan ends, when the new moon is seen | 10 Dhul-Hijjah — during Hajj, celebrated by all Muslims worldwide | 10 Muharram — the first month of the Islamic year |
| **Meaning** | "Festival of breaking the fast" | "Festival of sacrifice" | "Tenth" |
| **Mood** | Joy and thanksgiving | Joy, thanksgiving and sharing | Sunni: a day of fasting and thanks; Shi'a: a day of deep mourning |

### Id-ul-Fitr

- **Origins**: fasting in Ramadan was made compulsory after the hijrah (2:183-185), and the Prophet first celebrated Id-ul-Fitr in Madinah in 624 CE. The Qur'an tells Muslims to complete the fast and glorify God for guiding them, so that they may be grateful (2:185).
- **Practices**: the festival begins when the new moon is sighted. Before the Id prayer every Muslim who can must give **Zakat-ul-Fitr** (also called Sadaqat-ul-Fitr), a small payment so that poor Muslims can celebrate too. Muslims wash, wear new or best clothes and attend special **Id prayers** at the mosque or in large open spaces. People greet each other with "Eid Mubarak" (blessed Id), visit family and friends, give children presents or money, share special food, and many visit the graves of relatives.
- **Meaning**: thanking God for the Qur'an (revealed in Ramadan) and for the strength to complete the fast; celebrating the self-discipline learned; sharing with the poor; unity of the ummah.

### Id-ul-Adha

- **Origins**: Ibrahim dreamt that God commanded him to sacrifice his son. He told his son (Isma'il, in Muslim tradition), who replied that he should do as he was commanded and he would find him, God willing, among the steadfast (37:102). As Ibrahim was about to obey, God stopped him and ransomed the son with "a great sacrifice" — a ram (37:107).
- **Practices**: special Id prayers; then the sacrifice (**qurbani**) of a sheep, goat, cow or camel, following Ibrahim's example. The meat is traditionally divided into **three**: for the family, for relatives and friends, and for the poor. Pilgrims on Hajj sacrifice at Mina on the same day. Muslims wear best clothes, visit family and share food.
- **Meaning**: obedience and total submission to God, as Ibrahim showed; willingness to give up what you love for God; sharing with the poor. The Qur'an stresses that the meat and blood do not reach God — "what reaches Him is piety from you" (22:37).

### Ashura

| | **Sunni Muslims** | **Shi'a Muslims** |
|---|---|---|
| **Origins** | When the Prophet came to Madinah he found the Jews fasting on the tenth day because God had saved Musa (Moses) and the Israelites from Pharaoh on that day. He fasted and encouraged Muslims to fast too (hadith, Bukhari and Muslim). Some traditions also say Nuh (Noah) left the ark on this day. | In **680 CE** Imam **Husayn**, grandson of the Prophet and son of Ali, refused to give allegiance to the caliph **Yazid**, whom he saw as unjust. At **Karbala** (in modern Iraq) Husayn and a small band of family and companions (traditionally 72) were surrounded, cut off from water, and killed on 10 Muharram. |
| **Practices** | A voluntary fast (usually with the 9th or 11th as well); a hadith (Muslim) says it expiates the sins of the previous year. Some give charity. | The first ten days of Muharram are a period of **mourning**: people wear black, attend **majlis** gatherings where the story of Karbala is retold and poems recited, join **processions**, and some perform matam (rhythmic chest-beating) or re-enactment plays. A minority practise self-flagellation, which many Shi'a scholars discourage or forbid; instead many give **blood** — for example through the Imam Hussain Blood Donation Campaign, run with NHS Blood and Transplant, or the charity Who is Hussain? — to honour Husayn's sacrifice by saving lives. Many make pilgrimage to Husayn's shrine in Karbala. |
| **Meaning** | Thanks to God for rescuing the faithful; following the Prophet's sunnah | Husayn's martyrdom is a model of standing up against **injustice and tyranny** whatever the cost; it strengthens Shi'a identity and hope |

### Importance of the festivals for Muslims in Great Britain today

- **Community and identity**: the festivals bring Muslims together as a minority, e.g. large open-air Id prayers in city parks such as Small Heath Park in Birmingham, and Ashura processions through city centres such as London.
- **Charity**: Zakat-ul-Fitr and qurbani mean the poor are included. Many British Muslims pay charities such as **Islamic Relief** or Muslim Hands to carry out their qurbani abroad, where the meat is given to people in poverty; in the UK, animals must be slaughtered in licensed slaughterhouses, not at home.
- **Practical challenges**: Id is not a public holiday in Britain, so Muslims may need to book time off work or ask schools for authorised absence. Muslims sometimes celebrate on different days, because some follow local moon sighting and others follow the announcement from Saudi Arabia or astronomical calculation.
- **Sharing faith**: many mosques and schools invite non-Muslims to Id events or open iftars, building understanding.

::: terms
- **Jihad**: striving or struggling in the way of God; it has both an inner (greater) and an outer (lesser) meaning.
- **Greater jihad**: the inner, lifelong struggle of every Muslim to overcome selfish desires and live as God wants.
- **Lesser jihad**: the outward struggle to defend Islam and Muslims, which may include fighting only under strict conditions.
- **Id-ul-Fitr**: the festival of breaking the fast on 1 Shawwal, celebrating the end of Ramadan.
- **Zakat-ul-Fitr**: the charitable payment every able Muslim makes before the Id-ul-Fitr prayer so the poor can celebrate.
- **Id-ul-Adha**: the festival of sacrifice on 10 Dhul-Hijjah, remembering Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice his son.
- **Qurbani**: the sacrifice of an animal at Id-ul-Adha, with the meat shared between family, friends and the poor.
- **Ashura**: the tenth day of Muharram — a voluntary fast for Sunni Muslims and a day of mourning for Husayn for Shi'a Muslims.
- **Karbala**: the place in Iraq where Imam Husayn was killed in 680 CE, now the site of his shrine.
- **Majlis**: a Shi'a gathering during Muharram at which the story of Karbala is retold and mourned.
:::

::: example 4-mark contrasting
Explain two contrasting ways in which Muslims celebrate Id-ul-Adha. (4)

Many British Muslims arrange for their qurbani to be carried out in the UK at a licensed slaughterhouse (1). They then share the meat themselves, keeping a third for the family, giving a third to relatives and friends and a third to the poor, following Ibrahim's example of obedience (1).

In contrast, many other Muslims pay a charity such as Islamic Relief to carry out the sacrifice abroad (1). The meat goes directly to families in poverty who may rarely eat meat, so the celebration focuses on sharing with the poorest rather than feasting at home (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark jihad
Explain two conditions for declaring lesser jihad. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Muslim belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

First, lesser jihad must be for a just cause, such as self-defence (1). The Qur'an first gave permission to fight to Muslims who had been attacked and driven from their homes simply for saying "Our Lord is Allah" (22:39-40), so jihad can never be used to gain land or force people to convert — "There shall be no compulsion in religion" (2:256) (1) (1 for source).

Second, innocent people must not be harmed (1). Muslims fighting must not kill women, children or the elderly, or destroy crops and places of worship, as Abu Bakr instructed his army, and they must stop as soon as the enemy wants peace (8:61) (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark evaluation
"Greater jihad is more important than lesser jihad." Evaluate this statement. (12)

Most Muslims would agree. Greater jihad is the struggle every Muslim faces every single day — to pray five times, fast in Ramadan, control anger and resist temptation — whereas lesser jihad is only ever needed in rare circumstances and must be declared by a legitimate authority. The Qur'an promises that those who strive for God will be guided to his ways (29:69), and many Muslims quote the saying that the Prophet called the struggle against the self the "greater" jihad. Also, a person who has not won the inner struggle could never fight a just lesser jihad, since keeping its strict conditions — protecting civilians, stopping when the enemy offers peace — needs great self-control.

On the other hand, some would argue lesser jihad matters just as much because it protects the whole community. When Muslims were attacked in Madinah, God gave permission to fight (22:39-40); without that defence, the early Muslims might have been destroyed and nobody would be free to practise greater jihad at all. Shi'a Muslims include jihad among the Ten Obligatory Acts alongside commanding good and forbidding evil, showing that the outward struggle for justice is a real duty. Others also point out that the saying about the greater jihad has a weak chain of narration.

Overall, I think the statement is true. Lesser jihad is important when it is genuinely needed, but it is rare, conditional and limited, while greater jihad is required of every Muslim all the time and is the foundation of the lesser. Emphasising greater jihad also corrects the false idea, spread by extremists, that jihad simply means war.
:::
