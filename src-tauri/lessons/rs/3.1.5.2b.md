## What it is

This topic covers two of the Five Pillars that are also among the Ten Obligatory Acts — **sawm** (fasting) and **zakah** (almsgiving) — and the extra Shi'a duty of **Khums**. Both sawm and zakah involve giving something up for God, and both are about more than the individual: they build the **ummah**, the worldwide Muslim community.

### Sawm: fasting during Ramadan

**Ramadan** is the ninth month of the Islamic calendar. Because the calendar is lunar, Ramadan starts about eleven days earlier each year, so over a lifetime Muslims fast in every season — in Britain, summer fasts can last over eighteen hours. The month begins and ends with the sighting of the new moon.

**Origins**

- Ramadan is the month in which the Qur'an was first revealed: "The month of Ramadhan [is that] in which was revealed the Qur'an, a guidance for the people" (2:185).
- Fasting was made compulsory for Muslims in Madinah in the second year after the Hijrah (624 CE). The Qur'an presents it as a practice given to earlier communities too: "O you who have believed, decreed upon you is fasting as it was decreed upon those before you that you may become righteous" (2:183).

**Duties**

- From **dawn to sunset** Muslims do not eat, drink (not even water), smoke or have sex. The Qur'an says they may eat until "the white thread of dawn becomes distinct" from the black thread of night (2:187).
- The fast includes behaviour: Muslims should avoid lying, anger, gossip and bad thoughts. A hadith (Bukhari) teaches that if a person does not give up false speech and acting on it, God has no need for them to give up food and drink.
- Muslims make the **niyyah** (intention) to fast.
- **Suhur** — a meal before dawn. **Iftar** — breaking the fast at sunset, traditionally with dates and water, as Muhammad did, often with family, friends or at the mosque. Shi'a Muslims usually break the fast a little later than Sunni Muslims, when the redness in the sky after sunset has gone.
- Extra worship: reading the whole Qur'an during the month (it is divided into thirty parts, one for each night), extra prayers at night — Sunni Muslims pray **tarawih** in congregation at the mosque; Shi'a Muslims do not pray tarawih in congregation but keep other night prayers and recitations — and extra charity. Many make **i'tikaf**, a retreat in the mosque in the last ten days.
- At the end of the month, before the Id prayer, each Muslim household gives **Zakat al-Fitr** (a small, fixed charity per person) so that the poor can celebrate **Id-ul-Fitr** too.

**Benefits of fasting**

| Benefit | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Obedience and taqwa** | fasting is commanded by God, and its purpose is God-consciousness: "that you may become righteous" (2:183) |
| **Self-discipline** | controlling hunger, thirst and temper trains Muslims to control their desires the rest of the year |
| **Empathy** | feeling hunger helps Muslims understand the poor and makes them more generous |
| **Gratitude** | Muslims appreciate food, water and the blessings they take for granted |
| **Forgiveness** | a hadith (Bukhari) says whoever fasts Ramadan out of faith and hoping for reward will have their previous sins forgiven |
| **Community** | the whole ummah fasts together; iftars at home and in mosques bring families and communities together, and many invite non-Muslims to share |
| **Closeness to the Qur'an** | the month of revelation is spent reading and reflecting on it |

**The exceptions and their reasons**

The Qur'an teaches: "whoever is ill or on a journey — then an equal number of other days. Allah intends for you ease and does not intend for you hardship" (2:185). Fasting is meant to strengthen people, not harm them.

| Who does not have to fast | Reason | What they do instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Children** before puberty | too young; not yet responsible | many practise with part-day fasts |
| **The ill** | fasting could harm health | make up the days later (qada); if the illness is long-term, pay **fidyah** — feeding a poor person for each day missed (2:184) |
| **The elderly** or frail | fasting would cause hardship | fidyah |
| **Pregnant or breastfeeding women** | to protect mother and baby | make up the days later (or fidyah, depending on the school of law) |
| **Women who are menstruating** or after childbirth | they are not in a state of ritual purity | make up the days later |
| **Travellers** | journeys cause hardship | make up the days later |
| People who cannot understand (e.g. severe mental illness) | not accountable | no duty |

Deliberately breaking a fast without a valid reason is serious: the person must make up the day and pay **kaffarah** (expiation), traditionally fasting sixty consecutive days or feeding sixty poor people.

**The Night of Power (Laylat al-Qadr)**

The Night of Power is the night in 610 CE when Muhammad, meditating in the **Cave of Hira** on Mount Nur, received the first revelation from the angel **Jibril**. According to a hadith in Bukhari, Jibril commanded him to recite; Muhammad replied that he was not a reader, and Jibril squeezed him tightly three times before revealing the first verses:

"Recite in the name of your Lord who created — Created man from a clinging substance. Recite, and your Lord is the most Generous — Who taught by the pen — Taught man that which he knew not." (Qur'an 96:1-5)

- **Significance of the words**: God is the **Creator** of all things and of humans; God is **generous**; God is the source of all **knowledge** — the command "recite" (iqra, also "read") and the mention of "the pen" show that learning and knowledge are valued in Islam.
- **Significance of the night**: it is the start of the revelation of the Qur'an and of Muhammad's prophethood. "The Night of Decree is better than a thousand months" (97:3): worship on this one night is worth more than over eighty years of worship.
- **When**: it falls in the **last ten nights** of Ramadan; the exact night is not known. Many Sunni Muslims mark the **27th**; Shi'a Muslims especially mark the **19th, 21st and 23rd** nights (the 19th and 21st are also linked with the attack on and death of Imam Ali).
- **How it is marked**: Muslims spend the night in the mosque or at home in prayer, reading the Qur'an and asking for forgiveness, believing that God's decrees for the coming year are set on this night and that sins are forgiven.

### Zakah: almsgiving

**Zakah** means "purification" and also "growth". It is the compulsory payment of **2.5%** of a Muslim's wealth that is above a minimum amount, the **nisab** (commonly worked out as the value of 85 grams of gold or 595 grams of silver), once it has been held for a full lunar year.

**Origins**

- The Qur'an repeatedly joins zakah with prayer: "And establish prayer and give zakah" (2:43; also 2:110). It was made a formal, regular duty in Madinah, where the first Muslim community was organised, and was collected in Muhammad's time and by the early caliphs.
- Muslims believe giving to the poor was part of the message of all prophets.

**How it is given**

- Each year a Muslim calculates savings, gold and silver, business stock and other wealth above what they need to live on; 2.5% of it is due. There are also set rates on farm produce and livestock.
- Many pay during **Ramadan**, when good deeds are believed to earn greater reward.
- It may be given directly to those in need, through the mosque, or through Muslim charities such as **Islamic Relief** or the **National Zakat Foundation** in the UK. In some Muslim-majority countries the state collects it.
- It should be given discreetly, without showing off, so that the giver is sincere and the receiver is not embarrassed.
- The Qur'an lists who may receive it: "Zakah expenditures are only for the poor and for the needy and for those employed to collect [zakah] and for bringing hearts together [for Islam] and for freeing captives [or slaves] and for those in debt and for the cause of Allah and for the [stranded] traveler" (9:60).
- In **Shi'a** law, zakah is due on a narrower set of goods (certain crops, livestock, and gold and silver coins), and Khums (below) covers wider income.
- On top of zakah, Muslims give voluntary charity, **sadaqah**, which can be money, time or a kind act.

**Why Muslims give zakah**

- **It is commanded by God** and is one of the Five Pillars and Ten Obligatory Acts.
- **Everything belongs to God**; humans are only trustees (khalifah) of their wealth, so sharing it is returning what is God's.
- **Purification**: it purifies the wealth, making the rest halal and blessed, and purifies the giver's heart from greed and selfishness: "Take from their wealth a charity by which you purify them and cause them increase" (9:103).
- **Justice and the ummah**: it reduces the gap between rich and poor and builds a caring community.
- **Reward**: Muslims believe God rewards giving in this life and on the Day of Judgement.

**Benefits of receipt**

- The poor receive help with food, housing, debts and education, so their basic needs are met.
- It is a **right**, not a favour: "And those within whose wealth is a known right, for the petitioner and the deprived" (70:24-25). So the receiver keeps their **dignity** and need not feel ashamed.
- It helps people get back on their feet — paying off debt, starting work — so they may one day pay zakah themselves.
- It reduces envy, crime and division in society and strengthens the bond between Muslims.

### Khums in Shi'a Islam

**Khums** means "one fifth". It is one of the **Ten Obligatory Acts**.

- **Source**: "And know that anything you obtain of war booty — then indeed, for Allah is one fifth of it and for the Messenger and for [his] near relatives and the orphans, the needy, and the [stranded] traveler" (8:41). Shi'a Muslims understand "anything you obtain" to include **all gains**, not just war booty. Most Sunni Muslims apply this verse only to spoils of war (and to buried treasure and minerals), so Khums is not a general duty in Sunni Islam.
- **How much**: **20%** of a person's **surplus income** — what is left at the end of their financial year after paying for living expenses for themselves and their family.
- **How it is divided**:
  - **Sahm al-Imam** (the Imam's share, half): since the Twelfth Imam is hidden, it is given to a **marja'** (senior religious scholar) or their representative, and used for religious purposes — mosques, seminaries, schools, spreading Islam, and helping the needy.
  - **Sahm as-Sadat** (the share of the Sayyids, half): given to poor **descendants of the Prophet** (sayyids). Both Sunni and Shi'a Muslims teach that the Prophet's family may not receive zakah, so this share supports them instead.
- **Significance**: it shows that all gain comes from God; it keeps Shi'a religious institutions independent of governments; it links believers to their marja' and to the Prophet's family (tawalla); and it purifies wealth, as zakah does.

**Influence on Muslims today**: Muslims in Britain organise their year around Ramadan — adjusting school and work, taking part in community iftars, and giving generously. Many British Muslims calculate zakah online through charity websites, and charities such as Islamic Relief run large appeals in Ramadan. Shi'a Muslims pay Khums through their marja's offices, funding mosques and Islamic centres in the UK.

::: terms
- **Sawm**: fasting from dawn to sunset during Ramadan; one of the Five Pillars and Ten Obligatory Acts.
- **Ramadan**: the ninth month of the Islamic calendar, in which the Qur'an was first revealed and Muslims fast.
- **Suhur and iftar**: the meal eaten before dawn, and the meal that breaks the fast at sunset.
- **Fidyah**: compensation for missing fasts that cannot be made up — feeding a poor person for each day missed.
- **Laylat al-Qadr**: the Night of Power, when the first verses of the Qur'an (96:1-5) were revealed to Muhammad.
- **Zakah**: compulsory almsgiving of 2.5% of wealth above the nisab, held for a year; means "purification".
- **Nisab**: the minimum amount of wealth a Muslim must have before zakah is due.
- **Sadaqah**: voluntary charity — money, time or kind acts given freely in addition to zakah.
- **Khums**: "one fifth" — the Shi'a duty to pay 20% of surplus income, split between religious causes and the Prophet's poor descendants.
- **Zakat al-Fitr**: a small charity paid for each person before the Id-ul-Fitr prayer so that the poor can celebrate.
:::

::: example 4-mark benefits
Explain two benefits of fasting during Ramadan. (4)

Fasting teaches self-discipline (1). By controlling hunger, thirst and temper from dawn to sunset for a whole month, Muslims learn to control their desires, which helps them resist temptation and sin for the rest of the year (1).

Fasting also helps Muslims understand the poor (1). Feeling hungry themselves makes them more grateful for what they have and more generous — many give extra sadaqah and pay their zakah during Ramadan (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark Night of Power
Explain two reasons why the Night of Power is important to Muslims. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Muslim belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

It is the night on which Muhammad received the first revelation of the Qur'an from the angel Jibril in the Cave of Hira (1). The first words were "Recite in the name of your Lord who created" (96:1), which marks the start of God's final message to humanity and of Muhammad's prophethood (1 + 1 for source).

It is also the most blessed night of the year (1). The Qur'an says it is "better than a thousand months" (97:3), so Muslims spend the night — often in the last ten nights of Ramadan, such as the 27th for many Sunnis or the 23rd for many Shi'a — praying, reading the Qur'an and asking God's forgiveness (1).
:::

::: example 4-mark contrasting
Explain two contrasting ways in which Muslims give to the poor. (4)

All Muslims who have wealth above the nisab pay zakah, 2.5% of their savings held for a year (1). It is given to the groups listed in 9:60, such as the poor, the needy and those in debt, often during Ramadan through a mosque or a charity like Islamic Relief (1).

Shi'a Muslims also pay Khums, 20% of their surplus income at the end of their financial year (1). Half goes to a marja' for religious causes and the needy, and half to poor descendants of the Prophet, who cannot receive zakah (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark evaluation
"Zakah is more important than sawm." Evaluate this statement. (12)

**For the statement**: zakah directly helps other people. It meets the needs of the poor, the needy and those in debt (9:60), reducing poverty and inequality and strengthening the whole ummah. The Qur'an joins zakah with prayer again and again ("establish prayer and give zakah", 2:43), which suggests it is one of the most central duties. It also purifies the giver from greed (9:103), and because the poor have "a known right" to it (70:24-25), failing to pay it is a form of injustice to others, not just a personal failing. Fasting mainly benefits the person who fasts, so zakah may seem more important because its effects reach society.

**Against the statement**: sawm is commanded just as clearly — "decreed upon you is fasting" (2:183) — and both are pillars, so many Muslims say it is wrong to rank them: a building needs all its pillars. Sawm also has social benefits: it teaches empathy with the hungry and leads to extra charity, including Zakat al-Fitr, so the two pillars work together. Sawm is done by almost every adult Muslim, while zakah is only due from those above the nisab, so sawm could be seen as the more universal act of submission. Its purpose, taqwa (God-consciousness), shapes a Muslim's whole character, and fasting is linked with the month of the Qur'an and the Night of Power, "better than a thousand months" (97:3).

**Conclusion**: the arguments for zakah are strong, because its effect on the poor is direct and the Qur'an links it so closely with prayer. But the argument that the pillars cannot be ranked is stronger: both are commanded by God, and each supports the other — fasting builds the empathy that makes giving sincere, and giving turns that empathy into help. So I disagree that zakah is more important; they are equally important in different ways.
:::

::: watch
- Mixing up percentages: **zakah is 2.5%** of wealth above the nisab; **Khums is 20%** of surplus income. Khums is a Shi'a duty only.
- Saying Ramadan is "a festival". It is a month of fasting; the festival is **Id-ul-Fitr** at its end.
- Listing exceptions without reasons. "Explain" questions need why — usually 2:185, "Allah intends for you ease".
- Getting 96:1-5 wrong: it is the **first** revelation, in the Cave of Hira, through **Jibril**, on the Night of Power — and it stresses creation and knowledge ("taught by the pen").
- Forgetting that fasting includes behaviour, not just food and drink.
- In 12-mark answers, giving only one side or no teaching: that caps you at Level 2.
:::
