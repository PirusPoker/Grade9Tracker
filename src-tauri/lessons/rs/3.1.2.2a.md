## What it is

**Worship** is showing love, praise and reverence to God. Christians worship together in church and privately at home, and the style varies enormously between denominations. The spec asks for **different forms of worship and their significance** — **liturgical**, **non-liturgical** and **informal** worship (including how the **Bible** is used), and **private** worship — and **prayer and its significance**, including the **Lord's Prayer**, **set prayers** and **informal prayer**.

Christians worship together because Jesus promised, "where two or three gather in my name, there am I with them" (Matthew 18:20), and the first Christians "devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and to fellowship, to the breaking of bread and to prayer" (Acts 2:42). Sunday is the main day of worship because it is the day of the resurrection.

### Three forms of public worship

| Form | What it is like | Examples | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Liturgical** | A set order of service with fixed words, actions and prayers, following the Church's year and often led by a priest. | Catholic **Mass**; Orthodox **Divine Liturgy**; Anglican Holy Communion using the **Book of Common Prayer** (1662) or **Common Worship**. | Unites Christians worldwide and across centuries saying the same words; familiar words help people pray; ritual and symbol (vestments, candles, incense, icons) express reverence for a holy God; the Eucharist is at the centre. |
| **Non-liturgical** | A structured service but without a fixed text — the minister chooses hymns, readings and prayers, and the **sermon** is central. | Many **Baptist**, **Methodist**, **United Reformed** and independent evangelical churches. | Flexible — can respond to the congregation's needs and current events; focus on hearing and understanding God's word in the Bible; prayers in everyday language. |
| **Informal** | Spontaneous and unstructured, often led by lay people; people respond as they feel moved by the Holy Spirit. | **Pentecostal** and charismatic worship (lively music, raised hands, speaking in tongues); house churches; **Quaker** meetings. | Feels personal and alive; allows the Holy Spirit to guide the worship; everyone can take part, not just clergy. |

**Quaker worship** (the Religious Society of Friends) is a special form of informal worship: people sit together in silence, with no priest, set prayers or music, waiting on God. Anyone who feels moved by the Spirit may stand and speak — this is called **ministry**. Quakers believe there is "that of God" in everyone, so no one needs to stand between a person and God.

### Inside liturgical worship

- **Catholic Mass**: the **Liturgy of the Word** (readings from the Old Testament, a psalm, a New Testament letter and the Gospel, then a homily) and the **Liturgy of the Eucharist** (the bread and wine are consecrated and received as the body and blood of Christ). Catholics are expected to attend Mass every Sunday.
- **Orthodox Divine Liturgy**: long, sung services with incense and **icons**; the altar is behind the **iconostasis**, a screen of icons, symbolising the mystery of heaven; most of the congregation traditionally stands.
- **Anglican** worship ranges from very liturgical ("high church", close to the Mass) to simple ("low church"), but uses authorised texts such as Common Worship.
- The **Church year** (Advent, Christmas, Lent, Easter, Pentecost) gives shape to the readings, colours and prayers.

### The use of the Bible in worship

- **Readings**: in liturgical churches set passages are read according to a **lectionary**, so the whole Bible is heard over a cycle. In Catholic and Orthodox services the Gospel book is carried in procession and everyone stands for the Gospel, showing it is the words of Jesus.
- **Sermon or homily**: explains the passage and applies it to daily life. In many Protestant churches the sermon is the high point, reflecting the belief that the Bible is the supreme authority.
- **Prayers and hymns** often use Bible words — the Psalms are sung, and the Lord's Prayer comes from the Gospels.
- **Bible study** groups meet during the week, especially in evangelical churches, to read and discuss scripture together.

### Private worship

Private worship is worship done alone or with family, outside church services. Jesus taught, "when you pray, go into your room, close the door and pray to your Father, who is unseen" (Matthew 6:6).

- **Personal prayer**: daily prayer in the morning or evening, grace before meals, family prayers.
- **Bible reading**: daily devotional readings or reflective methods such as **lectio divina** (slow, prayerful reading).
- **Catholic devotions**: the **rosary** — a string of beads used to pray the Hail Mary, Our Father and Glory Be while meditating on events in the lives of Jesus and Mary.
- **Orthodox devotions**: many Orthodox homes have an **icon corner** with icons and a lamp, where the family prays; the **Jesus Prayer** ("Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner") is repeated often, sometimes with a prayer rope.
- **Meditation and silence**, worship music, and apps or books of daily prayers.

**Significance**: private worship builds a personal relationship with God every day, not just on Sundays; it fits any time and place; it lets Christians bring their own worries and thanks to God. Public worship, by contrast, builds community, lets Christians receive the sacraments and encourages them (Hebrews 10:25 urges believers not to give up meeting together). Most Christians see the two as complementary.

### Prayer and its significance

**Prayer** is communicating with God — speaking and listening. It is significant because it builds the relationship with God, lets Christians praise, thank, confess and ask for help, and follows Jesus' own example: he prayed often, sometimes alone, and in Gethsemane prayed, "Yet not what I will, but what you will" (Mark 14:36). Paul tells Christians to "pray continually" (1 Thessalonians 5:17).

Christians often describe four kinds of prayer (**ACTS**): **Adoration** (praising God), **Confession** (saying sorry for sin), **Thanksgiving** and **Supplication** (asking for help for oneself — petition — or for others — intercession).

- **Set prayers** are prayers with fixed words, written down and repeated: the Lord's Prayer, the Hail Mary, the Jesus Prayer, collects from the Book of Common Prayer. **Significance**: they are shared by Christians everywhere, so they unite the Church; they express beliefs accurately; they help when a person cannot find the words (in grief or illness); they can be learned by heart.
- **Informal (extempore) prayer** is prayer in one's own words, spontaneously. **Significance**: it is personal and honest, like talking to a friend; it can respond to any situation; in Pentecostal worship it may include **speaking in tongues**. Some Christians feel set prayers can become mechanical if said without thinking; others feel informal prayer can drift or become self-centred.

### The Lord's Prayer

Jesus taught this prayer when his disciples asked, "Lord, teach us to pray" (Luke 11:1–4); the longer version is in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 6:9–13). It is said at almost every Christian service and by millions privately every day. The traditional English words are:

> Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name; thy kingdom come; thy will be done; on earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread. And forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive those who trespass against us. And lead us not into temptation; but deliver us from evil. For thine is the kingdom, the power and the glory, for ever and ever. Amen.

| Line | Meaning |
|---|---|
| "Our Father, who art in heaven" | God is a loving parent close to believers ("our" — all Christians are one family), yet also holy and beyond the world. |
| "Hallowed be thy name" | God's name is holy and must be respected — **adoration**. |
| "Thy kingdom come; thy will be done…" | Christians pray for God's rule of justice and peace on earth, and commit to doing God's will — this motivates work for justice. |
| "Give us this day our daily bread" | Trust in God to provide what is needed (not luxuries) — **supplication**; reminds Christians to share with those who lack food. |
| "Forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive…" | **Confession** — and Christians must forgive others if they expect God's forgiveness (Matthew 6:14–15). |
| "Lead us not into temptation; but deliver us from evil" | Asking for strength to resist sin and protection from evil. |
| "For thine is the kingdom…" | A closing **doxology** (words of praise) added in early church worship; it is not in the oldest manuscripts of Matthew. Protestants usually say it; at Mass Catholics say a similar acclamation after a short prayer by the priest. |

**Significance of the Lord's Prayer**: it was given by Jesus himself, so it is the model for all prayer; it contains all the main types of prayer in a few lines; it unites all denominations — Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant Christians all use it; it teaches how to live (forgiveness, trust, seeking God's will before one's own needs).

::: terms
- **Worship**: acts of praise, love and reverence shown to God, in public services or in private.
- **Liturgical worship**: church services that follow a set order, fixed words and ritual, such as the Catholic Mass.
- **Non-liturgical worship**: structured services without a fixed text, where the minister chooses prayers and hymns and the sermon is central.
- **Informal worship**: spontaneous, unstructured worship, often led by lay people, such as charismatic or Quaker worship.
- **Private worship**: worship carried out alone or with family at home, such as personal prayer, Bible reading or the rosary.
- **Prayer**: communicating with God — praising, thanking, confessing and asking, and listening to God.
- **Set prayer**: a prayer with fixed words that is repeated, such as the Lord's Prayer or the Hail Mary.
- **Informal prayer**: prayer in a person's own words, made up at the time rather than read or learned.
- **The Lord's Prayer**: the prayer Jesus taught his disciples (Matthew 6:9–13; Luke 11:2–4), beginning "Our Father".
- **Lectionary**: a set list of Bible readings for each Sunday and festival, used in liturgical churches.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 (Christianity), Practices question: five parts worth 1, 2, 4, 5 and 12 marks.** About 25 minutes for the whole question. (The +3 SPaG marks go on the Beliefs 12-mark, not this one — but write accurately anyway.)

1. **1 mark — multiple choice**: four options, tick one. Common targets: which is a set prayer; which denomination holds silent worship; what "liturgical" means.
2. **2 marks — "Give two…"**: two short, separate, correct points ("the Mass"; "Quaker meetings"). Only the first two count.
3. **4 marks — "Explain two ways…" / "Explain two contrasting…"**: two points, each **developed** (1 simple + 1 detailed). **Contrasting** pairs for this topic: liturgical vs informal worship (Catholic Mass vs Quaker silence or Pentecostal praise); set vs informal prayer; public vs private worship. Each point needs *what it is* plus *an example or its significance*.
4. **5 marks — "Explain two… Refer to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching in your answer"**: two developed points (4) **+ 1 for a relevant, accurate source**. Good sources: Matthew 6:6 (pray in private), Matthew 6:9–13 (Lord's Prayer), Matthew 18:20 (two or three gathered), Acts 2:42, 1 Thessalonians 5:17, Mark 14:36. Link the source to the point.
5. **12 marks — "'Statement.' Evaluate this statement."** Paper 1 bullets: *refer to Christian teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion.* (On the themes paper: "refer to religious arguments; may refer to non-religious arguments".)

**How the 12-mark levels are reached** (the whole answer goes in one level):
- **Level 1 (1–3)**: a point of view with a reason stated.
- **Level 2 (4–6)**: one view argued with a logical chain, **or** different views recognised with reasons. **Maximum Level 2 with no reference to religion.**
- **Level 3 (7–9)**: reasoned consideration of **different** points of view, with logical chains drawing on knowledge, and **clear reference to religion**.
- **Level 4 (10–12)**: a **well-argued** response weighing views with logical chains **leading to a justified judgement**, with Christian teaching *applied* to the issue.

Practice statements here usually set two forms against each other ("Private prayer is more important than going to church"; "Set prayers are better than informal prayer"). Use named denominations as evidence on each side, and conclude by weighing — for most Christians the forms complement each other, but say *why* one matters more in your view.
:::

::: example 4-mark — contrasting forms of worship
Explain two contrasting forms of Christian worship. (4)

Liturgical worship follows a set pattern with fixed words and rituals (1), such as the Catholic Mass, where the priest leads the same prayers used by Catholics all over the world and the service centres on the Eucharist, which unites believers across the globe (1). In contrast, informal worship has no fixed order (1): at a Quaker meeting people sit in silence with no priest or set prayers, and anyone may stand to speak if they feel moved by the Spirit, because Quakers believe God can speak through anyone (1).
:::

::: example 5-mark — the Lord's Prayer
Explain two reasons why the Lord's Prayer is important to Christians. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

It is important because Jesus himself taught it (1). When his disciples asked, "Lord, teach us to pray" (Luke 11:1), he gave them this prayer, so Christians see it as the perfect model of how to pray, praising God before asking for their own needs (1 + 1 source). It also teaches Christians how to live (1): the line "forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive those who trespass against us" reminds them that they must forgive others if they want God's forgiveness, so it shapes how they treat people who hurt them (1).
:::

::: example 12-mark — private and public worship
"Christians do not need to go to church to worship God." Evaluate this statement. In your answer you should: refer to Christian teaching; give reasoned arguments to support this statement; give reasoned arguments to support a different point of view; reach a justified conclusion. (12)

Some Christians would agree. God is everywhere (omnipresent), so he can be worshipped anywhere — at home, at work or outdoors. Jesus taught, "when you pray, go into your room, close the door and pray to your Father, who is unseen" (Matthew 6:6), which suggests private prayer is at least as valuable as public worship, and he criticised people who prayed in public to be seen. Private worship, such as daily Bible reading, the rosary or the Jesus Prayer, can build a close personal relationship with God every day of the week, not just for an hour on Sunday. Christians who are ill, housebound or working shifts can still worship fully, for example through online services.

However, most Christians believe going to church is essential. Jesus promised to be present "where two or three gather in my name" (Matthew 18:20), and the first Christians met regularly for "the breaking of bread and prayer" (Acts 2:42). Catholics and Orthodox Christians believe the sacraments, especially the Eucharist, are the main way God's grace is received, and these need a priest and a community, so Catholics are expected to attend Mass every Sunday. Hebrews 10:25 tells believers not to give up meeting together, because church gives support, teaching through sermons and fellowship when faith is weak.

In conclusion, it is true that a Christian *can* worship God anywhere, so going to church is not the only way to worship. But the stronger view is that Christians do *need* church, because Christianity is a faith lived as one body, and the Eucharist and mutual support cannot be found alone. Private and public worship work best together.
:::

::: q
Give two features of liturgical worship. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): a set order of service; fixed words and prayers (e.g. from the Book of Common Prayer or the Roman Missal); led by a priest or minister; follows the Church year and a lectionary of Bible readings; ritual actions and symbols such as vestments, candles and incense; centred on the Eucharist.
:::

::: q
Describe Quaker worship and explain one reason why Quakers worship in this way. (3)
---
Quakers meet in silence, sitting together without a priest, set prayers, hymns or sacraments (1); anyone who feels moved by the Holy Spirit may stand and speak — "ministry" (1). Reason: Quakers believe there is "that of God" in everyone and God can speak to anyone directly, so no priest or ritual is needed between people and God (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two differences between set prayers and informal prayers. (4)
---
Set prayers use fixed words written by others and repeated, such as the Lord's Prayer or Hail Mary (1), whereas informal prayers are in a person's own words, spontaneously (1). Set prayers unite Christians who say the same words across the world and help when people cannot find words, e.g. in grief (1), while informal prayers are more personal and can respond to any situation, like talking to a friend — in Pentecostal worship they may include speaking in tongues (1).
:::

::: q
Name the four types of prayer in "ACTS" and give an example of each. (4)
---
Adoration — praising God, e.g. "hallowed be thy name" in the Lord's Prayer (1). Confession — saying sorry for sin, e.g. "forgive us our trespasses" (1). Thanksgiving — thanking God for blessings, e.g. saying grace before a meal (1). Supplication — asking God for help for oneself (petition) or others (intercession), e.g. "give us this day our daily bread" or praying for the sick (1).
:::

::: q
Explain two ways in which private worship is significant for Christians. (4)
---
Any two, developed (1 + 1 each). It builds a personal relationship with God every day (1) — e.g. daily prayer and Bible reading keep faith alive between Sundays (1). It follows Jesus' teaching (1): he told his followers to pray in their room with the door shut (Matthew 6:6), so it is sincere rather than for show (1). It lets people pray about their own needs (1), confessing or asking for help in their own words (1). Particular devotions deepen faith (1): Catholics meditate on the life of Jesus with the rosary; Orthodox pray before icons at home (1).
:::

::: q

Any two (1 each): Sunday is the day of Jesus' resurrection; the Church teaches it is an obligation; to receive the Eucharist, the body and blood of Christ; to hear the Bible read and explained; to worship as a community (Matthew 18:20; Acts 2:42); to receive God's grace.
