## What it is

This lesson covers all of Part two, **the development of the Cold War**: **the significance of events in Asia** (the USSR's support for Mao and the communist revolution in China; the campaigns of North Korea against the UN and of the Vietcong against France and the USA); **military rivalries** (the **arms race**, the membership and purposes of **NATO** and the **Warsaw Pact**, and the **space race**, including **Sputnik, ICBMs, Polaris, Gagarin and Apollo**); and **the "Thaw"** (Hungary, the protest movement and Nagy's reforms, Soviet fears and reactions and their effects, and the **U2 crisis** and its effects on the **Paris summit**).

The big idea: in the 1950s the Cold War spread from Europe to **Asia** and became a **military and technological** competition. China's revolution and the Korean War convinced the USA that communism was a global threat; the two sides built rival alliances and ever more destructive weapons. After Stalin's death a **Thaw** raised hopes of better relations, but Soviet tanks in Hungary (1956) and the shooting down of a U2 spy plane (1960) showed the limits of change.

### 1. Asia: China, 1949

- A civil war between the **Communists** under **Mao Zedong** (Mao Tse-tung) and the **Nationalists** under **Chiang Kai-shek** resumed after 1945. The USA backed the Nationalists with money and weapons.
- Mao won, and proclaimed the **People's Republic of China** in **October 1949**. Chiang fled to the island of **Taiwan** (Formosa).
- **The USSR's support:** Stalin recognised Mao's government and in **1950** signed a **treaty of friendship** with China, providing loans, technical advisers and military aid.
- **Significance:** the world's most populous country was now communist. Americans spoke of having "lost" China; fear of communism at home intensified, and the USA refused to recognise Mao's government, keeping Taiwan in China's seat at the United Nations. Containment now had to apply in Asia.

### 2. The Korean War, 1950–53

**Background.** Korea had been occupied by Japan. In 1945 it was divided at the **38th parallel**: the USSR occupied the north and the USA the south. Two rival states emerged in 1948: the communist **North** under **Kim Il-sung** and the anti-communist **South** under **Syngman Rhee**, each claiming the whole country.

| Stage | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Invasion (June 1950)** | On **25 June 1950** North Korea invaded the South, with Stalin's approval and Soviet weapons. It quickly captured Seoul and pushed the South's forces back to a small area around **Pusan** in the south-east |
| **The UN responds** | The USA took the issue to the **UN Security Council**, which voted to send forces to help South Korea. The USSR could not veto this because it was **boycotting** the Security Council in protest at the UN's refusal to give China's seat to Mao's government |
| **Inchon (September 1950)** | UN forces, mainly American and led by General **Douglas MacArthur**, landed at **Inchon** behind North Korean lines, recaptured Seoul and drove the North Koreans back |
| **Crossing the 38th parallel (October 1950)** | The UN forces pushed deep into North Korea, towards the **Yalu River** on the border with China. The aim shifted from containing communism to **rolling it back** |
| **China intervenes (late 1950)** | Mao, fearing an American army on his border, sent huge numbers of Chinese "volunteers". They drove the UN forces back south of the 38th parallel |
| **Stalemate and MacArthur's dismissal (1951)** | The front stabilised near the 38th parallel. MacArthur publicly called for attacks on China, possibly with atomic weapons; **Truman sacked him in April 1951** to avoid a wider war |
| **Armistice (July 1953)** | After two years of peace talks, an **armistice** was signed. Korea stayed divided close to the 38th parallel, with a demilitarised zone between the two states |

**Significance for superpower relations**
- The USA had shown it would **fight** to contain communism, and the war turned containment into a **global, military** policy.
- The USA greatly increased **defence spending** and built alliances in Asia, such as **SEATO (1954)**, and committed itself to defending **Taiwan**.
- **China** and the USA became bitter enemies. The USSR had avoided direct involvement but supplied weapons and some air support.
- The UN had acted, but it was clearly dominated by the USA, and a war of millions of casualties had ended roughly where it began.

### 3. Vietnam: from France to the USA

- **Vietnam** was part of **French Indochina**. After 1945 the communist-led **Vietminh** under **Ho Chi Minh** fought for independence against France.
- After 1949 **China** supplied the Vietminh, and the **USA** paid an increasing share of France's war costs, seeing the conflict as part of the struggle against communism.
- **Dien Bien Phu (1954):** the Vietminh besieged and defeated a large French force at this base in the north. France decided to withdraw.
- **The Geneva Agreements (1954):** Vietnam was temporarily divided at the **17th parallel**: a communist North under Ho Chi Minh and a non-communist South. Elections to reunite the country were planned for 1956 but were **never held**, because the USA and the South feared Ho would win.
- **US involvement:** the USA backed the South's government under **Ngo Dinh Diem**. President Eisenhower explained this with the **domino theory**: if one country fell to communism, its neighbours would follow, like a row of falling dominoes.
- In the South a communist guerrilla movement, the **Vietcong** (the National Liberation Front, formed in 1960), fought Diem's government with help from the North. The USA sent money, weapons and growing numbers of **military advisers**, and in the mid-1960s full-scale combat forces.

### 4. The arms race

| Date | Development |
|---|---|
| 1945 | The USA uses **atomic bombs** on Japan; it has a monopoly |
| **1949** | The **USSR tests its first atomic bomb**, much earlier than the West expected |
| 1952 | The USA tests the far more powerful **hydrogen bomb (H-bomb)** |
| 1953 | The USSR tests its own hydrogen bomb |
| 1950s | Both build fleets of long-range **bombers** to carry nuclear weapons |
| **1957** | The USSR tests the first **ICBM** (intercontinental ballistic missile), able to reach the USA from Soviet territory |
| Late 1950s | The USA develops its own ICBMs; Americans fear a **"missile gap"** in the Soviet favour (in fact the USA had more missiles) |
| **1960** | The USA successfully tests **Polaris**, a missile fired from a **submarine** under water. Submarines could hide and move close to the enemy, so a surprise attack could never destroy all of a country's weapons |

**Why it mattered:** each side feared the other might gain the ability to win a nuclear war. The result was **deterrence**: neither side dared attack because the other could retaliate and destroy it, an idea later called **mutually assured destruction**. The arms race consumed vast sums, kept fear high, and made every crisis potentially catastrophic.

### 5. NATO and the Warsaw Pact

| | **NATO** (North Atlantic Treaty Organisation) | **Warsaw Pact** |
|---|---|---|
| Founded | **April 1949**, after the Berlin Blockade | **May 1955**, just after **West Germany joined NATO** |
| Members | **12 founders**: USA, Canada, Britain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Denmark, Norway, Iceland, Italy, Portugal. **Greece and Turkey** joined in 1952 and **West Germany** in **1955** | The **USSR**, **Poland**, **Czechoslovakia**, **Hungary**, **Romania**, **Bulgaria**, **Albania** and **East Germany** |
| Key principle | An attack on one member is an attack on all (collective defence) | Mutual defence under a joint command |
| Purposes | Deter a Soviet attack on Western Europe; commit the **USA** and its nuclear weapons to Europe's defence; contain communism | Respond to West German rearmament; place Eastern armies under **Soviet command**; justify Soviet troops in Eastern Europe; keep the satellites loyal |
| Significance | The USA's first peacetime military alliance; ended isolationism for good | Formalised the division of Europe into two armed camps; later used against its own members (Hungary 1956, Czechoslovakia 1968) |

### 6. The space race

The space race was closely linked to the arms race: the rockets that launched satellites could also carry nuclear warheads, and success in space was a huge **propaganda** weapon showing which system was more advanced.

| Date | Achievement | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **October 1957** | The USSR launches **Sputnik 1**, the first artificial satellite | A shock to the USA: if the USSR could put a satellite in orbit, its rockets could reach America. It fuelled fears of a missile gap |
| November 1957 | **Sputnik 2** carries a dog, Laika, into orbit | Showed living things could be sent into space |
| 1958 | The USA launches its first satellite and sets up **NASA**; it increases spending on science education | The American response |
| **April 1961** | **Yuri Gagarin** (USSR) becomes the **first person in space** | Another Soviet propaganda triumph |
| 1961 | President **Kennedy** sets the goal of landing an American on the Moon before the end of the decade | The race shifts to the Moon |
| **July 1969** | **Apollo 11**: **Neil Armstrong** and **Buzz Aldrin** land on the **Moon** | The USA wins the race to the Moon; a major American propaganda victory |

### 7. The Thaw and Hungary, 1956

**The Thaw**
- **Stalin died in March 1953.** After a power struggle **Nikita Khrushchev** emerged as Soviet leader.
- Khrushchev spoke of **peaceful coexistence** with the West: competition without war. Relations improved: the Korean War ended, Soviet troops left **Austria** (1955), and the leaders met at a summit in **Geneva** (1955). Khrushchev also made peace with Tito.
- In **February 1956**, in a **"secret speech"** to the Communist Party, Khrushchev **denounced Stalin's crimes**. News of it spread, encouraging people in Eastern Europe to hope for more freedom.

**Hungary: the protest movement and Nagy's reforms**
- Hungary had been run by the harsh Stalinist **Rákosi**: secret police, poverty, Soviet control and the hated presence of Soviet troops. After the secret speech, Rákosi was removed in mid-1956.
- In **October 1956** students and workers demonstrated in **Budapest**, tearing down a huge statue of Stalin. Fighting broke out with the secret police and Soviet troops.
- The reformer **Imre Nagy** became Prime Minister. Soviet troops withdrew from Budapest. Nagy announced **reforms**: political prisoners released, other political parties allowed and **free elections** promised; and on **1 November** he announced that Hungary would **leave the Warsaw Pact** and become **neutral**.

**Soviet fears and reaction**
- Khrushchev could accept some reform, but leaving the Warsaw Pact threatened the **buffer zone** and could lead other satellites to follow. Allowing it would also make him look weak to hardliners in Moscow.
- On **4 November 1956** Soviet tanks and troops re-entered Budapest and crushed the rising. Thousands of Hungarians were killed and about **200,000** fled to the West. **Nagy** was arrested and later **executed** (1958). **János Kádár** became leader, loyal to Moscow.

**Effects on the Cold War**
- The **West did nothing** beyond protests and accepting refugees. The USA would not risk nuclear war in the Soviet sphere, and Britain and France were distracted by the **Suez crisis**. Hungarians who had been encouraged by Western radio broadcasts felt betrayed.
- It showed that **de-Stalinisation had limits**: the USSR would not allow a satellite to leave the Warsaw Pact.
- It confirmed that the West would accept Soviet control of Eastern Europe in practice: the Iron Curtain was not going to be challenged by force.
- The **UN** condemned the invasion but could do nothing. Western communist parties lost members in disgust.

### 8. The U2 crisis and the Paris summit, 1960

- By 1959–60 relations had improved again: Khrushchev visited the USA in 1959 and a **summit in Paris** was planned for **May 1960** to discuss Berlin and arms control.
- The USA had been flying high-altitude **U2 spy planes** over the USSR to photograph military sites.
- On **1 May 1960** a U2 piloted by **Francis Gary Powers** was **shot down** over the USSR. The USA claimed it was a weather research plane that had strayed off course. Khrushchev then revealed that Powers was **alive** and had confessed, and that the plane's spy equipment had been recovered. The Americans were caught lying.
- At the **Paris summit** Khrushchev demanded an **apology**. President **Eisenhower** suspended the flights but **refused to apologise**. Khrushchev **walked out** and the summit **collapsed**; his invitation to Eisenhower to visit the USSR was withdrawn.
- **Effects:** the Thaw ended in bitterness; no progress was made on **Berlin** or **arms control**; Khrushchev, under pressure from hardliners, took a tougher line, leading towards the Berlin crisis of 1961. Powers was imprisoned and later exchanged for a Soviet spy (1962).

::: terms
- **People's Republic of China**: the communist state proclaimed by Mao Zedong in October 1949.
- **38th parallel**: the line of latitude dividing North and South Korea after 1945.
- **Inchon landing**: the September 1950 UN landing behind North Korean lines that turned the Korean War.
- **Armistice**: an agreement to stop fighting; the Korean armistice was signed in July 1953.
- **Domino theory**: the US belief that if one country fell to communism its neighbours would follow.
- **Vietcong**: the communist guerrillas in South Vietnam, organised as the National Liberation Front from 1960.
- **Arms race**: the competition between the USA and the USSR to build more and better nuclear weapons.
- **ICBM**: intercontinental ballistic missile, a rocket able to carry a nuclear warhead between continents; first tested by the USSR in 1957.
- **Polaris**: the US nuclear missile launched from a submerged submarine, first successfully tested in 1960.
- **Deterrence**: preventing an attack by making sure the enemy knows it will face devastating retaliation.
- **NATO**: the Western military alliance founded in April 1949, based on collective defence.
- **Warsaw Pact**: the military alliance of the USSR and its Eastern European satellites, founded in May 1955.
- **Sputnik**: the first artificial satellite, launched by the USSR in October 1957.
- **The Thaw**: the easing of Cold War tension after Stalin's death in 1953, under Khrushchev's peaceful coexistence.
- **U2 crisis**: the shooting down of a US spy plane over the USSR in May 1960, which wrecked the Paris summit.
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of how the Korean War increased tension between the superpowers. (8)

After 1945 Korea was divided at the **38th parallel** into a Soviet-backed North and an American-backed South. **Because** each government claimed the whole country, conflict was likely, and in **June 1950** North Korea invaded with Stalin's approval and Soviet weapons.

The USA saw this as communist aggression following the "loss" of China in 1949, **so** it took the issue to the **UN Security Council**. **Because** the USSR was boycotting the Council over China's seat, it could not veto a resolution to send UN forces. **This meant** the war became a direct confrontation between the West and communism under the UN flag.

MacArthur's landing at **Inchon** in September 1950 drove the North back, and UN forces crossed the 38th parallel towards the Chinese border. **This provoked China** to send huge numbers of troops, which pushed the UN forces back south. The war now pitted the USA against communist China, and MacArthur's call to attack China, perhaps with atomic weapons, raised fears of a third world war until Truman sacked him in 1951.

**The consequences** lasted long after the armistice of July 1953. The USA massively increased its defence spending, extended containment across Asia through SEATO and the defence of Taiwan, and became locked in hostility with China. The Cold War had become global and militarised. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Two sources (12)
Source B, written for this practice question, is a description of a Soviet poster from 1961 showing Yuri Gagarin in his helmet, smiling, with a rocket rising behind him and the caption "Glory to the Soviet people, conquerors of space!" Source C, also written for practice, is in the style of an article in an American news magazine from July 1969, describing the Apollo 11 landing as proof that a free society can achieve anything it sets its mind to. How useful are Sources B and C to an historian studying the space race? (12)

**Source B** shows how the USSR used **Gagarin's flight** in April 1961, the first person in space, as **propaganda**: success in space was presented as proof of the superiority of the Soviet system and Soviet people. Context supports this: the USSR had already launched Sputnik (1957) and led the early space race, which alarmed the USA. As an official Soviet poster, it is one-sided, but this makes it **useful** for showing how the space race was a battle for prestige.

**Source C** shows the **American perspective** at the end of the race. Context: Kennedy had set the Moon goal in 1961 in response to Gagarin, and in July 1969 Armstrong and Aldrin landed. As an American magazine at the moment of triumph, it presents the landing as a victory for freedom, which shows that the USA, too, saw the race in ideological terms.

**Judgement.** Together the sources are very useful because they cover both the Soviet lead and the American victory, and show that both sides treated space as a contest of political systems, not just science. Neither mentions the military side, ICBMs and Polaris, so the historian would need other evidence for that. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Essay (16)
"The arms race was the main cause of tension between the USA and the USSR between 1949 and 1960." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. (16)

**The arms race.** The Soviet atomic test in 1949 ended the American monopoly; each side then built hydrogen bombs (1952–53), bombers and, from 1957, ICBMs. Fear of surprise attack and of a missile gap drove constant suspicion, and Polaris (1960) made nuclear war even harder to win. The arms race made every crisis potentially catastrophic.

**Events in Asia.** But the sharpest crises came from Asia. Mao's victory (1949) convinced the USA that communism was spreading, and the Korean War (1950–53) brought American and Chinese troops into direct combat. These events, more than weapons, turned containment into a global military policy.

**Alliances.** NATO (1949) and the Warsaw Pact (1955) divided Europe into armed camps; West Germany's entry into NATO directly provoked the Warsaw Pact.

**Hungary and the U2.** In 1956 Soviet tanks crushed Nagy's government when it tried to leave the Warsaw Pact, ending hopes raised by the Thaw. In 1960 the U2 incident wrecked the Paris summit.

**Judgement.** The arms race was the main cause of tension because it lay beneath all the others: Korea's escalation was feared because of atomic weapons; the alliances were built around nuclear deterrence; the space race was really a missile race; and the U2 was spying on Soviet missiles. Other events triggered specific crises, but the arms race kept the underlying fear constant. (Level 4.)
