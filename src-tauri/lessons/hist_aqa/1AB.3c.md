## What it is

This lesson covers the third bullet of Part three, **Control**: **Goebbels, the use of propaganda and censorship**; **Nazi culture**; **repression and the police state** and the roles of **Himmler, the SS and the Gestapo**; and **opposition and resistance**, including the **White Rose** group, the **Swing Youth**, the **Edelweiss Pirates** and the **July 1944 bomb plot**.

The big idea: the Nazis held power through a mix of **persuasion** (propaganda and culture, which aimed to make Germans believe) and **fear** (the police state, which punished those who did not). Most Germans neither resisted nor fully believed; they conformed. Resistance existed but was small, divided and crushed.

### 1. Goebbels and propaganda

**Joseph Goebbels** became Minister of **Public Enlightenment and Propaganda** in March 1933. His aim was to make every German think as the Nazis wanted, using simple messages repeated constantly.

| Method | How it worked |
|---|---|
| **Rallies** | Huge annual **Nuremberg rallies**: marching columns, searchlights, flags and Hitler's speeches gave a sense of power, unity and belonging |
| **Radio** | Cheap radios, the **People's Receiver**, were mass-produced so most homes could own one; their limited range made foreign stations hard to pick up. Loudspeakers in streets and workplaces broadcast speeches |
| **Film** | Every film was checked; newsreels showed Nazi achievements before every feature; films such as *Triumph of the Will* (1935), about the 1934 Nuremberg rally, glorified Hitler; antisemitic films spread hatred |
| **Newspapers** | Opposition papers were closed; editors had to be approved and follow daily instructions from the ministry |
| **Posters and the Hitler myth** | Hitler shown as a strong, wise father of the nation; posters blamed Jews and communists |
| **The 1936 Berlin Olympics** | A showcase of an efficient, modern, welcoming Germany; antisemitic signs were taken down for the visitors; Germany won the most medals, though the Black American athlete **Jesse Owens** won four gold medals, undermining Nazi racial claims |

### 2. Censorship and Nazi culture

- **The Reich Chamber of Culture (1933)**, under Goebbels: writers, artists, musicians, actors and journalists had to be members to work. Jews and opponents were excluded.
- **Book burning (10 May 1933)**: students and SA men burned thousands of books by Jewish, communist and other "un-German" authors in Berlin and other cities.
- **Art**: modern art was condemned as "degenerate"; the 1937 **Degenerate Art exhibition** in Munich mocked it. Approved art showed heroic soldiers, strong workers, peasant families and Aryan beauty.
- **Music**: jazz was condemned as "Black" and "Jewish"; music by Jewish composers was banned; German composers such as Wagner and Beethoven were promoted.
- **Architecture**: huge classical buildings, designed by **Albert Speer**, to show Nazi power.
- **Literature and film**: only approved works; many writers and film-makers left Germany.

**Effect:** censorship meant Germans heard only one message. Propaganda worked best where it built on existing beliefs (nationalism, resentment of Versailles, anti-communism) and on real successes such as falling unemployment; it was less effective at changing deeply held loyalties, such as religion.

### 3. The police state

| Organisation | Role |
|---|---|
| **The SS** (Schutzstaffel) | Led by **Heinrich Himmler** (Reichsführer-SS). Began as Hitler's bodyguard; after the Night of the Long Knives (1934) it became the core of the police state, running the concentration camps and later the death camps. Members were chosen for racial "purity" and loyalty |
| **The SD** | The SS security service under **Reinhard Heydrich**, gathering intelligence on opponents |
| **The Gestapo** | The secret state police. Could arrest, question and imprison people without trial; used torture. Relatively few officers for the whole country, so it relied heavily on **informers** and denunciations by ordinary Germans |
| **Block wardens** | Local Nazi officials who watched a block of homes and reported grumbling or suspicious behaviour |
| **Concentration camps** | The first, **Dachau**, opened in March 1933. Political opponents, then Jews, Roma, gay men, Jehovah's Witnesses and the "asocial", were held without trial in brutal conditions |
| **The courts** | Judges had to support Nazi principles; new **Special Courts** and the **People's Court** (from 1934) tried political crimes, often with no real defence; the number of offences carrying the death penalty rose sharply |

The aim was to make people **afraid** to oppose. Because the Gestapo depended on tip-offs, the police state worked partly through Germans watching one another.

### 4. Opposition and resistance

| Group | Who and when | What they did | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Edelweiss Pirates** | Working-class teenagers, mainly in western cities such as Cologne, from the late 1930s | Avoided the Hitler Youth, hiked and sang banned songs; some attacked Hitler Youth patrols; in the war some sheltered deserters and escaped prisoners and spread anti-Nazi leaflets | Many arrested; in **1944** a number of members were **publicly hanged in Cologne** |
| **Swing Youth** | Mostly middle-class teenagers, e.g. in Hamburg | Listened and danced to banned American jazz and swing, dressed in foreign fashions, mocked the Hitler Youth's values | Mostly cultural rebellion rather than politics; in **1942** Himmler ordered some leaders sent to concentration camps |
| **The White Rose** | Students at **Munich University**, led by **Hans and Sophie Scholl**, with a professor, 1942–43 | Printed and distributed **leaflets** condemning Nazi crimes, including the murder of Jews, and calling for resistance | Caught distributing leaflets in **February 1943**; tried by the People's Court and **executed**, along with other members |
| **The July 1944 bomb plot** | Army officers and conservatives, led by **Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg** | On **20 July 1944** Stauffenberg left a bomb in a briefcase at a meeting at Hitler's headquarters in East Prussia; the plotters planned to seize control in Berlin and make peace | The bomb exploded but **Hitler survived**; the coup collapsed; Stauffenberg was shot that night, and thousands were arrested and many executed |

**Other opposition:** church leaders such as **Niemöller** and **Bishop von Galen**; underground communist and socialist groups, many broken by the Gestapo in the 1930s; grumbling and non-cooperation, such as listening to foreign radio.

**Why opposition was limited:** the terror of the police state; the popularity of Hitler and his early successes; propaganda and censorship; the destruction of parties and unions, so there was no organisation to resist through; divisions among opponents; and patriotism in wartime, when resisting looked like betraying soldiers at the front.

::: terms
- **Propaganda**: spreading ideas and information, often one-sided or false, to influence what people think.
- **Censorship**: controlling what people can read, see and hear by banning material.
- **Ministry of Propaganda**: Goebbels' ministry, set up in March 1933 to control the media and culture.
- **People's Receiver**: the cheap radio set produced so that most German homes could hear Nazi broadcasts.
- **Reich Chamber of Culture**: the body that all writers, artists, musicians and journalists had to join to work.
- **Degenerate art**: the Nazi label for modern art they condemned, mocked in an exhibition in Munich in 1937.
- **SS**: the Schutzstaffel led by Himmler, which ran the police state and the camps.
- **Gestapo**: the Nazi secret state police, who could arrest and imprison without trial.
- **Concentration camp**: a prison camp where people were held without trial; the first was Dachau, opened in 1933.
- **People's Court**: the court set up in 1934 to try political crimes, which tried and condemned the White Rose.
- **White Rose**: the Munich student group led by Hans and Sophie Scholl that distributed anti-Nazi leaflets in 1942–43.
- **Edelweiss Pirates**: groups of working-class young people who rejected the Hitler Youth and some of whom actively resisted.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 Section A at a glance.** About an hour, 40 marks: Q1 differ (4), Q2 why differ (4), Q3 more convincing (8), Q4 describe two (4), Q5 in what ways (8), Q6 which bullet (12). Typical questions: interpretations on how far Germans supported the regime; "describe two methods the Gestapo used"; "in what ways did Goebbels control what people saw and heard"; "propaganda or terror: which was more important?".

1. **Q1 (4): content only.** E.g. one view says Germans were terrorised into obedience, the other that most supported Hitler willingly.
2. **Q2 (4): provenance + context.** A Gestapo officer at a post-war trial would play down terror; a former prisoner of Dachau would stress it; a historian using Gestapo files might find most cases came from denunciations.
3. **Q3 (8): test both.** Use the Gestapo's reliance on informers, Dachau, the People's Court, the People's Receiver, the Olympics, Hitler's popularity, and the small scale of resistance.
4. **Q4 (4): two features with precise detail.**
5. **Q5 (8): Level 4 explains how methods differed or worked together** (propaganda for the majority, terror for the minority; changes in wartime).
6. **Q6 (12): explain both bullets, then judge with a criterion,** e.g. "terror controlled actions; propaganda controlled minds".
:::

::: example Q1 How interpretations differ (4)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a man held in Dachau in 1933–34, writing in 1946: "Every German knew what happened to those who spoke out. The knock on the door at night, the camp, the silence afterwards. Fear kept the people quiet." Interpretation B, also written for practice, is in the style of a historian writing in 2015: "The Gestapo was small and overstretched. It depended on ordinary Germans to report their neighbours, and most cases began that way. The regime rested less on terror than on the willingness of millions to go along with it." How does Interpretation B differ from Interpretation A about how the Nazis controlled Germans? (4)

A says Germans were controlled by **fear** of arrest and the camps (1–2). B says control rested mainly on **consent and cooperation**: the Gestapo was small and relied on ordinary Germans informing (+1). So A sees a population held down by terror, B a population that largely went along with the regime (+1). Level 2, 4/4.
:::

::: example Q3 Which is more convincing (8)
Using Interpretations A and B above: which interpretation gives the more convincing opinion about how the Nazis controlled Germans? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretations A and B. (8)

**A is convincing about terror.** Dachau opened in March 1933 and political opponents were held there without trial. The Gestapo used torture; the People's Court and Special Courts gave harsh sentences; the White Rose students were executed in 1943 and Edelweiss Pirates hanged in 1944. People knew this, so fear discouraged resistance.

**B is convincing about consent.** The Gestapo had relatively few officers and relied on denunciations and block wardens. Goebbels' propaganda (rallies, the People's Receiver, the 1936 Olympics) and real successes like falling unemployment made Hitler genuinely popular. Resistance was small.

**Judgement.** B is more convincing for the majority: the system worked because many Germans cooperated, and terror alone could not have watched a whole country. But A is right that terror was always there for those who stepped out of line, and it was the reason why dislike rarely became action. Consent and terror worked together. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Describe two (4)
Describe two methods the Gestapo used to control Germans. (4)

**Arrest without trial.** The Gestapo could arrest people and hold them indefinitely (1), sending them to concentration camps such as Dachau without any court hearing (1).

**Informers.** The Gestapo had relatively few officers, so it relied on tip-offs from ordinary Germans and block wardens (1). People denounced neighbours and colleagues for grumbling or listening to foreign radio, so people were afraid to speak freely (1).
:::

::: example Q6 Which was more important (12)
Which of the following was more important in controlling the German people: propaganda; terror? Explain your answer with reference to both bullet points. (12)

**Propaganda.** Goebbels controlled every source of information: newspapers followed ministry instructions, the cheap People's Receiver brought Hitler's speeches into most homes, newsreels preceded every film, and the Nuremberg rallies and 1936 Olympics created a sense of national rebirth. The Reich Chamber of Culture and the book burnings of May 1933 removed alternative views.

**Terror.** Himmler's SS, the Gestapo and the SD could arrest anyone; Dachau opened in March 1933; the People's Court and Special Courts dealt with political crimes; block wardens and informers watched every street. Opponents such as the White Rose were executed.

**Judgement.** Propaganda was more important for most Germans, because it built on real successes and existing beliefs, and so persuaded or reassured millions who never met the Gestapo. Terror was decisive for the minority who might resist: communists, socialists, church critics. The two depended on each other: propaganda made Germans willing informers, and informers made terror effective. (Level 4.)
:::

::: q
Give two ways Goebbels used the radio. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): cheap People's Receivers mass-produced so most homes had one; limited range made foreign stations harder to receive; loudspeakers in streets, cafés and workplaces; Hitler's speeches broadcast; listening to foreign stations made a crime in wartime.

::: q
Why were the 1936 Berlin Olympics useful propaganda? (3)
---
They showed the world a modern, efficient and peaceful Germany (1); antisemitic signs were removed for visitors to hide persecution (1); Germany won the most medals, used to suggest Aryan superiority (1). Credit: Jesse Owens' four gold medals undermined the message.
:::

::: q
What was the role of Heinrich Himmler? (2)
---
Leader (Reichsführer) of the SS (1), and from 1936 chief of all German police, including the Gestapo; ran the concentration camps and the police state (1).
:::

::: q
Describe what the White Rose group did. (3)
---
Munich University students led by Hans and Sophie Scholl (1) printed and distributed leaflets condemning Nazi crimes and calling for resistance in 1942–43 (1); caught in February 1943, they were tried by the People's Court and executed (1).
:::

::: q
How did the Swing Youth differ from the Edelweiss Pirates? (4)
---
Swing Youth were mostly middle-class (1) and rebelled through culture: American jazz and swing, fashions, mocking Nazi values (1). Edelweiss Pirates were mostly working-class (1) and some resisted actively, attacking Hitler Youth patrols, sheltering deserters and spreading anti-Nazi leaflets (1).
:::

::: q
Why did the July 1944 bomb plot fail? (3)
---
The briefcase bomb was moved, so a heavy table shielded Hitler and he survived (1); the plotters in Berlin hesitated and could not take control once it was known he was alive (1); loyal troops and the SS crushed the coup that night, and Stauffenberg was shot (1).
:::

::: watch
- **Treating propaganda and terror as separate.** Top answers show how they worked together: propaganda produced informers; terror silenced doubters.
- **Overstating the Gestapo's size.** It was small and relied on denunciations; this is a common Level 4 insight.
- **Mixing up the youth groups.** Edelweiss Pirates (working-class, some active resistance, hanged in Cologne 1944); Swing Youth (middle-class, jazz); White Rose (students, leaflets, executed 1943).
- **Exaggerating resistance.** Judge it by scale and results: most Germans conformed; the most serious attempt came from army officers in July 1944.
- **Describing culture with no purpose.** Explain why the Nazis controlled art, music and books: to remove alternative ideas and spread their own.
:::
