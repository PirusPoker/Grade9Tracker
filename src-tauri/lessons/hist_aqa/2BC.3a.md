## What it is

This lesson covers the first two bullets of **Part three: Troubles at home and abroad**. **Religious matters**: the question of religion; English **Catholicism** and **Protestantism**; the **Northern Rebellion**; Elizabeth's **excommunication**; the **missionaries**; **Catholic plots** and the threat to the Elizabethan settlement; the **Puritans**; and the government's responses. **Mary, Queen of Scots**: her background; how Elizabeth and Parliament treated her; the challenge she posed; the plots; her **execution** and its impact.

The big idea: Elizabeth wanted a **broad, moderate** Protestant Church that most people could accept without too much questioning. From about **1568** that settlement was squeezed from both sides: by **Catholics** (and a Catholic heir, Mary, now on English soil) and by **Puritans** who wanted further reform. Government policy hardened as the threat grew. Top answers explain **why** the threat grew, judge **how serious** it was, and link Mary's execution to the war with Spain.

### 1. The religious settlement in outline

In **1559** Parliament passed the **Act of Supremacy** (Elizabeth became **Supreme Governor** of the Church of England) and the **Act of Uniformity** (a new **Book of Common Prayer**; everyone had to attend their parish church on Sundays, with a small **fine** for those who did not). Royal **Injunctions** set out how churches should be run.

The settlement was a **compromise** (sometimes called the "middle way"):
- **Protestant** in doctrine: services in English, a Protestant prayer book, clergy allowed to marry, the Pope's authority rejected.
- Some **Catholic-looking** features kept: bishops, **vestments** (special clothes for clergy), some ceremonies and church decoration, and wording on communion that both sides could accept.
- Elizabeth did not want to "make windows into men's souls": she cared most about **outward obedience**.

### 2. English Catholicism

- In 1568 many people, especially in the **north** and among older nobles and gentry, were still **Catholic** at heart. Many conformed outwardly ("church papists") and attended services.
- **Recusants** refused to attend the Church of England and paid the fines.
- At first the government was fairly **tolerant** as long as Catholics obeyed. Things changed after 1568, when **Mary, Queen of Scots** arrived and gave Catholics a possible alternative queen.

### 3. The Northern Rebellion, 1569

**Causes**
- **Religion**: the northern earls, the **Earl of Northumberland** (Thomas Percy) and the **Earl of Westmorland** (Charles Neville), were Catholic and wanted to restore Catholicism.
- **Power and resentment**: they had lost influence at court and in the north to new men and royal officials; they resented **Cecil**.
- **Mary**: a plan to marry Mary to the **Duke of Norfolk**, England's leading noble, and have her recognised as heir; when Elizabeth found out, Norfolk was arrested and the earls, summoned to court, rebelled rather than face her.

**Events (November–December 1569)**: the rebels took **Durham** and celebrated **Catholic Mass** in the cathedral, then marched south hoping to free Mary, who was moved out of reach. Expected Spanish help never came and few nobles joined; as a royal army approached, the rebels **retreated**, their army broke up and the earls fled to Scotland. A further rising in early 1570 (Leonard Dacre) was defeated.

**Consequences**
- **Hundreds** of rebels were **executed** to set an example; Northumberland was later handed back and executed (1572); Westmorland fled abroad.
- The **Council of the North** was strengthened under loyal Protestants.
- It showed that Catholicism and Mary could combine into a **real threat**, but also that most of England, even most Catholics, stayed **loyal**.

### 4. The papal bull of excommunication, 1570

- In **1570** Pope **Pius V** issued a **papal bull** (Regnans in Excelsis) **excommunicating** Elizabeth: it declared her a heretic and **released Catholics from obedience** to her, even encouraging them to overthrow her.
- It came too late to help the Northern Rebellion, but it changed things: Catholics now seemed forced to choose between **Pope and queen**, so the government began to treat Catholicism as potential **treason**.
- **Response**: the **Treason Act of 1571** made it treason to say Elizabeth was not the rightful queen or a heretic, and illegal to bring papal bulls into England.

### 5. The missionaries

- **William Allen** founded a **seminary** at **Douai** (Spanish Netherlands) in **1568**. Its **seminary priests** reached England from **1574**, saying Mass secretly in gentry houses and hiding in **priest holes**.
- **Jesuits** arrived from **1580**, led by **Edmund Campion** and **Robert Parsons**. Campion was captured, tortured and **executed** in **1581**.
- **Responses**: the **1581 Act** raised recusancy fines to **£20 a month** and made converting anyone to Catholicism **treason**; the **1585 Act** ordered Jesuits and seminary priests to leave within 40 days or be treated as **traitors**, and made sheltering them a capital crime. Well over a hundred priests were executed in the reign.

### 6. The Catholic plots

| Plot | Date | Key people | Aim | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ridolfi Plot** | 1571 | **Roberto Ridolfi** (a Florentine banker), the **Duke of Norfolk**, Mary | Spanish invasion from the Netherlands, rising of English Catholics, Mary to marry Norfolk and replace Elizabeth | Uncovered by Cecil's agents; Norfolk **executed** (1572); Spanish ambassador expelled; Parliament demanded Mary's execution, but Elizabeth refused |
| **Throckmorton Plot** | 1583 | **Francis Throckmorton**, the Spanish ambassador **Mendoza**, the French Catholic Duke of **Guise** | French Catholic invasion backed by Spain, English rising, Mary to be queen | Uncovered by **Walsingham**; Throckmorton executed; Mendoza **expelled**; Catholic suspects watched; **Bond of Association** (1584) |
| **Babington Plot** | 1586 | **Anthony Babington**, Catholic gentlemen, Mary | Murder Elizabeth, foreign invasion, free Mary and make her queen | Walsingham read Mary's coded letters (smuggled in **beer barrels** via a double agent); Mary's reply approved the plan; plotters executed; Mary **tried** |

**Walsingham's spy network** used agents, double agents, intercepted letters and code-breakers (notably **Thomas Phelippes**). It was crucial in exposing the plots and, in 1586, in getting written evidence against Mary.

**How serious was the Catholic threat?**
- **Serious**: the bull of 1570 gave Catholics papal approval to rebel; Mary was a real alternative; Spain and France were involved; plots aimed at **assassination** (William of Orange, a Protestant leader in the Netherlands, was assassinated in 1584).
- **Limited**: the plots were all **discovered**; most English Catholics stayed **loyal** (many fought against the Armada); seminary priests mostly sought to keep the faith alive, not to rebel; foreign help never arrived in time.

### 7. The Puritans

**Who they were.** Strict Protestants, many influenced by Calvin's Geneva, who thought the settlement had not gone far enough. They wanted to "purify" the Church of all remaining Catholic practices.

**Beliefs and demands**
- Simple churches and services; preaching and Bible study at the centre.
- **No vestments**: the "**vestments controversy**" of the 1560s, when some clergy refused to wear the surplice. Archbishop **Parker** enforced the rules and some ministers lost their posts.
- **Prophesyings**: meetings where clergy studied and preached on the Bible together, often with lay people listening.
- Some, the **Presbyterians** (led by figures such as Thomas Cartwright), wanted to **abolish bishops** and have the Church run by elected elders; a small number of **Separatists** wanted to leave the Church of England altogether.
- They opposed the theatre, gambling and Sunday sports.

**Why they were a challenge**: many were influential: MPs, gentry, some Privy Councillors (Leicester and Walsingham were sympathetic) and some bishops. They challenged the **royal supremacy** and the settlement Elizabeth had made, through **Parliament** and preaching.

**Responses**
- **Archbishop Grindal** (from 1575) sympathised with prophesyings and **refused** Elizabeth's order to suppress them (1576); she **suspended** him and ordered the bishops to stop them herself.
- **Archbishop Whitgift** (from **1583**) cracked down: clergy had to accept articles supporting the Prayer Book and bishops or lose their posts, enforced by the Court of High Commission.
- Puritan bills in Parliament (e.g. to replace the Prayer Book in the 1580s) were blocked.
- The anonymous **Marprelate Tracts** (1588–89) mocking bishops brought more repression; an Act of **1593** targeted Separatists, and some leaders were executed.

By the 1590s Puritanism had been **contained**: still strong in many parishes, but no longer able to change the Church through Parliament.

### 8. Mary, Queen of Scots

**Background**
- Born in **1542**, she became Queen of Scotland as a baby. She was brought up in **France** and married the French heir, briefly becoming Queen of France.
- A **Catholic**, and the granddaughter of Henry VIII's sister Margaret: next in line to the English throne by blood, and to many Catholics the **rightful** queen (they saw Elizabeth as illegitimate).
- Returning to Scotland in 1561, she married **Lord Darnley** (their son was **James**); Darnley was murdered in **1567**, and Mary soon married **Bothwell**, widely suspected of the murder. Scottish lords rebelled; Mary was imprisoned and forced to **abdicate** for her baby son, **James VI**.
- She escaped, was defeated, and fled to **England** in **May 1568**, asking Elizabeth for help.

**Elizabeth's dilemma**: helping Mary regain Scotland would anger the Protestant Scottish lords, England's allies; letting her go to France or Spain would hand Catholic powers a weapon; executing an anointed queen set a dangerous precedent. So Elizabeth **kept her in England**, where she became a **focus for plots**. An inquiry at York (1568–69) examined the "**Casket Letters**", which seemed to link Mary to Darnley's murder, but reached no verdict, which suited Elizabeth.

**Captivity (1568–1587)**: held in a series of houses and castles, mostly in the Midlands and north, such as **Tutbury**, **Sheffield** (in the care of the **Earl of Shrewsbury**), **Chartley** and finally **Fotheringhay**. She lived with a household and some comfort, but under watch, which tightened over time.

**Parliament's pressure**: after the **Ridolfi Plot** (1571–72), Parliament demanded her execution; Elizabeth refused. After the Throckmorton Plot, the **Bond of Association** (1584) and the **Act for the Queen's Safety** (1585) meant anyone who benefited from a plot against Elizabeth could be tried and executed.

**Trial and execution**
- After the **Babington Plot** (1586), Mary was **tried** at **Fotheringhay** in **October 1586** and found guilty.
- Elizabeth delayed for months, then signed the death warrant; her councillors sent it before she could change her mind. Mary was **executed** at Fotheringhay on **8 February 1587**.
- Elizabeth claimed she had not meant the warrant to be sent, blamed and punished her secretary **William Davison**, and wrote to James VI to express her grief.

**Impact**
- **At home**: the Catholic **heir** was gone; English Catholics lost their obvious alternative queen, so plots based on her ended. The succession now pointed to Protestant **James VI**.
- **Scotland**: James protested, but did not break his alliance with England; he had his English inheritance to protect.
- **Abroad**: Mary had left her claim to **Philip II** of Spain, and her execution gave him a **reason and a Catholic cause** for invading England. Together with Drake's raids and English help to the Dutch, it led directly towards the **Armada** of 1588 (see 2BC.3b).

::: terms
- **Supreme Governor**: Elizabeth's title as head of the Church of England under the 1559 Act of Supremacy.
- **Recusant**: a Catholic who refused to attend Church of England services and paid fines.
- **Papal bull**: a formal written order from the Pope, such as the 1570 bull excommunicating Elizabeth.
- **Excommunication**: expulsion from the Catholic Church; the 1570 bull also released Catholics from obedience to Elizabeth.
- **Seminary priest**: a Catholic priest trained abroad, for example at Douai, and sent secretly to England.
- **Jesuit**: a member of the Society of Jesus, a strict Catholic order loyal to the Pope, active in England from 1580.
- **Priest hole**: a secret hiding place for priests in a Catholic gentry house.
- **Puritan**: a strict Protestant who wanted to purify the Church of remaining Catholic practices.
- **Vestments controversy**: the 1560s dispute over Puritan clergy refusing to wear the surplice.
- **Prophesyings**: meetings of clergy to study and preach on the Bible, suppressed by Elizabeth.
- **Bond of Association**: a 1584 pledge by loyal gentlemen to kill anyone who benefited from a plot to murder Elizabeth.
:::

1. **Q1 (8).** Interpretations here usually judge the Catholic threat, Mary or the Puritans. Weigh plots and foreign backing against discovery and the loyalty of most Catholics; end with how convincing the claim is.
2. **Q2 (8).** "What was important about the Northern Rebellion?" Explain short- and longer-term consequences, especially how policy hardened.
3. **Q3 (8).** "Write an account of the ways in which Mary threatened Elizabeth." Move from 1568 through the plots to 1587, linking each plot to the response.
4. **Q4 (16).** Link to **Kenilworth**: Dudley as Protestant leader and patron of Puritans; the castle as a stage for the queen's image; weigh it against the wider threat.

::: example Q1 Interpretation (8)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a historian writing on Elizabethan religion in 2009. It says: "The Catholic threat was more feared than real. Every plot was uncovered, no foreign army landed, and the great mass of English Catholics wanted only to be left in peace." How convincing is Interpretation A about the Catholic threat to Elizabeth? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretation A. (8)

The interpretation is convincing that the plots **failed**. Walsingham's network uncovered the **Ridolfi** (1571), **Throckmorton** (1583) and **Babington** (1586) plots, and promised Spanish help never arrived. Most English Catholics stayed **loyal**: many conformed as church papists, and the seminary priests mostly aimed to keep the faith alive rather than to rebel.

But "more feared than real" understates the danger. The **Northern Rebellion** of 1569 put thousands in arms and Mass was said in Durham Cathedral. The **papal bull** of 1570 released Catholics from obedience to Elizabeth. The plots involved **Spain**, the **Duke of Guise** and Mary herself, and aimed at **assassination**: William of Orange was murdered in 1584, so the danger was not imaginary.

So the interpretation is convincing about the outcome and about ordinary Catholics, but less so about the threat: it was real, and it failed largely because the government took it seriously. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Explain what was important (8)
Explain what was important about the Northern Rebellion. (8)

The Northern Rebellion of 1569 was important first because it showed that **religion and Mary could combine** into a real threat. The Catholic earls of Northumberland and Westmorland heard Mass in **Durham Cathedral** and aimed to free Mary and marry her to the Duke of Norfolk.

Second, it was important for **how the government responded**. The rebels retreated when royal forces approached, and Elizabeth ordered **hundreds** of executions, showing she would be ruthless. The **Council of the North** was strengthened under loyal Protestants, breaking the power of the old northern families.

Third, it **hardened policy** towards Catholics. It led to the **papal bull** of 1570, and the government responded with the **Treason Act of 1571**. From now on Catholicism looked like potential treason. Yet it also showed most nobles stayed loyal, which suggests the threat was containable. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of the ways in which Mary, Queen of Scots threatened Elizabeth. (8)

When Mary fled to England in **1568**, she became a problem at once: as a **Catholic** and heir by blood, she was, to many Catholics, the rightful queen. Elizabeth could not safely free, help or execute her, so she kept her captive, but this made her a **focus for plots**.

In **1569** the **Northern Rebellion** aimed to free her and marry her to Norfolk. In **1571** the **Ridolfi Plot** planned a Spanish invasion to put her on the throne; Norfolk was executed and Parliament demanded Mary's death. In **1583** the **Throckmorton Plot** linked her to the Duke of Guise and Spain, which led to the **Bond of Association** and the **Act for the Queen's Safety**.

Finally, in **1586**, Walsingham intercepted letters in which Mary approved the **Babington Plot** to murder Elizabeth. This gave the evidence for her **trial** and **execution** in February 1587, but her death gave **Philip II** a reason to invade. So Mary was a threat alive and, in a different way, dead. (Level 4.)
:::
