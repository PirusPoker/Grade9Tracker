## What it is

This lesson covers all of Part one, **Peacemaking**: the **armistice** and the **aims of the peacemakers** (Wilson and the Fourteen Points, Clemenceau and Lloyd George) and how far they achieved them; the **Versailles settlement** (Diktat, territorial changes, military restrictions, war guilt and reparations); and the **impact of the treaty and the wider settlement** (Allied reactions, German objections, and the strengths and weaknesses of the settlement, including the problems of the **new states**).

The big idea: in 1919 three leaders with very different aims had to make peace after a war that had killed millions and destroyed four empires. The result, the Treaty of Versailles, was a **compromise** that satisfied nobody completely. Germans hated it, the French thought it too weak, and the Americans refused to ratify it. Its weaknesses gave later German leaders a powerful grievance.

### 1. The armistice and the problems facing the peacemakers

The fighting stopped with the **armistice of 11 November 1918**. Germany had not been invaded, and many Germans did not believe their army had been beaten. The **Paris Peace Conference** opened in January 1919. The defeated powers were **not invited** to negotiate, and Russia, now under the Bolsheviks, was not represented.

The peacemakers faced huge problems:
- **Conflicting aims** among the victors, especially between France and the USA.
- **Public opinion**: voters in Britain and France wanted Germany punished and made to pay.
- **Devastation**: north-eastern France and Belgium were wrecked; millions were dead or wounded.
- **The collapse of empires**: the Russian, German, Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires had fallen, leaving borders to be drawn across mixed populations.
- **Fear of communism**: revolution had spread from Russia, with uprisings in Germany and Hungary.
- **Promises made in wartime**, for example secret treaties offering territory to Italy, clashed with Wilson's idea of **self-determination**.
- **Time and pressure**: decisions had to be made quickly while Europe was hungry and unstable.

### 2. The Big Three and their aims

| | **Woodrow Wilson** (USA) | **Georges Clemenceau** (France) | **David Lloyd George** (Britain) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Situation | The USA joined the war in 1917 and suffered relatively light losses; its land was untouched | France lost well over a million soldiers; its north-east was devastated; it had been invaded twice by Germany within fifty years (1870 and 1914) | Britain had heavy losses but no fighting on its soil; it had just held an election in which many voters wanted Germany punished |
| Main aims | A fair, lasting peace based on his **Fourteen Points** (January 1918), so Germany would not seek revenge | **Security** for France and **revenge**: a Germany too weak ever to attack again; reparations to rebuild France | A compromise: satisfy voters by **punishing** Germany, but let it **recover** as a trading partner and barrier to communism; protect Britain's **navy** and **empire** |
| Specific wishes | Self-determination; disarmament for all; freedom of the seas; free trade; open diplomacy; a **League of Nations** | Return of **Alsace-Lorraine**; a weak German army; the Rhineland separated from Germany or at least demilitarised; heavy reparations | German navy and colonies reduced; avoid a peace so harsh it led to another war |

**Key Fourteen Points to know:** no secret treaties; freedom of navigation on the seas; free trade; disarmament by all nations; self-determination for the peoples of Eastern Europe; an independent Poland with access to the sea; a League of Nations to settle disputes.

### 3. The Treaty of Versailles, 28 June 1919

Signed in the **Hall of Mirrors** at Versailles, five years to the day after the assassination at Sarajevo.

**Territory**
- **Alsace-Lorraine** returned to France.
- **West Prussia and Posen** went to the new Poland, creating the **Polish Corridor**, which split East Prussia from the rest of Germany.
- **Danzig**, a German-speaking port, became a **free city** run by the League of Nations.
- The **Saar**, with its coalfields, was run by the League for 15 years; France could take its coal. A vote would then decide its future.
- Smaller losses: Eupen and Malmédy to Belgium, North Schleswig to Denmark (after a vote), Memel; part of Upper Silesia after a vote.
- **Anschluss** (union) with Austria was **forbidden**.
- All Germany's **colonies** were taken and became **mandates**, run mainly by Britain and France for the League.
- Overall Germany lost about **13 per cent** of its European territory and millions of people.

**Military restrictions**
- Army limited to **100,000** men; **no conscription**.
- **No tanks**, **no air force** and **no submarines**; the navy limited to **six battleships**.
- The **Rhineland** was **demilitarised**: no German troops there, and Allied troops occupied it for 15 years.

**War guilt and reparations**
- **Article 231**, the **war guilt clause**: Germany had to accept responsibility for causing the war and the loss and damage it caused.
- This justified **reparations**. The amount was not fixed in the treaty: a commission set it in **1921** at **£6,600 million**.

**The League of Nations** was set up by the treaty; Germany was **not allowed to join** at first.

### 4. How far did the Big Three get what they wanted?

| | Got | Did not get | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Clemenceau** | Alsace-Lorraine; demilitarised and occupied Rhineland; tiny German army; war guilt; reparations; the Saar's coal | The Rhineland as a separate state; a Germany broken up for good | **Mostly**, but many French people felt the treaty was too soft, and Clemenceau soon lost power |
| **Lloyd George** | German navy cut and colonies taken (many as British mandates); Germany punished, pleasing voters | A treaty moderate enough to avoid German revenge; he feared it was too harsh | **Largely** satisfied on British interests, uneasy about the future |
| **Wilson** | A League of Nations; self-determination for many East Europeans (Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, the Baltic states) | Self-determination for Germans (Anschluss banned; Germans placed in Poland and Czechoslovakia); freedom of the seas (Britain refused); disarmament for all; a fair peace | **Least**: and the **US Senate refused to ratify** the treaty, so the USA never joined his League |

### 5. Reactions to the treaty

**German objections**
- It was a **Diktat** (dictated peace): Germany was not allowed to negotiate, and was threatened with renewed war if it refused to sign.
- Germans had expected a peace based on the **Fourteen Points**; instead self-determination did not apply to them.
- **War guilt** was seen as a national humiliation: Germans believed they had fought a defensive war.
- **Reparations** were seen as impossible and designed to cripple the economy.
- Military limits left Germany weak while its neighbours remained armed.
- Losing the Corridor, colonies and industrial land hurt pride and the economy.
- In protest the German fleet interned at **Scapa Flow** was **scuttled** (sunk by its own crews) in June 1919. The Weimar politicians who signed were called the **"November criminals"**, linked to the myth that the army had been **"stabbed in the back"**.

**Allied reactions**
- **France:** many thought it **too lenient**, as Germany remained united and potentially strong.
- **Britain:** at first welcomed, but some, such as the economist **John Maynard Keynes**, argued it was **too harsh** and would ruin Europe's economy; this view grew in the 1920s and later encouraged **appeasement**.
- **USA:** the **Senate rejected** the treaty; many Americans wanted no more involvement in Europe (**isolationism**).
- **Italy** felt cheated of territory it had been promised (a "mutilated victory"); **Japan** was also dissatisfied.

### 6. The wider settlement

| Treaty | Defeated power | Key terms (outline) |
|---|---|---|
| **Saint-Germain** (1919) | Austria | Lost land to Italy, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia and Poland; Anschluss forbidden; army limited |
| **Trianon** (1920) | Hungary | Lost about two-thirds of its territory, leaving many Hungarians in neighbouring states |
| **Neuilly** (1919) | Bulgaria | Lost land to Greece and Yugoslavia, including its Aegean coastline |
| **Sèvres** (1920) | Turkey | Lost its Arab lands, which became British and French mandates; Turkish nationalists resisted and it was replaced by the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) |

**Problems of the new states**
- **Poland:** contained German and other minorities; it fought over borders with Russia and Lithuania; the Corridor was a constant German grievance.
- **Czechoslovakia:** included about **three million German speakers** in the **Sudetenland**, along with Slovaks, Hungarians and others: an ideal grievance for Hitler later.
- **Yugoslavia:** Serbs, Croats, Slovenes and others with different religions and histories, and tension between them.
- **Austria and Hungary** became small, poor states cut off from old markets.
- All were **small and weak**, sandwiched between Germany and Russia, and hard for Britain and France to defend.

**Strengths and weaknesses of the settlement**

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Created the **League of Nations** to settle disputes | Germany humiliated and resentful: a **lasting grievance** |
| Applied **self-determination** to millions in Eastern Europe | Self-determination denied to Germans, Austrians and Hungarians; minorities in every new state |
| Disarmed Germany, so it could not attack immediately | Germany kept its industry and population, so could recover; no one was ready to **enforce** the treaty |
| Many historians argue it was not as harsh as it could have been (compare Germany's own treaty with Russia at Brest-Litovsk, 1918) | The USA withdrew; Russia was excluded; Italy and Japan were dissatisfied |

::: terms
- **Armistice**: an agreement to stop fighting; the First World War armistice took effect on 11 November 1918.
- **Fourteen Points**: Wilson's January 1918 plan for a fair peace, including self-determination, disarmament and a League of Nations.
- **Self-determination**: the right of a people to govern themselves in their own nation state.
- **Diktat**: a dictated peace; the German name for Versailles because Germany was not allowed to negotiate.
- **Article 231**: the war guilt clause, making Germany accept responsibility for the war and its damage.
- **Reparations**: compensation for war damage, set for Germany at £6,600 million in 1921.
- **Demilitarised zone**: an area where no troops or weapons are allowed, such as the Rhineland after 1919.
- **Polish Corridor**: the strip of land given to Poland to reach the sea, separating East Prussia from Germany.
- **Mandate**: a former colony of a defeated power, run by a victor on behalf of the League of Nations.
- **Anschluss**: union between Germany and Austria, forbidden by the peace treaties.
- **Isolationism**: the policy of keeping the USA out of European and world affairs.
- **Ratify**: to formally approve a treaty, as the US Senate refused to do with Versailles.
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of the problems facing the peacemakers in 1919. (8)

The peacemakers began with **conflicting aims**. Clemenceau, whose country had lost over a million soldiers and seen its north-east devastated, wanted Germany crippled, while Wilson wanted a fair peace based on the Fourteen Points so that Germany would not seek revenge. **This meant that** every decision about reparations or the Rhineland became a bitter argument, and Lloyd George had to act as a go-between.

These disagreements were made worse by **public opinion**. British and French voters wanted Germany to pay, and Lloyd George had just won an election in which promises to punish Germany were popular. **As a result**, the leaders could not offer the moderate terms that Wilson, and privately Lloyd George, thought wise, which led to the war guilt clause and reparations.

At the same time the collapse of the **German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian and Ottoman empires** forced them to draw new borders. Self-determination proved impossible to apply cleanly because peoples were mixed, so new states such as Czechoslovakia and Poland **ended up** with large German minorities, and wartime promises to Italy clashed with Wilson's principles.

Finally, the **fear of communism** spreading from Russia, and revolts in Germany itself, put pressure on the peacemakers to decide quickly. **This explains why** they produced a compromise that pleased no one and stored up future problems. (Level 4: a linked account organised around causation.)
:::

::: example Q2 Two sources (12)
Source B, written for this practice question, is in the style of a statement by the German delegation in Paris in May 1919, protesting that the terms break the promise of a peace based on the Fourteen Points and will leave the German people enslaved for generations. Source C, also written for practice, is a description of a British cartoon from 1919 showing a fat German figure complaining loudly about paying a modest bill, while ruined French towns appear in the background. How useful are Sources B and C to an historian studying reactions to the Treaty of Versailles? (12)

**Source B** is useful because it shows the **official German objection**: that the treaty broke the Fourteen Points, on which Germans thought the armistice had been based. This matches context: self-determination was denied to Germans in the Corridor and Austria, and Article 231 and reparations were seen as unbearable. Its **provenance** adds to its value: it comes from the delegation itself in May 1919, after the terms were handed over and before signing, so it shows how Germany tried to win changes. Its exaggeration ("enslaved") is useful too, as it shows the depth of anger that later fed the Diktat and "stab in the back" myths.

**Source C** is useful because it shows a **British** reaction: that German complaints were self-pitying when France had suffered terribly. This fits the mood of the 1918 election in Britain, when many voters wanted Germany to pay. Its provenance as a cartoon means it aims to persuade British readers, so it is useful as evidence of popular feeling rather than of what the treaty did.

**Judgement.** Together they are very useful because they show the two sides of the argument in 1919: Germans saw a broken promise and a Diktat; many British people saw a fair price for German aggression. Neither shows the later British view, as argued by Keynes, that the treaty was too harsh, so they are most useful for reactions in 1919 itself. (Level 4.)

::: example Q4 Essay (16)
"The main reason Germans hated the Treaty of Versailles was the loss of territory." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. (16)

**Territory.** Germany lost about 13 per cent of its European land: Alsace-Lorraine, West Prussia and Posen (the Polish Corridor, which cut East Prussia off), Danzig, and all its colonies. Millions of Germans now lived under Polish or other rule, which seemed to break Wilson's promise of self-determination. Losing the Saar's coal and industrial regions hurt the economy.

**War guilt and reparations.** But Article 231 was arguably resented more, because it was a **moral** humiliation: Germans believed they had fought defensively, and the clause justified reparations, fixed at £6,600 million in 1921, which they saw as designed to cripple them.

**The Diktat.** Underlying everything was the way the treaty was imposed. Germany was excluded from negotiations and threatened with invasion if it refused to sign. Because the army had not been defeated on German soil, many believed the "stab in the back" myth and blamed the politicians who signed.

**Military terms.** The 100,000 army and demilitarised Rhineland left Germany defenceless next to armed neighbours, a blow to national pride.

**Judgement.** Territory mattered, but it was not the main reason. The war guilt clause was hated most because it turned defeat into shame and made all the other terms, including reparations and lost land, seem like punishment for a crime Germans denied. The Diktat explains why the anger was directed at the treaty as a whole. (Level 4 needs this kind of sustained, reasoned judgement.)
