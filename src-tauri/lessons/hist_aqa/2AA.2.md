## What it is

This lesson covers **Part two: The beginnings of change** (c1500 to c1800): the **Renaissance** and the work of **Vesalius, Paré and Harvey**; opposition to change; how disease was dealt with, including **quackery** and the **Great Plague of 1665**; the growth of **hospitals** and the rising status of surgeons; **John Hunter**; and the prevention of smallpox through **inoculation** and **Jenner's vaccination**.

The big idea: in this period **ideas** about the body changed far more than **treatments** did. Anatomy and physiology were transformed, but most doctors still bled and purged their patients, and nobody yet knew what caused disease. Change had begun, but it was slow and patchy.

### 1. The Renaissance: challenging authority

The **Renaissance** was a revival of interest in ancient learning that also encouraged people to **observe, question and test** for themselves. Several developments helped medicine:
- **Printing**: the printing press (reaching England in the 1470s) allowed identical copies of books and accurate **illustrations** to spread quickly and cheaply. New ideas could no longer be controlled by monks copying old texts.
- **Art**: artists studied the body closely to draw it accurately, and some worked with doctors.
- **The Reformation**: the Church lost much of its control over learning, so challenging accepted authorities such as Galen became easier.
- **Scientific societies**: the **Royal Society** (founded in the 1660s) encouraged experiment and published findings so scientists could share them.

### 2. Three individuals

| | **Andreas Vesalius** (anatomy) | **Ambroise Paré** (surgery) | **William Harvey** (physiology) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who | Professor of anatomy at Padua in Italy | French army surgeon | English physician to James I and Charles I |
| What he did | Dissected **human** corpses himself; published **On the Fabric of the Human Body** (1543) with detailed, accurate drawings | Ran out of boiling oil to treat gunshot wounds and used an **ointment** of egg yolk, oil of roses and turpentine; used **ligatures** to tie arteries in amputations instead of cauterising; designed **artificial limbs** | Showed the **heart is a pump** and that blood **circulates** around the body through arteries and veins; published in **1628** |
| Galen challenged | Showed the jaw is **one bone** and found no holes in the septum of the heart; said doctors must dissect for themselves | Showed that boiling oil caused more pain and harm than a soothing ointment | Galen said blood was made in the liver and used up; Harvey showed the same blood circulates, using experiments, dissection of live animals and calculation |
| Why limited | Anatomy did not tell doctors how to **cure** anything; many doctors still defended Galen | Ligatures could carry **infection** into the wound and took longer to apply, so bleeding was a risk; no antiseptics | No immediate effect on **treatment**; he could not explain how blood passed from arteries to veins (tiny capillaries were seen under a microscope a few decades later); many doctors rejected him and his practice reportedly suffered |

**Why they succeeded:** printing spread their work (Paré also wrote in French, not Latin, so ordinary surgeons could read it); war gave Paré huge experience; Vesalius had legal access to corpses; Harvey used the **scientific method** and was protected by royal patronage.

**Opposition to change:** physicians had trained in Galen and their reputations depended on him; older doctors were conservative; new ideas did not improve cures, so patients and doctors saw little reason to change; Harvey's ideas were mocked at first.

### 3. Dealing with disease

**Traditional methods** carried on: bleeding, purging, sweating, herbal remedies and the humours. **New methods** included chemical remedies, and new plants from the Americas such as **quinine** (from cinchona bark), which worked against malaria, and tobacco, wrongly believed to protect against disease. The physician **Thomas Sydenham** (17th century) revived Hippocrates' stress on careful observation of each disease.

**Quackery** grew in the 17th and 18th centuries. **Quacks** were unqualified sellers of remedies who travelled to fairs and markets, or advertised in the new newspapers, selling pills and potions with wild claims. They thrived because doctors were expensive, regulation was weak, and real medicine could rarely cure.

**The Great Plague of 1665** killed tens of thousands in London, perhaps around a fifth of its people.

| Beliefs | Treatments and remedies | Measures by the authorities (Lord Mayor and the king's government) |
|---|---|---|
| God's punishment; **miasma**; the positions of the planets; some believed it spread from person to person | Posies of herbs, smoking tobacco, bleeding, sweating, remedies bought from physicians and quacks | Infected houses **shut up** with the family inside for 40 days, marked with a **red cross** and guarded by watchmen; **searchers** reported causes of death; bodies buried in **plague pits** at night; **cats and dogs killed**; bonfires to purify the air; crowds, fairs and theatres banned; the king and court left London |

The response shows **change and continuity**: beliefs about the cause were still medieval, but the **authorities were far more organised** than in 1348, recording deaths in the **Bills of Mortality** and enforcing rules. The plague never returned on that scale after 1665.

### 4. Hospitals, training and status

- When Henry VIII **dissolved the monasteries** (1536–40), many hospitals closed. A few, such as **St Bartholomew's** and **St Thomas's** in London, were refounded under the city.
- In the 18th century many new **voluntary hospitals** were set up, paid for by wealthy subscribers, including **Guy's Hospital** in London (1720s). They aimed to **treat and cure** patients, and became places for **training** doctors, though most still refused infectious cases.
- **Physicians** still took university degrees, but training became more practical, with dissection and observation of patients. The **Royal College of Physicians** (1518) licensed physicians in London.
- **Surgeons** rose in status. The barbers and surgeons were joined in one company in 1540, but surgeons **separated** from barbers in 1745, and their company later became the **Royal College of Surgeons** (1800). Surgeons now trained by attending lectures, dissection and hospital work.

**John Hunter** (1728–93), a Scottish surgeon in London:
- Gained experience as an **army surgeon**, then worked at St George's Hospital and taught anatomy.
- Insisted on the **scientific method**: observe, experiment, test, and then apply findings to treatment. He developed a new operation for an **aneurysm** (a swollen artery) in the leg, tying the artery instead of amputating.
- Trained hundreds of surgeons, including **Edward Jenner**, and built a huge anatomical collection used for teaching.
- **Significance**: he raised surgery from a craft to a **science** and spread the experimental approach through his pupils.

### 5. Prevention of disease: smallpox

**Smallpox** was one of the biggest killers of the 18th century, and survivors were often scarred or blinded.

**Inoculation**: **Lady Mary Wortley Montagu** saw it in the Ottoman Empire (Turkey), had her children inoculated, and publicised it in Britain in the 1720s. Pus from a mild case was rubbed into a cut, giving a **mild** dose and lifelong immunity. It became popular and profitable for **inoculators**, but it could cause a **serious case** and the patient could **spread** smallpox to others.

**Edward Jenner** (1749–1823), a country doctor in Gloucestershire, heard that **milkmaids** who caught **cowpox** did not catch smallpox.
- In **1796** he took matter from a cowpox sore on a milkmaid and put it into a cut on the arm of a boy, **James Phipps**. Later he inoculated the boy with **smallpox**, and he did not catch it.
- He tested it on more people and published his findings in **1798**. He called it **vaccination** (from the Latin for cow).
- **Spread**: vaccination spread quickly in Britain and abroad, and Parliament gave Jenner large grants.

**Opposition**
- Jenner **could not explain why** it worked; germ theory came in the 1860s.
- The **Royal Society** would not publish his work at first, and some doctors had poor results with contaminated material.
- **Inoculators** lost income and attacked it.
- **Religious** objections to putting animal matter into humans; cartoons mocked people growing cow features.
- **Government compulsion** angered people: vaccination of infants was made **compulsory in 1853**, and the **Anti-Vaccination League** campaigned against it as an attack on liberty.

**Significance**: the first safe, scientific method of **preventing** a disease; it showed government could play a role in public health; smallpox was eventually declared **eradicated** worldwide in 1980. But because nobody knew why it worked, it could not be applied to other diseases until Pasteur's work.

### 6. Factors in Part two

| Factor | Effect c1500–c1800 |
|---|---|
| **Individuals** | Vesalius, Paré, Harvey, Hunter and Jenner drove the main changes in ideas and prevention |
| **Science and technology** | Printing spread new ideas; dissection and experiment replaced trust in books; early microscopes |
| **Communication** | Printed books, the Royal Society and newspapers spread both new science and quack advertising |
| **War** | Gave Paré and Hunter experience of wounds |
| **Chance** | Paré running out of oil; Jenner hearing about milkmaids |
| **Religion** | Weaker after the Reformation; still shaped beliefs about plague and some opposition to vaccination |
| **Government** | Organised plague measures in 1665; later grants to Jenner and compulsory vaccination in 1853 |

::: terms
- **Renaissance**: a revival of classical learning that encouraged observation, questioning and new discoveries.
- **Printing press**: the machine that made identical copies of books and illustrations, spreading ideas quickly.
- **Ligature**: a thread tied round a blood vessel to stop bleeding, used by Paré instead of cauterising.
- **Circulation of the blood**: Harvey's discovery that the heart pumps the same blood round the body continuously.
- **Quack**: an unqualified seller of remedies who made false claims, often at fairs or through advertisements.
- **Voluntary hospital**: an 18th-century hospital paid for by charitable subscribers, aiming to treat and cure patients.
- **Scientific method**: finding out by observation, experiment and testing rather than by trusting old books.
- **Inoculation**: deliberately giving a mild dose of smallpox to create immunity, which risked serious illness.
- **Vaccination**: Jenner's method of protecting against smallpox using cowpox, a milder disease.
- **Anti-Vaccination League**: a 19th-century campaign against compulsory vaccination as an attack on liberty.
:::

::: example Q1 Source utility (8)
Source A, written for this practice question, is in the style of an order issued by the Lord Mayor of London in the summer of 1665. It says that every house visited by the plague must be shut up for forty days with a red cross and the words "Lord have mercy upon us" on the door, that watchmen must guard it day and night, and that dogs and cats must be killed. How useful is Source A to an historian studying how people dealt with the Great Plague of 1665? (8)

Source A is useful because it shows that the **authorities** took organised action. Shutting up houses, marking them with a red cross and posting watchmen were real measures of 1665, and they show that some people believed plague spread from **person to person**. Killing cats and dogs also fits: they were thought to carry the infection, although we now know the real carriers were fleas on rats, so killing cats may have made things worse.

The **provenance** makes it valuable: an official order shows what the government intended, and it shows a far more organised response than in 1348, alongside searchers, plague pits and the Bills of Mortality.

However, an order shows what was **meant** to happen, not how well it worked. Many people broke the rules or fled, and it says nothing about what ordinary people did themselves, such as carrying posies, smoking tobacco or buying remedies from quacks, which were based on **miasma** and religion. So it is very useful for the official response, but less useful for individual treatment or for how effective the measures were. (Level 4.)

::: example Q2 Significance of Harvey (8)
Explain the significance of William Harvey. (8)

**At the time**, Harvey's discovery of the **circulation of the blood** (1628) was significant because it overturned Galen's belief that blood was made in the liver and used up. He proved it by **experiment and calculation**, showing the value of the scientific method.

However, its **immediate impact** was small. Doctors still bled patients, because his discovery did not suggest a new treatment, and many rejected it out of loyalty to Galen. He could not explain how blood passed from arteries to veins until capillaries were seen under a microscope.

**Over time** his significance grew. Understanding circulation became the basis of physiology and was essential for later developments such as **blood transfusion** and heart surgery in the 20th century. So Harvey was more significant for **changing ideas** and methods than for changing treatment in his lifetime. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Similarity (8)
Explain two ways in which the work of Vesalius and Harvey was similar. (8)

**First, both challenged Galen using their own observation.** Vesalius dissected human bodies and showed that the jaw is one bone and that there were no holes in the septum of the heart. Harvey used dissection of animals, experiments and calculation to show that blood circulates rather than being made in the liver and used up. Both proved that the ancient authority could be wrong.

**Second, both had little immediate effect on treatment.** Vesalius's **On the Fabric of the Human Body** (1543) improved anatomy, but it did not help doctors cure illness. Harvey's work (1628) was rejected by many doctors and bleeding carried on, because knowing that blood circulates did not suggest new cures. Both needed later developments before their work made a practical difference. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Factor essay (16)
Has the role of the individual been the main factor in the development of medicine? Explain your answer with reference to the role of the individual and other factors. Use a range of examples from across your study of Health and the people: c1000 to the present day. (16)

**Individuals** were vital, especially from the Renaissance. Vesalius corrected Galen's anatomy, Harvey proved circulation, and Jenner developed vaccination in 1796. Later, Pasteur's germ theory, Lister's antiseptics and Fleming's penicillin were turning points. But individuals could also **hold back** change: Galen's authority lasted over 1,000 years.

**Individuals needed other factors.** **Communication** mattered: printing spread Vesalius's drawings, while Ibn al-Nafis' discovery was lost to Europe. **Chance** helped Paré and Fleming. **War** gave Paré and Hunter experience and made governments fund penicillin's mass production. **Science and technology** lay behind the breakthroughs: microscopes for Koch, X-rays for diagnosis.

**Government** turned individual discoveries into improvements for everyone: compulsory vaccination (1853), the 1875 Public Health Act, and the NHS (1948).

**Judgement.** The individual was the most important factor in producing **new ideas**, but not the main factor in **improving health**. Jenner's vaccine saved lives only when government made it compulsory, and Florey and Chain needed governments and industry to mass-produce penicillin. The individual was the spark; other factors decided whether it spread. (Level 4.)
