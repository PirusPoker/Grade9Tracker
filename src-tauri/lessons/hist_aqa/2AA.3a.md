## What it is

This lesson covers the first two bullets of **Part three: A revolution in medicine** (c1800 to c1900): **germ theory** and its impact on treating disease in Britain (**Pasteur**, **Koch**, microbe hunting, Pasteur's vaccines, **Ehrlich's** magic bullets, everyday remedies), and the **revolution in surgery** (anaesthetics with **Simpson and chloroform**, antiseptics with **Lister and carbolic acid**, new surgical procedures and **aseptic** surgery).

The big idea: for the first time, doctors learned **what actually causes disease**. Germ theory explained infection, made new vaccines possible and transformed surgery. But progress was **uneven**: ideas were resisted, and anaesthetics at first made surgery **more** dangerous.

### 1. Germ theory: Louis Pasteur

- Before the 1860s most scientists believed in **spontaneous generation**: that microbes seen under microscopes were **created by** decay. Disease itself was still blamed on **miasma**.
- **Pasteur**, a French chemist, was asked by industry to find out why liquids such as wine and beer went sour. He showed that **microbes in the air** caused the decay, using sealed flasks with long, curved necks: broth stayed fresh unless air-borne microbes could reach it.
- He published his **germ theory** in **1861**: microbes cause decay, and he went on to argue that particular microbes cause particular diseases.
- **Impact in Britain**: slow. Many British doctors clung to miasma or spontaneous generation, and Pasteur at first could not prove which germ caused which disease. But the theory inspired **Lister** in surgery and, over time, strengthened the case for clean water and public health.

### 2. Robert Koch and microbe hunting

**Koch**, a German doctor, proved that **specific germs cause specific diseases**.
- He identified the bacterium causing **anthrax** (1876), then **tuberculosis** (**1882**) and **cholera** (**1883**).
- **Technology** made this possible: he used new **dyes** to **stain** bacteria so they could be seen, **photographed** them through improved **microscopes**, and grew pure cultures on a **solid jelly** (agar) in flat dishes.
- He set out a method for proving that a particular microbe caused a particular disease, which his team and others followed.

**Microbe hunting and rivalry.** After France's defeat by Prussia (Germany) in the war of **1870–71**, Pasteur and Koch led **rival national teams**. Each government funded research to win prestige, and both sides raced to identify microbes. Within about 20 years the germs causing many major diseases (such as typhoid, diphtheria and plague) had been found. **Rivalry** pushed the pace, though it also made them reluctant to share results.

### 3. Pasteur and vaccination

| Disease | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Chicken cholera** (1879–80) | By **chance**, a culture of chicken cholera was left standing over the summer. When injected, the **weakened** germs did not kill the chickens, and they were then **immune** to fresh germs. Pasteur saw that weakening a germ could make a vaccine |
| **Anthrax** (1881) | A dramatic **public trial** on sheep: vaccinated animals survived a deadly dose; unvaccinated ones died |
| **Rabies** (1885) | Pasteur used a vaccine on a boy, **Joseph Meister**, bitten by a rabid dog. He survived |

Pasteur called his method **vaccination** in honour of Jenner. Unlike Jenner, he understood **why** it worked, so the method could be **applied to other diseases**.

### 4. Paul Ehrlich and magic bullets

- Vaccines prevent disease; they cannot **cure** someone already infected. **Ehrlich**, who had worked with Koch, searched for chemical "**magic bullets**" that would kill a specific germ without harming the body.
- His team tested hundreds of arsenic compounds against **syphilis**. Compound number 606, which had been rejected, was re-tested by a colleague, **Sahachiro Hata**, and found to work. It was launched as **Salvarsan 606** (around **1909–10**): the **first** magic bullet.
- It was hard to use and had side effects, but it proved that **chemicals could target germs**. A second magic bullet, **Prontosil**, followed in the 1930s.

### 5. Everyday treatments and remedies

Most ordinary people still could not afford doctors, so they relied on:
- **Patent medicines**: pills and tonics sold by companies with huge **newspaper advertising** and big claims. Ingredients were often secret and some contained **alcohol** or **opium** (laudanum).
- **Chemists and pharmacists**, whose cheaper mass-produced medicines grew with the industrial economy.
- **Home remedies** and herbal cures passed down in families.
These rarely cured anything; the real benefits of germ theory reached ordinary people slowly, mainly through public health and later through drugs and vaccines.

### 6. The revolution in surgery

Surgeons in 1800 still faced the three problems: **pain**, **infection** and **bleeding**. Operations had to be **fast**, and many patients died afterwards from infection ("hospital gangrene" or blood poisoning).

**Anaesthetics: solving pain**

| Development | Details | Problems |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitrous oxide** | "Laughing gas": its pain-killing effect was noted around 1800; used in dentistry in the 1840s | Not strong enough for major operations |
| **Ether** | Used in America in 1846; the London surgeon **Robert Liston** soon used it for an amputation | Irritated the lungs, caused coughing and vomiting, and was **flammable** |
| **Chloroform** | **James Simpson**, an Edinburgh professor of midwifery, tested chemicals on himself and friends and found chloroform in **1847** | **Dosage** was hard to judge; some patients died, for example a teenage girl, Hannah Greener, in 1848 |

**Opposition to chloroform**: fears about deaths and side effects; **religious** objections that pain in childbirth was God's will; some surgeons and army officers believed pain was useful or part of survival; doubts about a new and untested chemical.

**Acceptance**: **Queen Victoria** used chloroform during the birth of her son Leopold in **1853**, given by **John Snow**. Royal approval made it respectable.

**The "black period"** (roughly the 1840s to 1870s): because patients were unconscious, surgeons attempted **longer and deeper** operations, but still with dirty hands, instruments and coats. **Infection and blood loss rose**, and death rates after surgery may have increased. Solving pain without solving infection made things **worse** for a time.

**Antiseptics: fighting infection**
- **Joseph Lister**, a surgeon in Glasgow, read Pasteur's germ theory and reasoned that germs in the air caused wound infection.
- He knew **carbolic acid** was used to treat sewage. From **1865** he used it on wounds and dressings, starting with a boy's compound fracture, and published his results in **1867**. He later used a **carbolic spray** in the operating theatre and treated instruments and catgut ligatures.
- Death rates among his amputation patients **fell sharply**.

**Opposition to Lister**: carbolic **irritated** skin and lungs and was unpleasant to work in; it **slowed** operations; many surgeons did not accept germ theory, because they could not see germs; Lister kept **changing his methods**, which looked uncertain; some surgeons already had good results with cleanliness.

**Acceptance**: by the 1880s and 1890s, as Koch's work proved germ theory, antiseptic methods spread. Lister moved to King's College Hospital in London (1877) and won over a new generation.

**Aseptic surgery: keeping germs out**

From the late 1880s and 1890s surgeons moved from **killing germs** (antiseptic) to **preventing germs reaching the wound** (aseptic):
- instruments **sterilised** with steam; theatres scrubbed clean;
- surgeons in sterile **gowns** and **masks**; **rubber gloves** introduced (in America in the 1890s);
- the carbolic spray was abandoned.

With pain and infection controlled, surgeons could carry out **new procedures** inside the abdomen and chest, such as removing the appendix. **Bleeding** remained the unsolved problem until blood groups and transfusion in the 20th century.

### 7. Factors in Part three (medicine and surgery)

| Factor | Effect c1800–c1900 |
|---|---|
| **Science and technology** | Better **microscopes**, dyes and culture methods let Koch see and grow germs; chemistry produced anaesthetics, carbolic and Salvarsan |
| **Individuals** | Pasteur, Koch, Ehrlich, Simpson and Lister; their persistence overcame opposition |
| **Government** | French and German governments **funded** research teams; national pride drove the race |
| **War** | The Franco-Prussian War fuelled rivalry between Pasteur and Koch |
| **Communication** | Publishing in journals spread germ theory to Lister; newspapers sold patent medicines |
| **Chance** | The weakened chicken cholera culture; Hata re-testing compound 606 |
| **Religion** | Some objections to chloroform in childbirth, removed by royal example |

::: terms
- **Spontaneous generation**: the theory that microbes were created by decay, disproved by Pasteur.
- **Germ theory**: Pasteur's theory (1861) that microbes cause decay, extended to the idea that specific germs cause specific diseases.
- **Microbe hunting**: the race by rival teams, led by Pasteur and Koch, to identify the germs causing particular diseases.
- **Staining**: dyeing bacteria so they can be seen clearly under a microscope, used by Koch.
- **Magic bullet**: a chemical cure that kills a specific germ without harming the rest of the body.
- **Salvarsan 606**: Ehrlich's arsenic compound against syphilis, the first magic bullet, launched around 1909–10.
- **Patent medicine**: a branded remedy with secret ingredients sold through advertising, often with false claims.
- **Anaesthetic**: a substance that removes pain or consciousness during an operation, such as chloroform.
- **Black period**: the decades after anaesthetics, when longer operations without antiseptics led to more infection and death.
- **Antiseptic**: killing germs at the wound or during surgery, as Lister did with carbolic acid.
- **Aseptic surgery**: preventing germs from reaching a wound by sterilising instruments, gowns and theatres.
:::

::: example Q1 Source utility (8)
Source A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a report written by Joseph Lister for a medical journal in 1867. He describes treating compound fractures by dressing the wound with lint soaked in carbolic acid, and says that since he began this method none of these patients has developed blood poisoning or hospital gangrene. How useful is Source A to an historian studying the development of antiseptic surgery? (8)

Source A is useful because it shows Lister's **method**: carbolic acid applied to wounds to kill germs. This fits the context: Lister had read **Pasteur's germ theory** and reasoned that microbes caused infection, and he knew carbolic was used to treat sewage. The claim that infections stopped matches the sharp fall in deaths among his patients.

Its **provenance** is valuable but needs care. Lister wrote it himself in **1867**, the year he published his results, so it is an expert, first-hand account. But he wanted to **persuade** other surgeons, so he stresses his successes. Many surgeons at the time rejected his method: carbolic irritated the skin, slowed operations, and many did not believe in invisible germs.

So the source is very useful for **how** antiseptic surgery began and the evidence Lister used, but it gives only his side; an historian would need other sources to see the **opposition** and the later shift to aseptic surgery. (Level 4.)

::: example Q2 Significance of germ theory (8)
Explain the significance of germ theory. (8)

**At the time**, Pasteur's germ theory (1861) was significant because it overturned spontaneous generation and offered, for the first time, a **scientific explanation** of decay and infection. In Britain it inspired **Lister**, who used carbolic acid from 1865 to kill germs in wounds.

It became more significant once **Koch** proved that specific germs cause specific diseases, identifying TB (1882) and cholera (1883). This led to **microbe hunting**, and to **vaccines**: Pasteur's weakened cultures produced vaccines for chicken cholera, anthrax and rabies, and, unlike Jenner, he knew why they worked.

**Over time** it shaped all modern medicine: Ehrlich's search for **magic bullets**, aseptic surgery, and later antibiotics. It also strengthened the case for **public health** reform, because clean water and sewers could be justified scientifically. It was not accepted quickly in Britain, which shows that even a revolutionary idea needed proof and time. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Similarity (8)
Explain two ways in which the work of Simpson and Lister was similar. (8)

**First, both used chemicals to solve one of surgery's main problems.** Simpson discovered **chloroform** in 1847 by testing chemicals on himself and his friends, and it solved **pain**. Lister used **carbolic acid** from 1865 to solve **infection**, spraying it in theatres and soaking dressings in it.

**Second, both faced strong opposition before being accepted.** Chloroform was hard to dose, some patients died, and some argued that pain in childbirth was God's will; it was accepted after **Queen Victoria** used it in 1853. Carbolic irritated the skin and lungs, slowed operations, and many surgeons did not believe in germs; Lister's methods were accepted only as Koch's work proved germ theory in the 1880s. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Factor essay (16)
Has science and technology been the main factor in the development of medicine? Explain your answer with reference to science and technology and other factors. Use a range of examples from across your study of Health and the people: c1000 to the present day. (16)

**Science and technology** made the greatest changes. In the Middle Ages its absence explains why Galen's errors survived. Printing spread Vesalius's work, and Harvey's experiments proved circulation. In the 19th century better **microscopes** and **staining** let Koch identify the germs causing TB and cholera, and chemistry produced chloroform and carbolic acid. In the 20th century X-rays, mass-production of penicillin and keyhole surgery transformed treatment.

**Individuals** were needed to use it: Pasteur and Lister made the connections between science and practice, and Simpson experimented on himself. **Government** provided money: France and Germany funded Pasteur and Koch, and in the Second World War the British and US governments paid for penicillin's production. **War** often supplied urgency, from the Franco-Prussian rivalry to surgery on the Western Front. **Religion**, by contrast, mostly slowed change, as when the Church backed Galen.

**Judgement.** Science and technology was the main factor, because without microscopes, chemistry and machines no individual could have identified germs or made drugs. But it rarely worked alone: the biggest advances came when science was combined with **government funding** and the pressure of **war**. (Level 4.)
