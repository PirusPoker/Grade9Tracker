## What it is

This lesson covers the third bullet of Part one, **Divided society**: **organised crime and prohibition** and their impact on society; **the causes of racial tension**, **the experiences of immigrants** and the impact of immigration; **the Ku Klux Klan**; and **the Red Scare and the significance of the Sacco and Vanzetti case**.

The big idea: the 1920s were not only a boom. Many "old" Americans, white, Protestant and rural, felt their way of life was threatened by cities, immigrants, alcohol, Black migration and radical politics. Their reaction (prohibition, immigration quotas, the Klan and the Red Scare) divided the nation.

### 1. Prohibition

**What:** the **18th Amendment** banned the manufacture, sale and transport of alcohol; the **Volstead Act (1919)** defined alcohol and set out enforcement. Prohibition came into force in **January 1920** and lasted until **1933**.

**Why it was introduced:**
- Campaigns by the **Anti-Saloon League** and the **Woman's Christian Temperance Union**, strongest among rural Protestants.
- Religious and moral belief that alcohol caused crime, poverty, violence and broken families.
- **Anti-German feeling** in the First World War: many big brewers were of German origin; grain was needed for food.
- Some employers thought sober workers would be more productive.

**Why it failed:**

| Problem | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Huge demand** | Millions of Americans kept drinking |
| **Speakeasies** | Illegal bars, tens of thousands in New York alone |
| **Bootlegging** | Smuggling alcohol (from Canada and the Caribbean) and making **moonshine** at home, sometimes poisonous |
| **Too few agents** | A few thousand federal agents for an enormous country with long borders |
| **Corruption** | Gangsters bribed police, judges and politicians |
| **Organised crime** | Gangs grew rich supplying alcohol and fought for control of cities |

**Repeal:** the **21st Amendment (December 1933)** ended prohibition. By then it was clearly unenforceable, it had boosted organised crime, and in the Depression the government wanted the jobs and tax revenue a legal alcohol industry would bring.

### 2. Organised crime and Al Capone

- **Gangsters** controlled bootlegging, speakeasies, gambling and protection rackets. Rival gangs fought violent wars.
- **Al Capone** dominated Chicago in the late 1920s, earning a fortune and bribing police and officials; he presented himself as a businessman giving people what they wanted.
- **The St Valentine's Day Massacre (14 February 1929)**: seven men linked to the rival Bugs Moran gang were shot dead in a Chicago garage. No one was convicted, but public opinion turned against the gangs.
- Capone was jailed in **1931** for **tax evasion**, because prosecutors could not prove his other crimes.
- **Impact on society:** violence, corruption and lack of respect for the law; organised crime survived prohibition and moved into other rackets.

### 3. Immigration and the experiences of immigrants

- Before 1914 the USA had an **"open door"** policy: millions arrived, increasingly "new immigrants" from **southern and eastern Europe** (Italians, Poles, Russians, Jews), many Catholic or Jewish and poor.
- **Experiences:** they settled in city districts such as "Little Italy", in crowded tenements; took low-paid, unskilled jobs; faced prejudice from **WASPs** (White Anglo-Saxon Protestants) who saw them as dirty, criminal, radical or a threat to jobs and wages.
- **Restrictions:**

| Law | What it did |
|---|---|
| **Literacy test (1917)** | Immigrants had to pass a reading test |
| **Emergency Quota Act (1921)** | Each nationality limited to 3% of the number of that nationality living in the USA in 1910 |
| **Immigration Act (1924)** | Cut the quota to 2% and based it on the 1890 census, when there were fewer southern and eastern Europeans, so it favoured northern Europeans; immigration from Asia was effectively banned |

- **Impact:** immigration fell sharply; communities such as Italian Americans felt targeted; immigrants still contributed hugely to the boom's workforce and to American culture.

### 4. Racial tension

- **Jim Crow laws** in the Southern states **segregated** schools, transport, restaurants and public places; African Americans were kept from voting by literacy tests and poll taxes.
- **Lynching**: mob murders of African Americans, often unpunished.
- **The Great Migration**: hundreds of thousands of African Americans moved to northern cities such as Chicago, New York and Detroit for jobs and to escape Jim Crow. In the North they met discrimination in jobs and housing, and competition for work led to **race riots**, such as **Chicago in 1919**.
- **Responses:** the **NAACP** campaigned in the courts against discrimination and lynching; **Marcus Garvey's** movement promoted Black pride; the **Harlem Renaissance** saw a flowering of African American music, writing and art.

### 5. The Ku Klux Klan

- **Origins:** founded after the Civil War, it was **revived in 1915**, inspired partly by the film *The Birth of a Nation*, which glorified the original Klan.
- **Beliefs:** **white Protestant supremacy**; hostile to African Americans, **Catholics, Jews, immigrants** and communists; claimed to defend morality and prohibition.
- **Membership** peaked in the mid-1920s at several million (estimates of up to around five million), not only in the South but in states such as **Indiana**. Many members were respectable, middle-class people; it had political influence and helped elect officials.
- **Methods:** secret rituals, white robes and hoods, **burning crosses**; intimidation, beatings, **tarring and feathering**, and murders including lynchings; mass marches, including through **Washington, DC in 1925**.
- **Decline:** in **1925** **David Stephenson**, the Klan's leader in Indiana, was convicted of the rape and murder of a young woman. The scandal exposed hypocrisy and corruption, and membership collapsed by the end of the decade.

### 6. The Red Scare

- **Causes:** the **Russian Revolution (1917)** and fear that communism would spread; many **strikes** in 1919; a wave of **bombings** by anarchists in 1919, including one at the home of **Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer**; immigrants were blamed as radicals.
- **The Palmer Raids (1919–20):** thousands of suspected radicals, mostly immigrants, were arrested, often without evidence; hundreds were **deported**.
- The scare faded by mid-1920 when predicted uprisings did not happen, but suspicion of immigrants and radicals lasted and fed the immigration laws.

### 7. Sacco and Vanzetti

- **Nicola Sacco** and **Bartolomeo Vanzetti** were Italian immigrants and **anarchists**. In **1920** they were arrested for an armed robbery at a shoe factory in South Braintree, Massachusetts, in which two men were killed.
- **The trial (1921):** the evidence was weak and contradictory, and many witnesses who said they were elsewhere were Italian immigrants, so were not believed. The judge, **Webster Thayer**, showed open prejudice against them. They were found guilty.
- Despite appeals, another man's confession and protests in the USA and around the world, they were **executed in August 1927**.
- **Significance:** the case showed how **prejudice against immigrants and radicals** could override justice in the 1920s. It divided Americans, became a worldwide symbol of injustice, and in 1977 the governor of Massachusetts declared they had not received a fair trial.

::: terms
- **Prohibition**: the ban on the manufacture, sale and transport of alcohol in the USA from 1920 to 1933.
- **Volstead Act**: the 1919 law that defined alcoholic drinks and set out how prohibition would be enforced.
- **Speakeasy**: an illegal bar selling alcohol during prohibition.
- **Bootlegger**: someone who made, smuggled or sold illegal alcohol.
- **Organised crime**: crime run by gangs as a business, such as Al Capone's operation in Chicago.
- **WASP**: a White Anglo-Saxon Protestant, the group that dominated American society and politics.
- **Quota**: a fixed limit on the number of immigrants from each country, as set by the 1921 and 1924 laws.
- **Jim Crow laws**: laws in the Southern states enforcing racial segregation.
- **Great Migration**: the movement of African Americans from the rural South to northern cities.
- **Ku Klux Klan**: the white supremacist organisation revived in 1915 that targeted African Americans, Catholics, Jews and immigrants.
- **Red Scare**: the fear of communism and anarchism in the USA in 1919–20.
- **Anarchist**: someone who believes all government should be abolished, like Sacco and Vanzetti.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 Section A at a glance.** About an hour, 40 marks: Q1 differ (4), Q2 why differ (4), Q3 more convincing (8), Q4 describe two (4), Q5 in what ways (8), Q6 which bullet (12). Typical: interpretations on the Klan or on Sacco and Vanzetti; "in what ways were immigrants affected"; "which caused more division: prohibition or racial tension".

1. **Q1 (4): content only.** E.g. one interpretation says Sacco and Vanzetti were guilty criminals, the other that they were victims of prejudice.
2. **Q2 (4): provenance + context.** A Klan member's speech, an Italian American newspaper, a prosecutor's memoir, a modern historian: explain how identity, time and purpose shape each view. The Red Scare is often the key context.
3. **Q3 (8): test both against precise knowledge**: the 1921 and 1924 quotas, the Palmer Raids, Judge Thayer, the Stephenson scandal, the number of agents, Capone's conviction. Judge with a reason.
4. **Q4 (4): two features, each with a detail.**
5. **Q5 (8): Level 4 shows different effects on different groups** (Italians versus Asians; northern versus southern African Americans) or links between them (the Red Scare feeding the quotas).
6. **Q6 (12): explain both, then compare by a criterion**: how many people were affected, how long the division lasted, how violent it was.
:::

::: example Q1 and Q2: interpretations on Sacco and Vanzetti (4 + 4)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a Massachusetts newspaper editorial in 1927: "Two foreign anarchists who preached violence were found guilty by a jury of decent citizens, and every appeal has failed. The law must now take its course. Outsiders who protest know nothing of the facts." Interpretation B, also written for practice, is in the style of a historian writing in 2017: "Sacco and Vanzetti were tried as much for who they were as for what they were said to have done. Weak evidence, a hostile judge and a frightened public sealed their fate." How does Interpretation B differ from Interpretation A about the trial of Sacco and Vanzetti? (4)

A says the trial was **fair** and the verdict should stand, because a jury found them guilty and appeals failed (1–2). B says they were **convicted because of prejudice** against them as immigrants and radicals, on weak evidence, with a hostile judge (+1). So A defends the justice system, B says it failed (+1). Level 2, 4/4.

Why might the authors have a different interpretation? (4)

The editorial was written in Massachusetts in 1927 as the execution approached, when the state was under worldwide criticism; the newspaper was defending its own state's courts and reflected local fear of radicals after the Red Scare (1–2). The historian in 2017 can look at all the evidence, including the weaknesses in the case and the 1977 declaration that the trial was unfair, without the fears of the time (+1–2). Level 2, 4/4.
:::

::: example Q4 Describe two (4)
Describe two problems caused by prohibition. (4)

**Organised crime.** Gangs made fortunes supplying illegal alcohol (1). In Chicago, Al Capone's gang fought rivals, as in the St Valentine's Day Massacre of 1929, when seven men were shot (1).

**Corruption.** Gangsters bribed police, judges and politicians to ignore speakeasies and bootlegging (1). This meant the law was openly broken and people lost respect for it (1).
:::

::: example Q5 In what ways (8)
In what ways were immigrants affected by American attitudes in the 1920s? Explain your answer. (8)

**Restricted entry.** The Emergency Quota Act (1921) limited each nationality to 3% of its 1910 population in the USA; the 1924 Act cut this to 2% of the 1890 figure. Immigration fell sharply.

**Prejudice and suspicion.** WASPs blamed "new immigrants" for crime, radicalism and low wages. In the Red Scare, the Palmer Raids (1919–20) arrested thousands, mostly immigrants, and hundreds were deported. Sacco and Vanzetti, Italian anarchists, were executed in 1927 after a trial marked by prejudice.

**Violence.** The Ku Klux Klan targeted Catholics, Jews and immigrants as well as African Americans.

**Complexity (Level 4).** Different immigrants were affected differently. The 1924 Act was designed to favour northern Europeans, while southern and eastern Europeans were cut sharply and Asians were effectively banned. And the effects were linked: the Red Scare made immigrants look dangerous, which built support for the quotas and coloured the Sacco and Vanzetti trial.
:::

::: example Q6 Which caused more division (12)
Which of the following caused more division in American society in the 1920s: prohibition; racial tension? Explain your answer with reference to both bullet points. (12)

**Prohibition.** It split "dry" rural Protestants from "wet" city dwellers and immigrants. Millions broke the law in tens of thousands of speakeasies; gangsters such as Capone grew rich; corruption spread through police and courts. It ended with the 21st Amendment in 1933.

**Racial tension.** Jim Crow laws segregated the South and lynchings went unpunished. The Great Migration brought African Americans north, where competition for jobs and housing led to riots such as Chicago in 1919. The Ku Klux Klan, with several million members by the mid-1920s, attacked African Americans, Catholics, Jews and immigrants.

**Judgement.** Racial tension caused more division, because it was violent, affected millions every day through segregation, and lasted long after the 1920s. Prohibition divided people over a law, which could be repealed, and did so in 1933. Racial division was built into law and society itself. (Level 4.)
:::
