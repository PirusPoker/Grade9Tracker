## What it is

This lesson covers Part two, **Germany and the Depression**: the **impact of the Depression** (the growth in support for the Nazis and other extremist parties 1928–32, the role of the SA, and Hitler's appeal); the **failure of Weimar democracy** (election results, the roles of Papen and Hindenburg, and Hitler's appointment as Chancellor); and the **establishment of Hitler's dictatorship** (the Reichstag Fire, the Enabling Act, the elimination of political opposition and trade unions, Röhm and the Night of the Long Knives, and Hitler becoming Führer).

The big idea: the Depression turned the Nazis from a fringe party into the largest party, but Hitler was never elected to power. He was **appointed** by Hindenburg on the advice of conservatives who thought they could control him, and then took about eighteen months to destroy every rival centre of power.

### 1. The impact of the Depression

- **The Wall Street Crash (October 1929)** led American banks to **recall their short-term loans** to Germany. German firms that depended on them cut production or went bankrupt; world trade collapsed, so exports fell.
- **Unemployment** rose from under two million in 1929 to about **six million by early 1932**, roughly a third of the workforce. Many more were on short time.
- **Effects on people:** hunger, homelessness and despair; the middle classes feared falling into poverty again, only six years after hyperinflation; farmers faced collapsing prices and debts; young people had no jobs.
- **Government failure.** The coalition collapsed in March 1930 over cuts to unemployment benefit. The new Chancellor, **Heinrich Brüning**, had no Reichstag majority and governed by **presidential decree (Article 48)**. He cut spending, wages and benefits and raised taxes, earning the nickname "the hunger chancellor". Democracy was already being bypassed before Hitler came to power.

### 2. Growth in support for extremist parties

| Election | Nazi seats (vote) | Communist (KPD) seats |
|---|---|---|
| May 1928 | 12 (about 2.6%) | 54 |
| September 1930 | **107** (about 18%) | 77 |
| July 1932 | **230** (about 37%): **largest party** | 89 |
| November 1932 | 196 (about 33%) | 100 |

Both extremes grew: desperate voters wanted radical answers. The KPD's rise also **frightened** the middle classes, business leaders and farmers, who turned to the Nazis as a barrier against communism.

### 3. Hitler's appeal and Nazi methods

- **Hitler as a speaker.** Powerful, emotional speeches; in 1932 he toured by aeroplane to speak in many cities in a day, which seemed modern and dynamic.
- **Promises to everyone.** "Work and bread" for the unemployed; protection from communism for businessmen and the middle classes; better prices for farmers; national pride and the overturning of Versailles for nationalists; a strong leader instead of squabbling parties.
- **Scapegoats.** The Nazis blamed Germany's problems on the "November criminals", communists and Jews: simple explanations for frightening times.
- **Propaganda.** Under **Goebbels**, posters, rallies, leaflets and films repeated simple messages. The nationalist press owner **Alfred Hugenberg** gave Hitler publicity after they campaigned together against the Young Plan in 1929.
- **The SA (stormtroopers).** Led by **Ernst Röhm**, it grew to hundreds of thousands of men, many unemployed. It protected Nazi meetings, broke up opponents' meetings, fought communists in the streets and marched in disciplined parades. It made the Nazis look strong and ready to restore order, even though it caused much of the disorder.

Support came most strongly from Protestant, rural and small-town Germany, the middle classes and the young; the Catholic Centre Party and the industrial working class were more resistant.

### 4. The failure of Weimar democracy, 1930–33

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| March 1930 – May 1932 | **Brüning** rules by decree; unemployment rises |
| March–April 1932 | **Presidential election**: Hindenburg beats Hitler, but Hitler wins over a third of the votes in the run-off |
| May 1932 | **Franz von Papen** becomes Chancellor; he has little Reichstag support |
| July 1932 | Nazis win 230 seats. Hitler demands to be Chancellor; Hindenburg refuses |
| November 1932 | Nazi seats fall to 196; the party is short of money |
| December 1932 | **General Kurt von Schleicher** replaces Papen but cannot win a majority |
| January 1933 | **Papen** persuades Hindenburg to appoint Hitler Chancellor, with Papen as Vice-Chancellor and only two other Nazis in the cabinet |
| **30 January 1933** | **Hitler appointed Chancellor** |

**Why the conservatives did it.** Papen, Hindenburg and their allies wanted a government with mass support that would crush the communists and end democracy, and they believed they could use Hitler and control him. Papen is supposed to have boasted that they had "hired" him. The Nazis had fallen back in November 1932, which made the deal look safe. It was a fatal miscalculation.

### 5. Establishing the dictatorship, 1933–34

| Date | Event | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **27 February 1933** | **Reichstag Fire**; a Dutch communist, Marinus van der Lubbe, was arrested | Hitler claimed a communist plot. The next day Hindenburg signed an **emergency decree** suspending civil liberties; thousands of communists were arrested |
| **5 March 1933** | Election: the Nazis won **288 seats (about 44%)**, still no majority alone | Achieved under intimidation, with KPD leaders arrested |
| **23 March 1933** | **Enabling Act**, passed with Centre Party support while SA men surrounded the building; only the SPD voted against | Gave Hitler power to make laws **without the Reichstag** for four years: the legal basis of the dictatorship |
| **2 May 1933** | **Trade unions abolished**, offices occupied, leaders arrested | Replaced by the **German Labour Front (DAF)** |
| **14 July 1933** | **Law against the formation of new parties** | Germany a **one-party state**; other parties had already been banned or dissolved themselves |
| **30 June 1934** | **Night of the Long Knives** | See below |
| **2 August 1934** | **Hindenburg died**. Hitler combined the offices of Chancellor and President as **Führer** | The army swore an **oath of personal loyalty to Hitler**; a plebiscite later that month approved |

**The Night of the Long Knives.** Röhm wanted the SA, by now millions strong, to absorb the army and talked of a "second revolution" against big business. The army generals, the business elites and Hindenburg feared and despised the SA. Hitler needed the army's support to succeed Hindenburg. On **30 June 1934** the **SS** murdered **Röhm** and other SA leaders, along with old enemies such as **Schleicher** and Gregor Strasser: at least 85 people, probably more. Hitler justified it as defending Germany from a plot. Results: the SA was sidelined, the **SS** under Himmler rose, the army was reassured, and Germans saw that Hitler would kill to keep power.

::: terms
- **Depression**: the world economic slump after the Wall Street Crash of October 1929, which brought mass unemployment to Germany.
- **Rule by decree**: governing through emergency presidential orders under Article 48 instead of laws passed by the Reichstag.
- **SA**: the Sturmabteilung or stormtroopers, the Nazi paramilitary force led by Ernst Röhm.
- **KPD**: the German Communist Party, whose growth in 1930–32 frightened the middle classes and elites.
- **Reichstag Fire**: the burning of the parliament building on 27 February 1933, which Hitler blamed on a communist plot.
- **Enabling Act**: the law of 23 March 1933 allowing Hitler to make laws without the Reichstag for four years.
- **German Labour Front (DAF)**: the Nazi organisation that replaced the trade unions in May 1933.
- **One-party state**: a state where only one political party is allowed, as Germany became in July 1933.
- **Night of the Long Knives**: the purge of 30 June 1934 in which Röhm and other opponents were murdered by the SS.
- **Führer**: "leader"; Hitler's title after he merged the posts of Chancellor and President in August 1934.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 Section A at a glance.** About an hour, 40 marks: Q1 differ (4), Q2 why differ (4), Q3 more convincing (8), Q4 describe two (4), Q5 in what ways (8), Q6 which bullet was more important (12). This Part produces "describe two problems Hitler faced", "in what ways were people affected by the Depression" and "Depression or Papen and Hindenburg" questions.

1. **Q1 (4): content only.** State the difference in the overall view (e.g. people voted Nazi from belief, or from desperation), backed by detail from each.
2. **Q2 (4): provenance + context.** Explain how an author's experience, date and purpose shape the view: a former SA man remembering comradeship, a Social Democrat who was arrested in 1933, a historian using voting statistics.
3. **Q3 (8): test each view against precise knowledge.** Election figures (12 → 107 → 230 → 196), six million unemployed, the SA, propaganda, Papen's deal. Judge which explains more.
4. **Q4 (4): two features, each with a precise detail.**
5. **Q5 (8): several developed effects; Level 4 shows how effects differed by group** (unemployed workers, middle classes, farmers, businessmen) or how economic distress turned into political change.
6. **Q6 (12): explain both bullets with evidence, then judge.** A strong line: "the Depression made Hitler powerful enough to be considered, but Papen and Hindenburg made him Chancellor."
:::

::: example Q1 and Q2: interpretations on Nazi voters (4 + 4)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a man who joined the Nazis in 1931, interviewed in 1975: "I had been out of work for two years. The other parties just argued. Hitler said there would be work and bread, and his men in the street looked like they meant business. We didn't think much about the Jews or the rest of it. We wanted a future." Interpretation B, also written for practice, is in the style of a historian writing in 2014: "Nazi voters were not simply desperate. Many shared the party's hatred of the Republic, of Versailles and of Marxism. The Depression gave old prejudices a new urgency." How does Interpretation B differ from Interpretation A about why Germans supported the Nazis? (4)

A says people supported the Nazis out of **economic desperation**, wanting jobs, and paid little attention to Nazi ideas (1–2). B says many supported them because they **already shared Nazi beliefs**, such as hatred of the Republic and Marxism, and the Depression only sharpened them (+1). So A stresses practical need, B ideological agreement (+1). Level 2, 4/4.

Why might the authors have a different interpretation? (4)

The author of A was an unemployed worker who joined in 1931; speaking in 1975, after the Holocaust, he had a reason to play down the ideas he signed up to and stress hunger, which is easier to defend (1–2). The historian in B is looking at the whole electorate with the benefit of research on who voted Nazi, such as Protestant middle-class and rural voters who were not unemployed, so sees beliefs as well as hardship (+1–2). Level 2, 4/4.
:::

::: example Q4 Describe two (4)
Describe two problems Hitler faced in establishing his dictatorship after January 1933. (4)

**He was not in full control of the government.** Hitler was only Chancellor, with just two other Nazis in the cabinet, and Hindenburg could dismiss him (1). Papen and the conservatives expected to control him, so he needed events like the Reichstag Fire to win emergency powers (1).

**The SA.** Röhm's SA had millions of members and wanted a "second revolution" and to take over the army (1). The army and businessmen feared it, and Hitler needed the army's support, so he had Röhm killed in the Night of the Long Knives, 30 June 1934 (1).
:::

::: example Q5 In what ways (8)
In what ways were the lives of the German people affected by the Depression, 1929–1932? Explain your answer. (8)

**Unemployment and poverty.** American loans were recalled after the Wall Street Crash, firms collapsed and unemployment reached about six million by early 1932. Families went hungry; many lost their homes.

**Government cuts.** Brüning ruled by decree, cutting wages and unemployment benefit and raising taxes, so even those still in work were poorer. He became known as the "hunger chancellor".

**Political extremism.** Desperate people turned to radical parties. The KPD rose to 100 seats by November 1932 and the Nazis became the largest party in July 1932. Street battles between the SA and communists made towns violent.

**Complexity (Level 4).** The effects differed by group. Industrial workers were hit hardest by unemployment and many turned to the KPD. The middle classes and farmers were frightened as much as impoverished: they feared a communist revolution and another collapse like 1923, so many turned to the Nazis as the party of order. Economic distress became political change.
:::

::: example Q6 Which was more important (12)
Which of the following was the more important reason why Hitler became Chancellor in January 1933: the Depression; the role of Papen and Hindenburg? Explain your answer with reference to both bullet points. (12)

**The Depression.** It turned the Nazis into a mass party. Before it they had 12 seats (1928); with unemployment at six million they won 107 seats in 1930 and 230 in July 1932, the largest party. It discredited the Republic, as Brüning's decree rule showed democracy was not working, and it boosted the KPD, frightening the middle classes towards Hitler.

**Papen and Hindenburg.** Hitler was never elected Chancellor. Hindenburg refused to appoint him in August 1932, and in November the Nazi vote fell to 196 seats. It was Papen, wanting revenge on Schleicher and a government with mass support, who persuaded Hindenburg in January 1933 to appoint Hitler with Papen as Vice-Chancellor, believing they could control him.

**Judgement.** The Depression was a necessary condition, because without 230 seats Hitler would never have been considered. But it was not enough: by late 1932 the Nazis were losing votes and money. The decisive reason was the conservatives' decision to bring him in. The Depression made him powerful; Papen and Hindenburg made him Chancellor. (Level 4.)
:::

What was the Enabling Act and how was it passed? (4)
---

Explain why Hitler ordered the Night of the Long Knives. (4)
---

::: watch
- **"Hitler was elected Chancellor."** He was appointed by Hindenburg; the Nazi vote peaked at about 37% (July 1932).
- **Forgetting the November 1932 dip.** The Nazis fell to 196 seats; it is key evidence that Papen and Hindenburg, not votes, decided the outcome.
- **Treating the dictatorship as instant.** It took from January 1933 to August 1934: Reichstag Fire, Enabling Act, unions, parties, Night of the Long Knives, Führer.
- **Confusing the SA and SS.** The SA was Röhm's mass army of stormtroopers; the SS carried out the purge of 30 June 1934 and grew afterwards.
- **Describing the Depression without explaining its political effects.** In Q5 and Q6 always connect unemployment to votes, fear of communism and decree rule.
:::
