## What it is

This lesson covers the first two bullets of **Part one: The Normans: conquest and control**: the **causes of the Norman Conquest** (the death of **Edward the Confessor**, the **claimants** and their **claims**) and the **military aspects** of 1066 (**Stamford Bridge**, **Hastings**, Anglo-Saxon and Norman **tactics**, and the military innovations of **cavalry** and **castles**).

The big idea: 1066 was decided by a combination of **claims**, **preparation**, **timing** and **tactics**. Harold Godwinson had the strongest position in England, but he had to fight two invasions in three weeks at opposite ends of the country. Examiners reward answers that **weigh** these causes rather than listing them, and that remember that luck (the wind, the timing) only mattered because of the choices people made.

### 1. Edward the Confessor and the succession crisis

- **Edward the Confessor** (king from 1042) had lived for years in exile in **Normandy** and favoured some Normans. He had **no children**, so there was no clear heir.
- The **Witan** (council of leading nobles and churchmen) chose the king, usually from the royal family and taking account of the dying king's wishes.
- The **Godwins** were the most powerful family. **Harold Godwinson**, **Earl of Wessex**, was Edward's leading subject; his sister Edith was Edward's queen.
- Edward died on **5 January 1066**; Harold was crowned the next day in Westminster Abbey. The speed shows he expected a challenge.

### 2. The four claimants

| Claimant | Who he was | His claim | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Harold Godwinson** | Earl of Wessex, Edward's brother-in-law, an experienced soldier | Said Edward named him as successor on his deathbed; chosen by the **Witan**; the most powerful man in England and already ruling in practice | Not of royal blood; William claimed Harold had **sworn an oath** to support him |
| **William, Duke of Normandy** | Edward's distant cousin (Edward's mother Emma was William's great-aunt); a tough, experienced ruler | Said Edward **promised** him the throne (probably around 1051); said Harold swore on **holy relics** in Normandy (around 1064) to support his claim; later gained the **Pope's** backing | Only a distant family link; not chosen by the Witan; the English did not accept a promise as binding |
| **Harald Hardrada** | King of Norway, a famous Viking warrior | Based on an agreement made between earlier kings of Norway and England (Magnus and Harthacnut) that each would inherit the other's kingdom; backed by **Tostig**, Harold's exiled brother | The deal was old and not recognised in England; he had to invade to make it real |
| **Edgar Aethling** | Edward's great-nephew, a teenager | The strongest **blood** claim: grandson of King Edmund Ironside | Too young, with no army and no powerful supporters in 1066 |

**Tostig.** Harold's brother Tostig had been Earl of Northumbria until a northern rebellion in 1065. Harold accepted the rebels' demands and Tostig was **exiled**. Bitter, he raided the English coast in spring 1066 and then joined Hardrada. This family split helped bring a Norwegian army to Yorkshire.

**Why the Normans stressed the oath.** Norman writers such as William of Poitiers (William's chaplain), and the **Bayeux Tapestry** (made for William's half-brother Bishop Odo), present Harold as an **oath-breaker** to make the invasion look lawful. Sources written for the winners justify the Conquest: keep this in mind with interpretations.

### 3. William's preparations

- **Support**: he won over his nobles and recruited knights from **Brittany**, **Flanders** and elsewhere with promises of **land** in England.
- **The Pope**: Alexander II gave his blessing and a **papal banner**, making the invasion a holy cause.
- **Ships and supplies**: a fleet of probably several hundred ships, carrying **horses** as well as men; the army camped for weeks at the mouth of the Dives, fed and disciplined.
- **Prefabricated forts**: Norman accounts say he brought wooden parts for a fort to put up on landing.
- **Waiting**: contrary winds kept the fleet in port into September; it moved to St Valery. The delay turned out to help him.

### 4. Harold's problems in the summer of 1066

Expecting William first, Harold kept the **fyrd** and fleet on the **south coast** all summer. The fyrd served only for a limited time and was needed for the **harvest**; food ran out, and on **8 September** he sent it home. Within days **Hardrada and Tostig** landed in the north.

### 5. Fulford and Stamford Bridge

| Battle | Date | What happened | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gate Fulford** | 20 September 1066 | Hardrada and Tostig landed, then defeated the northern earls **Edwin and Morcar** outside **York** | York surrendered; the northern earls' forces were badly weakened |
| **Stamford Bridge** | 25 September 1066 | Harold marched north with his **housecarls** at great speed, gathering men on the way, and caught the Norwegians by **surprise** east of York. Many had left their armour with the ships | A crushing English victory. **Hardrada and Tostig were killed**. So few Norwegians survived that only a small part of their fleet was needed to take them home |

**What was important about Stamford Bridge?**
- It **ended the Viking threat**: one claimant and the dangerous Tostig were dead.
- It showed Harold's **leadership and speed**, and the skill of his housecarls.
- But it came at a **cost**: Harold lost experienced men, his army was tired, and he was **in the north** when William landed in the south.
- Edwin and Morcar's forces, already damaged at Fulford, did not join Harold at Hastings.

### 6. William lands

- The wind changed and William landed at **Pevensey** on **28 September**, unopposed, because the fyrd and fleet had gone home.
- He built a fort inside the old Roman walls, then moved to **Hastings** and built a castle there. His men **ravaged** the surrounding land, part of Harold's own earldom, which may have been meant to provoke Harold into fighting quickly.
- Harold heard the news in the north, marched back to London, and then marched south **without waiting** for all the men he could have gathered.

### 7. The Battle of Hastings, 14 October 1066

**The armies.** Both were probably several thousand strong; estimates vary and the sources are not reliable on numbers.

| | Anglo-Saxon army | Norman army |
|---|---|---|
| **Troops** | **Housecarls** (professional, with two-handed axes), **thegns**, and men of the **fyrd** (less trained, some poorly armed) | **Knights** (mounted cavalry), **infantry**, **archers** and crossbowmen; Normans, Bretons, Flemings and French |
| **Formation** | A **shield wall** along the top of a ridge (Senlac), shields overlapping | Three divisions (Bretons on the left, Normans in the centre, French and Flemings on the right) in three lines: archers, infantry, cavalry |
| **Strength** | Very hard to break while it held, especially uphill | **Flexibility**: different troop types could be combined |

**The course of the battle**
1. Norman **archers**, infantry and cavalry attacked uphill in turn and failed against the shield wall.
2. The **Bretons** on the left fell back in disorder and a rumour spread that William was dead. He **lifted his helmet** and rallied his men. English who chased the Bretons downhill were surrounded and killed.
3. Norman sources describe **feigned retreats**: cavalry pretended to flee, drew more English off the ridge, then turned on them. Whether these were planned is debated, but the shield wall was thinned.
4. Late in the day **Harold was killed**, with his brothers **Gyrth and Leofwine** (the Tapestry's "arrow in the eye" figure is debated). The leaderless English broke.

### 8. Why did William win?

- **William's preparation and leadership**: papal support, a supplied mixed army, rallying his men at the moment of panic.
- **Norman tactics**: archers, infantry and cavalry combined; feigned retreats broke the shield wall's discipline.
- **Harold's decisions**: rushing south without the full army; a narrow ridge that packed his men in.
- **Luck and timing, and Stamford Bridge**: the wind delayed William until the fyrd had gone; Harold's men were tired and depleted.
- **Harold's death**: without him and his brothers, the English collapsed.

**Judgement.** Luck explains why William landed unopposed, but it does not explain why he won the battle. The best answers argue that William's **preparation and tactics** made the most of the luck, and that Harold's **decisions** (speed, position) turned a strong position into a defeat.

### 9. Military innovations: cavalry and castles

**Cavalry.** Anglo-Saxon armies rode to battle but fought **on foot**. Norman knights, trained from boyhood, fought **on horseback** with lance and sword. Cavalry could charge, chase and surround men who broke formation, as at Hastings, and later move fast to crush revolts.

**Castles.** The Anglo-Saxons had **burhs** (fortified towns protecting a whole community) but almost no private castles. The Normans built **motte and bailey** castles: an earth **motte** with a wooden tower, beside a **bailey** courtyard enclosed by a ditch and timber **palisade**. They went up in days or weeks with forced local labour, as at Pevensey and Hastings, and served as **bases**, **refuges** and **symbols** of conquest. The most important were later rebuilt in **stone**, like the **White Tower** in London.

### 10. If you sit in June 2027: the battlefield of Hastings as the "site"

AQA's specified site for Norman England in June 2027 is the **Battle of Hastings**. A site question asks how the place shaped events, and how events connect to it.
- **Location**: a ridge (often called Senlac) about seven miles from Hastings, on the road from London. Harold could block William's route inland.
- **Ground**: the ridge was **narrow**, so the English were packed tightly; the slopes favoured defenders; the ground at the foot was **marshy** and boggy in places, which slowed attackers and caught men chasing downhill.
- **Woodland** behind (the Weald) gave Harold's men cover to gather, but limited room to manoeuvre and to retreat.
- **Afterwards**: William founded **Battle Abbey** on the site as penance for the bloodshed, traditionally with its high altar where Harold fell; the town of Battle grew around it. The abbey is evidence of how the Normans used the Church to mark and justify their victory.

::: terms
- **Witan**: the council of leading Anglo-Saxon nobles and churchmen who advised the king and chose a successor.
- **Claimant**: a person who said they had a right to the English throne in 1066.
- **Housecarls**: professional, well-armed soldiers paid to serve the king or an earl, famous for two-handed axes.
- **Fyrd**: the part-time Anglo-Saxon army of free men, called up for a limited period of service.
- **Shield wall**: a tight line of soldiers with overlapping shields, very hard to break while it held together.
- **Feigned retreat**: a tactic in which cavalry pretend to flee to lure enemies out of formation, then turn on them.
- **Papal banner**: the flag sent by the Pope to William, showing Church support for the invasion.
- **Cavalry**: soldiers who fight on horseback, such as Norman knights with lances.
- **Motte and bailey**: an early Norman castle of an earth mound with a tower, beside a fenced courtyard.
- **Burh**: an Anglo-Saxon fortified town that protected the whole community rather than one lord.
:::

::: example Q1 Interpretation (8)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a modern popular history book. It says: "William did not win the Battle of Hastings so much as Harold lost it. Exhausted by his victory in the north, he rushed south and threw his men into battle before his army was ready. Had he waited a few more weeks, England's shield wall would have held." How convincing is Interpretation A about why William won the Battle of Hastings? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretation A. (8)

Interpretation A is partly convincing. Harold's men **were exhausted**: they had beaten Hardrada at **Stamford Bridge** on 25 September, then marched back south after William landed at **Pevensey** on 28 September. Harold did not wait for the full army, so many of the fyrd and Edwin and Morcar's men were missing.

But the claim that the shield wall "would have held" ignores **William**. He had prepared carefully, with papal backing and a mixed army including **cavalry**. When the Bretons fled he **rallied** his men, and **feigned retreats** drew the English off the ridge. Overall the interpretation is convincing about Harold's weaknesses but overstates them: William's leadership and tactics turned them into victory. (Level 4: claims tested with precise knowledge; sustained judgement.)
:::

::: example Q2 Explain what was important (8)
Explain what was important about the Battle of Stamford Bridge. (8)

Stamford Bridge was important first because it **ended the Norwegian threat**. Harald Hardrada, one of the claimants, and Harold's exiled brother **Tostig** were both killed, so Harold no longer faced a rival from the north and a brother who had been raiding the coast.

Second, it was important because it showed Harold's **strengths**: he marched north quickly with his housecarls and caught the Norwegians by surprise, many without armour. This victory may have made him **confident**, which helps explain why he rushed south to face William.

Third, and most important for what came next, it **weakened** Harold's army. Experienced housecarls were killed or wounded, his men were exhausted, and he was far from the south coast when William landed unopposed at Pevensey three days later. The northern earls Edwin and Morcar, already beaten at Fulford, did not bring their men to Hastings. So a great victory helped cause Harold's defeat. (Level 4: several consequences, explained and linked.)
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of the ways in which William prepared his invasion of England in 1066. (8)

When **Harold was crowned** in January 1066, William argued that **Edward had promised** him the throne and that Harold had **sworn an oath** to support him. Because this made Harold an oath-breaker, William won the **Pope's backing** and a **papal banner**.

This helped him **recruit** knights from Brittany and Flanders, attracted by promises of English land. To carry them and their **horses** he gathered a large **fleet** and kept his army fed and disciplined at the mouth of the Dives for weeks.

Contrary winds then delayed him, with an important consequence: by the time he sailed, Harold had **sent the fyrd home** (8 September) and gone north. So William's preparation meant that when the chance came he could land unopposed at Pevensey and quickly put up **forts**. (Level 4: each stage leads to the next.)
:::

::: example Q4 Site essay (16)
"The Normans conquered England mainly through military power." How far does a study of the White Tower support this statement? Explain your answer. You should refer to the White Tower and your contextual knowledge. (16)

**The White Tower supports the statement strongly.** It is a huge stone **great tower**, begun under William I and finished under William II, in the corner of London's Roman walls beside the Thames. Its thick walls, first-floor entrance and position over the city show a purpose of **military control** of England's largest town. It fits the wider pattern: victory at **Hastings** by knights, archers and infantry, then **motte and bailey castles** across England and ruthless force such as the Harrying of the North.

**But the tower also shows that conquest needed more than force.** Its size and Caen stone from Normandy made it a **symbol** that worked by **intimidation**. Its **chapel of St John** presented Norman rule as God's will, as the **papal banner** had in 1066. It was a royal **residence** and centre of government, linked to William's use of **Anglo-Saxon institutions** such as sheriffs and writs.

**Judgement.** Without battle and castles William could not have won or held England, so the statement is largely supported; but the tower shows force was combined with **symbolism, religion and government**, which turned conquest into lasting control. (Level 4.)
:::
