## What it is

This lesson covers **Part four: Equality and rights** (c1900 to the present): the campaign for **women's suffrage** and the reasons for votes for women in **1918** and **1928**, with progress towards equality after 1945; **workers' rights**, including the **General Strike** (1926) and trade union reform in the late 20th century; and **minority rights** in a multi-racial Britain since the Second World War, including the **Brixton riots** (1981) and the **Scarman Report**.

The big idea: by 1900 most men could vote, but **women**, many **workers** and, later, **ethnic minorities** still lacked equal rights. In the 20th century they won them through campaigns, protest, strikes and changes in the law. Governments responded with a mix of **repression and reform**, and **war** often changed what seemed possible.

### 1. The campaign for women's suffrage

**Why women wanted the vote:** they paid taxes and obeyed laws they had no say in; more were educated and working, and some could already vote in local elections. **Against:** many claimed women belonged in the home ("separate spheres"); some women opposed it too; politicians worried how women would vote.

| | **Suffragists** | **Suffragettes** |
|---|---|---|
| **Organisation** | **NUWSS**, formed **1897**, led by **Millicent Fawcett** | **WSPU**, formed **1903** in Manchester by **Emmeline Pankhurst**, with her daughters **Christabel** and **Sylvia** |
| **Members** | Mostly middle-class, men and women, with branches across the country; the largest group | Women only; smaller, tightly led by the Pankhursts |
| **Methods** | **Peaceful**: petitions, meetings, leaflets, lobbying MPs, large marches; later supported Labour candidates who backed votes for women | **"Deeds not words"**: heckling politicians, then from about 1912 window-smashing, arson, attacks on post boxes and property, chaining to railings; **hunger strikes** in prison |
| **Strengths and weaknesses** | Respectable and persistent, but easy to ignore | Huge publicity, but violence alienated some MPs and gave opponents an excuse |

The WSPU split over the Pankhursts' leadership; **Sylvia** broke away to campaign among working-class women in **East London**.

**Key events and government responses:**
- Imprisoned hunger strikers were **force-fed**, which many found shocking. The **Cat and Mouse Act** (**1913**) let them be released when weak and **re-arrested** once recovered, avoiding deaths that would create martyrs.
- **Emily Wilding Davison** died after running onto the course at the **Epsom Derby** in **June 1913**; her funeral became a huge public procession.
- Prime Minister **Asquith** opposed votes for women, and several bills that would have given some women the vote failed.

**The First World War (1914–18):** the WSPU **suspended** militancy and **Emmeline and Christabel** backed the war effort; the NUWSS campaigned quietly. Women took on men's jobs in **munitions**, transport, farming and offices.

**Why women got the vote in 1918:** women's **war work** changed attitudes; a new Act was needed anyway because many **soldiers** had lost the vote by being away, which gave a chance to include women; **Lloyd George**, more sympathetic than Asquith, became Prime Minister in **1916**; politicians wanted to avoid a **return of militancy**; and decades of **campaigning** had made the issue impossible to ignore.

**The 1918 Representation of the People Act:** votes for **all men over 21** and for **women over 30** who met a property qualification (or whose husbands did). Women could stand as **MPs**; **Nancy Astor** was the first to take her seat (1919). Women were kept off equal terms partly so they would not be a majority of voters.

**The 1928 Equal Franchise Act:** women over **21** gained the vote on the same terms as men.

**Progress towards equality after 1945:**
- After war work again in the **Second World War**, many women returned to lower-paid jobs; the **women's liberation movement** of the late 1960s and 1970s campaigned on pay, work and education.
- In **1968**, women **sewing machinists at Ford's Dagenham** factory went on strike over pay grading; their action helped push the **Equal Pay Act** (**1970**), requiring equal pay for the same work.
- The **Sex Discrimination Act** (**1975**) banned discrimination in jobs, education and services, and set up the **Equal Opportunities Commission**.
- Gaps remained: women were still under-represented in Parliament and senior jobs, and on average paid less.

### 2. Workers' rights: the General Strike (1926)

**Causes in the coal industry:**
- Coal was in **decline** after the First World War (old pits, foreign competition, falling exports); wartime government control ended in 1921, returning the mines to private **owners**.
- In **1925** a return to the **gold standard** made British exports dearer. Owners demanded **wage cuts** and **longer hours**. The miners' leader **A. J. Cook** answered with the slogan "Not a penny off the pay, not a minute on the day".
- On **"Red Friday"** (July 1925), facing a TUC threat, the government gave a temporary **subsidy** and set up the **Samuel Commission**, which in 1926 accepted wage cuts and an end to the subsidy.
- When miners refused, owners **locked them out** (1 May 1926), and the **TUC** called a **General Strike**, which began at midnight on **3 May 1926**.

**Events:** well over a million workers in key industries (transport, docks, printing, iron and steel, power) stopped work. Most were peaceful, though there were some clashes.

**The government's preparations and reactions:** Prime Minister **Stanley Baldwin** had used the subsidy period to **prepare**. The **Organisation for the Maintenance of Supplies** had recruited **volunteers**, many middle-class students, to drive buses and trains and unload food. **Troops** escorted convoys. The government published its own newspaper, the **British Gazette** (edited by **Winston Churchill**), and the **BBC** broadcast news that largely followed the government's line; the TUC replied with the **British Worker**. Baldwin presented the strike as an attack on the **constitution**.

**End:** the TUC called off the strike on **12 May**, after nine days, without guarantees for the miners. The miners stayed out for months and returned defeated, on **lower wages** and **longer hours**.

**Impact:** the **Trade Disputes and Trade Unions Act** (**1927**) made **general and sympathy strikes illegal** and made it harder for unions to fund the **Labour Party**. Union membership fell. But the strike was peaceful, and many workers turned to **political action** through Labour instead (the Act was repealed in 1946).

### 3. Trade union reform in the late 20th century

- Strikes in the 1970s, including the **"Winter of Discontent"** (1978–79), turned many voters against powerful unions.
- **Margaret Thatcher's** Conservative governments (from **1979**) passed a series of laws in the 1980s: **secret ballots** were required before strikes; **secondary picketing** (picketing a workplace not directly involved) was banned; the **closed shop** (compulsory union membership) was restricted; unions could be **sued** for damages from unlawful strikes.
- **The miners' strike (1984–85):** the **NUM**, led by **Arthur Scargill**, struck against planned **pit closures**, without a national ballot. The government had **stockpiled coal**, and police were used on a huge scale; there were violent clashes, such as at **Orgreave** (1984). After about a year the miners returned to work **defeated**, and many pits closed.
- **Impact:** union membership and power fell sharply. Supporters said the laws gave members a democratic voice; critics said they weakened workers' ability to defend jobs.

### 4. Minority rights since the Second World War

**Post-war migration:**
- In **June 1948** the **Empire Windrush** brought several hundred people from the **Caribbean** to Britain. The **British Nationality Act** (**1948**) confirmed that people from the colonies and Commonwealth were British citizens with the right to settle.
- **Labour shortages** led the **NHS**, London Transport and factories to recruit; migrants from **India**, **Pakistan** and later **East Africa** followed.

**Discrimination:** migrants faced a **"colour bar"** from landlords, employers, pubs and clubs, and violence such as the attacks in **Notting Hill** and **Nottingham** (**1958**). From **1962** laws restricted Commonwealth immigration.

**Protest and reform:**
- **The Bristol Bus Boycott** (**1963**): the Bristol Omnibus Company refused to employ Black or Asian bus crews. Local campaigners, including **Paul Stephenson**, organised a **boycott** of the buses, supported by students and some politicians. After several months the company **backed down**.
- **Race Relations Acts:**

| Act | Change |
|---|---|
| **1965** | Banned discrimination in **public places** (such as pubs and hotels); set up the Race Relations Board. Did not cover housing or jobs |
| **1968** | Extended the ban to **housing and employment** |
| **1976** | Covered **indirect** discrimination too, and set up the **Commission for Racial Equality** with stronger powers |

**The Brixton riots (April 1981):**
- **Causes:** high **unemployment** among young Black people; poor housing and deprivation in Brixton, south London; anger at police use of **"sus" laws** to stop and search young Black men; a large police operation in early April (**Swamp 81**) in which many people were stopped and searched.
- **Events:** a confrontation over an injured young Black man led to a weekend of rioting; many were injured and buildings burned. Riots followed that summer in other cities, such as Toxteth in Liverpool.
- **The Scarman Report** (November **1981**): **Lord Scarman** found that **racial disadvantage** and deprivation, and heavy-handed **policing**, had caused the riots. He recommended recruiting more **ethnic minority** police officers, better training, consulting local communities, and controls on stop and search. He did **not** find that the police were institutionally racist.
- **Impact:** the "sus" law was abolished (1981) and the **Police and Criminal Evidence Act** (**1984**) set rules for stop and search. Critics said change was slow: the **Macpherson Report** (**1999**), after the murder of **Stephen Lawrence**, found the Metropolitan Police **institutionally racist**, which led to further reform.

::: terms
- **Suffragists**: members of the NUWSS, led by Millicent Fawcett, who campaigned peacefully for votes for women.
- **Suffragettes**: members of the WSPU, founded by Emmeline Pankhurst in 1903, who used militant direct action.
- **Cat and Mouse Act**: the 1913 law letting hunger-striking suffragettes be released when weak and re-arrested when well.
- **Representation of the People Act 1918**: the law giving the vote to all men over 21 and to women over 30 who met a property qualification.
- **Equal Franchise Act 1928**: the law giving women the vote on the same terms as men, from age 21.
- **General Strike**: the nine-day strike in May 1926 by workers in key industries in support of the locked-out miners.
- **Trade Disputes Act 1927**: the law making general and sympathy strikes illegal after the General Strike.
- **Secondary picketing**: picketing a workplace not directly involved in a dispute, banned in the 1980s.
- **Colour bar**: discrimination against Black and Asian people in housing, jobs and public places.
- **Bristol Bus Boycott**: the 1963 boycott that ended a bus company's refusal to employ Black and Asian crews.
- **Race Relations Acts**: the laws of 1965, 1968 and 1976 that banned racial discrimination in more and more areas of life.
- **Scarman Report**: Lord Scarman's 1981 inquiry into the Brixton riots, blaming racial disadvantage and policing methods.

::: example Q1 Source utility (8)
Source A, written for this practice question, is a description of a poster published by the WSPU in 1913. It shows a thin woman in prison clothes being released by a grinning cat in a police helmet, while a second picture shows the cat pouncing on her again once she has recovered. How useful is Source A to an historian studying government responses to the suffragettes? Explain your answer using Source A and your contextual knowledge. (8)

Source A is useful because it shows the **Cat and Mouse Act** (**1913**). Hunger-striking suffragettes were released when they became weak, then **re-arrested** once they recovered, so that the government could avoid deaths in prison. The thin prisoner reflects the effects of **hunger strikes** and **force-feeding**.

Its **provenance** is important. The **WSPU** published it in 1913, at the height of militancy, as **propaganda**: it presents the government as cruel and playful, and the suffragette as a helpless victim, to win public sympathy. It omits why so many suffragettes were in prison: **arson** and **window-smashing**.

So it is very useful for **how the suffragettes portrayed the government** and for one key response, but it is one-sided. (Level 4.)

::: example Q2 Significance of the Brixton riots (8)
Explain the significance of the Brixton riots. (8)

**At the time**, the riots of **April 1981** were significant because they showed the anger of young Black people in Brixton at **unemployment**, poor housing and policing, especially the use of **"sus" laws** and the mass stop and search operation of **Swamp 81**. The violence shocked the country, and further riots followed that summer in other cities.

They led to the **Scarman Report** (1981), which blamed **racial disadvantage** and heavy-handed policing. Its recommendations, such as recruiting more ethnic minority officers and community consultation, changed policing. The **sus law** was abolished and the **Police and Criminal Evidence Act** (1984) set rules for stop and search.

**Over time**, their significance is mixed. Scarman denied **institutional racism**, and the **Macpherson Report** (1999) later found the Metropolitan Police institutionally racist, suggesting earlier reforms had not gone far enough. But Brixton marked the point when **race relations** and policing became central national issues. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Similarity (8)
Explain two ways in which the General Strike and the Peasants' Revolt were similar. (8)

**First, both were caused by economic grievances about pay.** In 1381, peasants were angry that the **Statute of Labourers** held down wages after the Black Death, and the **poll tax** was the trigger. In 1926, mine owners demanded **wage cuts** and **longer hours** because coal was in decline, and the TUC called a General Strike to support the **locked-out miners**.

**Second, both ended in short-term defeat after the authorities outmanoeuvred the protesters.** In 1381 **Richard II** made promises at Mile End, then revoked them and executed the leaders. In 1926 **Baldwin's** government had prepared with volunteers and propaganda (the British Gazette), and the TUC called off the strike after **nine days**; the miners returned on worse terms, and the **1927 Act** banned general strikes. In both cases, the authorities restored control. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Factor essay (16)
Has war been the main factor in the development of rights in Britain? Explain your answer with reference to war and other factors. Use a range of examples from across your study of Power and the people: c1170 to the present day. (16)

**War** often created the conditions for change: war taxes provoked **Magna Carta** and the 1381 revolt, and the Civil War produced the Levellers' radical ideas. Most importantly, the **First World War** helped bring the **1918 Act**: women's war work changed attitudes, and the need to give returning soldiers the vote gave a chance to include women. The Second World War's labour shortages encouraged **Commonwealth migration**.

But war rarely won rights by itself. **Campaigning** by the **suffragists** and **suffragettes** for decades made votes for women a live issue; the **Chartists** and the **Reform Acts** extended male voting in peacetime. **The economy** drove workers' movements, from the dockers' strike to the **General Strike**. **Ideas** of equality gave campaigns their aims, and **government** passed the Equal Pay Act (1970), Sex Discrimination Act (1975) and Race Relations Acts in peacetime, often after protests such as the **Bristol Bus Boycott**.

**Judgement.** War was a powerful **trigger** that sped up change, especially in 1918, but it was not the main factor overall: most rights were won through long **campaigns** and **ideas** of equality, and turned into law by government in peacetime. (Level 4.)

::: watch
- **Saying war alone won the vote.** War work mattered, but so did decades of campaigning, the need to reform soldiers' votes and a change of Prime Minister.
- **Forgetting the limits of 1918.** Women had to be over 30 and meet a property qualification; equal votes came in 1928.
- **Calling the General Strike a success.** It lasted nine days, the miners were defeated, and the 1927 Act limited unions.
- **Treating minority rights as only Brixton.** Use Windrush, the colour bar, the Bristol Bus Boycott and the Race Relations Acts.
- **Mixing up Scarman and Macpherson.** Scarman (1981) denied institutional racism; Macpherson (1999) found it.
