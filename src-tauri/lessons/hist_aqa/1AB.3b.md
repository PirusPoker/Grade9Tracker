## What it is

This lesson covers the second bullet of Part three, **social policy and practice**: the **reasons for** Nazi policies, and their practices and impact on **women**, **young people and youth groups**, **education**, the **control of churches and religion**, and **Aryan ideas, racial policy and persecution**, ending with **the Final Solution**.

The big idea: Nazi social policy aimed to build a **Volksgemeinschaft** (people's community): a racially "pure", obedient nation, ready for war, in which every group had a fixed role. Those who did not fit were excluded, persecuted and, in the end, murdered.

### 1. Reasons for Nazi social policies

- **Racial ideas**: belief in an **Aryan** "master race" that had to be kept "pure" and made to grow.
- **Volksgemeinschaft**: loyalty to the nation and the Führer above class, religion or family.
- **Preparing for war**: healthy, obedient young men to fight; women to produce the next generation.
- **Control**: removing every rival source of loyalty, such as churches, independent youth groups and schools.

### 2. Women

**Ideal:** the "three Ks", **Kinder, Küche, Kirche** (children, kitchen, church). Women were to be mothers and homemakers, not workers or politicians.

| Policy | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Law for the Encouragement of Marriage (1933)** | Loans to newly married couples if the wife left work; a quarter of the loan was cancelled for each child born |
| **Mother's Cross** (from the late 1930s) | Bronze, silver and gold medals for mothers of four or more, six or more, and eight or more children |
| **Pushing women out of professions** | Women doctors, teachers and civil servants were dismissed or discouraged; from 1936 women could not be judges or lawyers |
| **Lebensborn (1935)** | An SS programme to increase "racially pure" births, including homes for unmarried mothers |
| **Image and appearance** | Make-up, trousers and smoking discouraged; the German Women's Enterprise ran classes on homecraft |

**Change after 1937.** Rearmament created a **labour shortage**, so the regime reversed course: the requirement for wives to give up work was dropped from marriage loans in 1937, and the number of women in work actually **rose** in the late 1930s. In the war, the Nazis were reluctant to conscript women, but from 1943 women had to register for work.

**Impact:** the birth rate rose in the 1930s, though economic recovery helped too. Many women welcomed the honour given to motherhood; professional women lost careers and freedoms.

### 3. Young people, youth groups and education

**Youth groups.**

| Age | Boys | Girls |
|---|---|---|
| 10–14 | German Young People | Young Girls' League |
| 14–18 | **Hitler Youth** | **League of German Maidens (BDM)** |

- Led by **Baldur von Schirach**. A law of **1936** made the Hitler Youth the official youth organisation and other youth groups were banned (except, at first, Catholic ones); membership became **compulsory in 1939**. Millions were members by the late 1930s.
- **Boys**: camping, hiking, sport, marching, shooting and map-reading: preparation for the army; ideology and loyalty to Hitler.
- **Girls**: fitness, homecraft and preparation for motherhood, plus ideology.
- **Impact:** many enjoyed the activities, the comradeship and the sense of importance. Others resented the drill and the control; attendance fell in the late 1930s, and groups such as the **Edelweiss Pirates** and **Swing Youth** rejected it (lesson 1AB.3c).

**Education.**
- **Teachers** had to join the **National Socialist Teachers' League**; Jewish and politically unreliable teachers were sacked.
- **Curriculum**: more **PE** to build fitness; **race studies**; **history** rewritten to glorify Germany and blame the Jews and the "November criminals"; **biology** teaching racial ideas; maths problems with Nazi themes; religious education reduced; girls taught domestic science.
- **Textbooks** were rewritten and checked; *Mein Kampf* became a key text.
- **Elite schools**: the **Napolas** and **Adolf Hitler Schools** trained future leaders.
- From November 1938 Jewish children were banned from German schools.

### 4. The churches

Germany was a Christian country, about two-thirds Protestant and one-third Catholic. Christianity's values of love and mercy clashed with Nazi ideas, and the churches were a rival loyalty.

| Policy | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Concordat (July 1933)** | Agreement with the Pope: the Catholic Church would stay out of politics; the Nazis would leave Catholic schools and worship alone. Hitler soon broke it: Catholic youth groups were closed, priests arrested and church schools taken over. In 1937 the Pope issued a letter criticising Nazi policies, read out in Catholic churches |
| **The Reich Church (1933)** | Protestant churches were united under a pro-Nazi **Reich Bishop, Ludwig Müller**; the "German Christians" combined Christianity with Nazi ideas |
| **The Confessing Church (1934)** | Protestants who refused to accept Nazi control. Its leader **Pastor Martin Niemöller** was arrested in 1937 and held in concentration camps until 1945. Hundreds of pastors were arrested |
| **German Faith Movement** | A Nazi-backed pagan alternative to Christianity, with few followers |

**Impact:** the churches were weakened but **not destroyed**. Most clergy and churchgoers conformed; a minority resisted. Catholic Bishop **von Galen** publicly condemned the killing of disabled people in 1941.

### 5. Aryan ideas, racial policy and persecution

Nazi racial ideas ranked people: **Aryans** at the top, **Jews** at the bottom as the supposed enemy behind communism, capitalism and defeat in 1918. Also targeted: **Roma and Sinti**, **disabled people**, **gay men**, **Jehovah's Witnesses** and the "asocial" (e.g. the homeless and alcoholics).

| Date | Step |
|---|---|
| **1 April 1933** | **Boycott of Jewish shops and businesses**, enforced by the SA |
| April 1933 | Jews dismissed from the civil service, then other professions |
| July 1933 | **Sterilisation law**: hundreds of thousands of people with disabilities or illnesses thought hereditary were sterilised |
| **September 1935** | **Nuremberg Laws**: Jews lost German citizenship; marriage and sexual relations between Jews and non-Jews were banned |
| **9–10 November 1938** | **Kristallnacht** ("night of broken glass"): after a Jewish student shot a German diplomat in Paris, Nazis attacked Jewish shops, homes and synagogues across Germany; around 100 Jews were killed and about 30,000 Jewish men sent to concentration camps; the Jewish community was then fined for the damage |
| **1939 onwards** | The **T4 programme**: the systematic murder of disabled people, tens of thousands killed by 1941 |

### 6. The Final Solution

- **Ghettos.** After the invasion of Poland (1939), Jews were forced into sealed **ghettos** such as Warsaw and Łódź, where many died of starvation and disease.
- **Einsatzgruppen.** After the invasion of the USSR (June 1941), SS killing squads shot Jews and others in mass executions; over a million people were murdered this way.
- **The Wannsee Conference (20 January 1942).** Senior Nazi officials met under **Reinhard Heydrich** to coordinate the "Final Solution to the Jewish question": the murder of all Europe's Jews.
- **Death camps.** Camps such as **Auschwitz-Birkenau**, **Treblinka** and **Sobibor** in occupied Poland were used to murder people on an industrial scale, mainly in gas chambers.
- About **six million Jews** were murdered in the **Holocaust**, along with hundreds of thousands of Roma and Sinti and many others.

The persecution moved step by step, from exclusion (1933) to legal separation (1935) to violence (1938) to genocide (1941–45). Exams often ask how and why it escalated: ideology was constant, while war, conquest of large Jewish populations and the removal of restraints made genocide possible.

::: terms
- **Volksgemeinschaft**: the "people's community", the Nazi ideal of a united, racially pure nation loyal to the Führer.
- **Kinder, Küche, Kirche**: "children, kitchen, church", the slogan summing up the Nazi ideal role for women.
- **Hitler Youth**: the Nazi youth organisation for boys, made the official youth group in 1936 and compulsory in 1939.
- **League of German Maidens (BDM)**: the Nazi organisation for girls aged 14–18, focused on fitness, homecraft and motherhood.
- **Concordat**: the 1933 agreement between Hitler and the Pope, which the Nazis soon broke.
- **Confessing Church**: the Protestant church formed in 1934 by pastors such as Niemöller who rejected Nazi control.
- **Aryan**: the Nazi term for the supposed racially superior people of northern Europe.
- **Nuremberg Laws**: the laws of 1935 that took citizenship from Jews and banned marriage between Jews and non-Jews.
- **Kristallnacht**: the violent attacks on Jewish property, synagogues and people on 9–10 November 1938.
- **Wannsee Conference**: the meeting of January 1942 that coordinated the plan to murder all of Europe's Jews.
- **Final Solution**: the Nazi term for the systematic genocide of the Jews of Europe.
- **Holocaust**: the murder of about six million Jews by the Nazis and their collaborators.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 Section A at a glance.** About an hour, 40 marks: Q1 differ (4), Q2 why differ (4), Q3 more convincing (8), Q4 describe two (4), Q5 in what ways (8), Q6 which bullet (12). This bullet produces "in what ways were women / young people affected", "describe two ways the Nazis controlled the churches" and "women or young people: which group was more affected".

1. **Q1 (4): content only.** E.g. one interpretation says the Hitler Youth was fun and exciting, the other that it was boring and oppressive.
2. **Q2 (4): provenance + context.** A former BDM leader with happy memories; a Jewish pupil expelled in 1938; a historian with membership figures. Explain how experience and purpose shape the view.
3. **Q3 (8): test both.** Use precise policies and their limits (the 1937 U-turn on women; falling Hitler Youth attendance; the broken Concordat). Judge which fits more of the evidence.
4. **Q4 (4): two features with precise detail.**
5. **Q5 (8): several effects; Level 4 shows differences** between groups (professional women versus mothers; enthusiastic versus resistant youth; Catholics versus Protestants) or changes over time (women before and after 1937).
6. **Q6 (12): explain both, then judge** using a criterion, e.g. how deeply lives were changed, or how successfully the Nazis achieved their aims.
- Write about persecution and the Holocaust with **precise, sober** facts and dates.
:::

::: example Q1 and Q2: interpretations on the Hitler Youth (4 + 4)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a man who was in the Hitler Youth, writing his memoirs in 1982: "For a boy from a poor street it was paradise. Camping by the lakes, singing round the fire, the feeling that we mattered and that Germany needed us. No one forced me. I went because I loved it." Interpretation B, also written for practice, is in the style of a historian writing in 2013: "By the late 1930s many teenagers found the Hitler Youth dull and bossy. Endless drill and lectures drove some away, and the authorities had to make membership compulsory to keep the ranks full." How does Interpretation B differ from Interpretation A about young people's attitudes to the Hitler Youth? (4)

A says young people **joined willingly and loved it**, enjoying camping, comradeship and a sense of importance (1–2). B says many found it **boring and oppressive**, with drill and lectures driving them away (+1), so that membership had to be made compulsory (+1). Level 2, 4/4.

Why might the authors have a different interpretation? (4)

The author of A was a member from a poor background, so the free activities were a real change for him, and as an old man he may remember his youth with nostalgia (1–2). The historian in B is studying the movement as a whole, including the late 1930s when attendance fell and the 1939 law made membership compulsory, and evidence of groups like the Edelweiss Pirates (+1–2). Level 2, 4/4.
:::

::: example Q4 Describe two (4)
Describe two ways the Nazis tried to control the churches. (4)

**The Concordat.** In July 1933 Hitler agreed with the Pope that the Catholic Church would stay out of politics in return for freedom of worship and its schools (1). The Nazis soon broke it, closing Catholic youth groups and arresting priests (1).

**The Reich Church.** In 1933 the Nazis united the Protestant churches into a Reich Church under the pro-Nazi Reich Bishop Ludwig Müller (1). Pastors who refused, such as Niemöller, formed the Confessing Church, and Niemöller was arrested in 1937 (1).
:::

::: example Q5 In what ways (8)
In what ways were the lives of women affected by Nazi policies? Explain your answer. (8)

**Family and motherhood.** The Nazis promoted Kinder, Küche, Kirche. Marriage loans from 1933 were given if the wife left work, with a quarter cancelled per child; the Mother's Cross honoured mothers of large families. The birth rate rose in the 1930s.

**Work and careers.** Women doctors, teachers and civil servants were pushed out, and from 1936 women could not be judges or lawyers. Professional women lost their careers.

**Freedom and appearance.** Make-up, trousers and smoking were discouraged; women were excluded from politics; Lebensborn treated some women as breeders for the SS.

**Complexity (Level 4).** The effects differed and changed. Many mothers welcomed the honour given to the home, while educated women lost most. And policy changed: from 1937 labour shortages forced the regime to drop the marriage-loan condition and more women went out to work, and in 1943 women had to register for war work. Ideology gave way to the needs of rearmament and war.
:::

::: example Q6 Which group was more affected (12)
Which of the following groups was more affected by Nazi policies: young people; women? Explain your answer with reference to both bullet points. (12)

**Young people.** Their whole lives were reshaped. Schools had Nazi teachers, rewritten textbooks, race studies, more PE and rewritten history; elite schools trained leaders. The Hitler Youth and BDM, official from 1936 and compulsory from 1939, filled their free time with drill, camps and ideology. Boys were prepared for war, girls for motherhood.

**Women.** Policy aimed to send them home: marriage loans, the Mother's Cross, dismissal from professions. But after 1937 labour shortages reversed this, and women's employment rose. Many women's daily lives as mothers changed little.

**Judgement.** Young people were more affected, because the Nazis controlled both their schooling and their leisure for years at a time, and aimed to change how they thought, not only what they did. Women's policies were inconsistent and partly abandoned when the economy needed workers. But the effect on young people was also limited: some rejected the movement, as the Edelweiss Pirates and Swing Youth showed. (Level 4.)
:::

::: q
What were the Nuremberg Laws (1935)? (2)
---
The Reich Citizenship Law took German citizenship away from Jews (1); the Law for the Protection of German Blood and Honour banned marriage and sexual relations between Jews and non-Jews (1).
:::

::: q
Give two ways the school curriculum changed under the Nazis. (2)
---
Any two (1 each): more PE; race studies introduced; history rewritten to glorify Germany and blame Jews and the November criminals; biology taught racial ideas; maths problems with Nazi themes; religious education reduced; girls taught domestic science.
:::

::: q
Why did Nazi policy towards women change after 1937? (3)
---
Rearmament created a labour shortage (1); the economy needed more workers, so women were needed in factories (1); so the marriage-loan condition was dropped and women's employment rose (1).
:::

::: q
Explain what happened on Kristallnacht and why it was significant. (4)
---
On 9–10 November 1938, after a German diplomat was shot in Paris, Nazis attacked Jewish shops, homes and synagogues across Germany (1); around 100 Jews were killed and about 30,000 men sent to camps (1). It was significant as the first large-scale, state-organised violence against Jews in Germany (1), followed by the fine on the Jewish community and the exclusion of Jews from economic life and schools (1).
:::

::: q

---
A Protestant pastor and leader of the Confessing Church, which rejected Nazi control (1); arrested in 1937 and held in concentration camps until 1945 (1).
:::

::: q
What was decided at the Wannsee Conference in January 1942? (2)
---
Senior Nazi officials under Heydrich coordinated the "Final Solution" (1): the systematic murder of all Europe's Jews, organised through deportation to death camps (1).

::: watch
- **Saying the Hitler Youth was compulsory from 1933.** It became the official youth organisation in 1936; membership was compulsory from 1939.
- **Saying all women stopped working.** The number of women in work rose after 1937; show the change over time.
- **Treating the churches as crushed.** They were weakened and divided, not destroyed; most clergy conformed and a minority resisted.
- **Jumbling the steps of persecution.** Boycott (1933), Nuremberg Laws (1935), Kristallnacht (1938), ghettos and Einsatzgruppen (1939–41), Wannsee (1942), death camps.
- **Leaving out other persecuted groups.** Roma and Sinti, disabled people (sterilisation, T4), gay men and Jehovah's Witnesses are part of racial policy.
