## What it is

This lesson covers the third bullet of Part two: **the impact of the Second World War** on America: **America's economic recovery**, **Lend Lease**, **exports**, and **social developments, including the experiences of African Americans and women**.

The big idea: where the New Deal had brought relief but not full recovery, the war brought **full employment** and a booming economy. It also opened new opportunities for African Americans and women, but those changes were **limited**: segregation continued, and many women were pushed out of their jobs in 1945. The war raised expectations that would shape the civil rights and women's movements later.

### 1. From neutrality to war

- When war began in Europe in **September 1939**, most Americans wanted to stay out (**isolationism**). Neutrality laws from the 1930s banned selling weapons to countries at war.
- **Cash and carry (November 1939):** Britain and France could buy American weapons if they paid cash and carried them in their own ships. Orders flowed into American factories.
- **Lend Lease (March 1941):** when Britain ran out of money, Congress allowed the President to **lend or lease** weapons, ships, aircraft, food and raw materials to countries whose defence was vital to the USA. Britain was the main receiver; the USSR was added after Germany invaded it in June 1941. Roosevelt called the USA the **"arsenal of democracy"**. Over the war, Lend Lease supplies were worth tens of billions of dollars.
- **Pearl Harbor (7 December 1941):** Japan attacked the American fleet in Hawaii, and the USA entered the war.

### 2. Economic recovery

| Change | Detail |
|---|---|
| **War production** | Factories switched to tanks, ships, aircraft and munitions. Car plants built military vehicles and planes. The USA built around 300,000 aircraft during the war |
| **Government spending** | Federal spending rose to levels far above the New Deal's; the government signed huge contracts with private firms. The War Production Board (1942) directed industry |
| **Full employment** | Unemployment, still in the millions in 1940, fell to around 1 per cent by 1944. Millions of men joined the forces (over 15 million served), creating labour shortages |
| **Exports** | Lend Lease and cash-and-carry orders, then supplies for allies, made the USA the world's biggest exporter of weapons and food |
| **Farmers** | Demand for food rose and farm prices and incomes recovered |
| **Wages and savings** | Wages rose and there was plenty of overtime; with rationing and few consumer goods, Americans **saved** heavily, which later fuelled the post-war consumer boom |
| **Long-term** | The national economy roughly doubled in size during the war; the USA ended it as the world's richest and strongest power |

**Why this matters for exam judgements:** the war, not the New Deal alone, ended the Depression. But the New Deal had built some foundations (dams and power from the TVA, roads, a stronger role for federal government), so the two can be linked.

### 3. African Americans

| Opportunities and progress | Limits and continued discrimination |
|---|---|
| **War work and migration:** over a million more African Americans moved from the South to northern and western cities for jobs in war industries | They met hostility over jobs and housing; race riots broke out, most seriously in **Detroit in 1943** |
| **Executive Order 8802 (1941):** after **A. Philip Randolph** threatened a mass march on Washington, Roosevelt banned discrimination in defence industries and set up a **Fair Employment Practices Committee** | Enforcement was weak, and many employers still discriminated |
| **About a million African Americans served** in the armed forces; units such as the **Tuskegee Airmen** (fighter pilots) won respect | The **armed forces stayed segregated**; many Black troops were given labour and supply roles; the Red Cross even separated blood donations by race |
| **The Double V campaign (1942):** launched by an African American newspaper, the *Pittsburgh Courier*: **victory over fascism abroad and over racism at home** | Jim Crow laws continued in the South; veterans returning home in uniform were sometimes attacked |
| Membership of the **NAACP** grew hugely; the **Congress of Racial Equality (CORE)** was founded in 1942 and tried non-violent protests | Real legal change mostly came later |

**Significance:** fighting a war against Nazi racism exposed the hypocrisy of segregation at home. After the war, President Truman ordered the **desegregation of the armed forces (1948)**. The war raised expectations and built organisations that fed the civil rights movement of the 1950s.

### 4. Women

| Opportunities and progress | Limits |
|---|---|
| About **six million more women** entered paid work during the war, many in heavy industry such as shipyards and aircraft factories | Women were usually paid less than men for similar work |
| **"Rosie the Riveter"**: a famous image of a confident woman war worker used to recruit women into industry | Many jobs were seen as "for the duration" only; childcare was scarce |
| Hundreds of thousands of women served in uniform in auxiliary services such as the Women's Army Corps, as nurses, clerks, drivers and pilots ferrying aircraft | Women were not allowed in combat roles |
| Women gained skills, confidence, independence and wages of their own | After 1945 many women were dismissed or encouraged to leave so returning servicemen could have jobs; propaganda stressed home and family again |

**Long term:** the number of women in work fell after 1945 but stayed higher than before the war, and more married women went on working. The memory of wartime work and its loss later helped the feminist movement of the 1960s.

::: terms
- **Isolationism**: the policy of keeping out of other countries' affairs and wars, popular in the USA in the 1930s.
- **Cash and carry**: the 1939 policy letting Britain and France buy American weapons if they paid cash and transported them themselves.
- **Lend Lease**: the 1941 law allowing the USA to lend or lease war supplies to countries whose defence mattered to American security.
- **Arsenal of democracy**: Roosevelt's phrase for the USA's role in supplying weapons to the countries fighting the Axis.
- **Full employment**: a situation where almost everyone who wants a job has one, as in the USA by 1944.
- **Executive Order 8802**: Roosevelt's 1941 order banning racial discrimination in defence industries.
- **Fair Employment Practices Committee**: the body set up in 1941 to investigate discrimination in war industries.
- **Double V campaign**: the 1942 African American campaign for victory over fascism abroad and racism at home.
- **Tuskegee Airmen**: the African American fighter pilots who served in segregated units in the Second World War.
- **Rosie the Riveter**: the image of a female war worker used to encourage women into industry.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 Section A at a glance.** About an hour, 40 marks: Q1 differ (4), Q2 why differ (4), Q3 more convincing (8), Q4 describe two (4), Q5 in what ways (8), Q6 which bullet (12). Typical questions: "In what ways were African Americans affected by the Second World War?"; "Which did more to end the Depression: the New Deal or the Second World War?"; interpretations on how far women's lives changed.

1. **Q1 (4): content only.** E.g. one interpretation says the war transformed women's lives, the other that change was temporary.
2. **Q2 (4): provenance + context.** A riveter remembering her wartime job; a government recruiting campaign; a Black soldier who served in a segregated unit; a historian with employment figures: explain how each author's position shapes the view.
3. **Q3 (8): test both views.** Six million more women at work; Rosie the Riveter **against** lower pay and dismissals after 1945. Executive Order 8802 and Double V **against** segregated forces and Detroit 1943. Judge whether change was lasting.
4. **Q4 (4): two features with precise detail.**
5. **Q5 (8): Level 4 shows how effects differed** (North and South; during and after the war; men and women) or how wartime change led to later change.
6. **Q6 (12): explain both bullets, then judge.** On recovery, set the New Deal's relief and infrastructure against the war's full employment, and decide which mattered more and why.
:::

::: example Q1 How interpretations differ (4)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a woman who worked in an aircraft factory, interviewed in 1984: "The war changed everything for me. I learned to rivet, I earned my own money, and I found out I could do anything a man could do. I was never the same again." Interpretation B, also written for practice, is in the style of a historian writing in 2009: "Wartime work was a temporary emergency, not a revolution. Women were paid less, told the jobs were only for the duration, and pushed back into the home when the men returned." How does Interpretation B differ from Interpretation A about the effect of the Second World War on women? (4)

A says the war **transformed** women's lives, giving them skills, independence and confidence (1–2). B says the change was **temporary**: women were paid less and pushed back into the home after 1945 (+1). So A sees a lasting personal change, B a short-term emergency measure that did not change women's position (+1). Level 2, 4/4.
:::

::: example Q3 Which is more convincing (8)
Using Interpretations A and B above: which interpretation gives the more convincing opinion about the effect of the Second World War on women? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretations A and B. (8)

**A is convincing.** About six million more women went into paid work, many in heavy industry such as shipyards and aircraft plants; Rosie the Riveter became a symbol of their ability. Hundreds of thousands served in uniform. Many, like the author, gained confidence and earned their own wages.

**B is convincing.** Women were usually paid less than men; jobs were described as "for the duration"; after 1945 many were dismissed to make way for returning servicemen, and propaganda again stressed the home.

**Judgement.** B is more convincing about women's position in society in the short term, since most lost the skilled jobs. But A is more convincing about the long term: the number of women in work stayed above the pre-war level, more married women worked, and the experience of independence followed by exclusion fed the feminist movement of the 1960s. Change was real for individuals but delayed for women as a group. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Describe two (4)
Describe two ways the Second World War helped the American economy. (4)

**War production.** Factories switched to making tanks, ships and around 300,000 aircraft (1). The government's huge contracts created millions of jobs and unemployment fell to around 1 per cent by 1944 (1).

**Lend Lease.** From March 1941 the USA supplied weapons, food and raw materials to Britain and later the USSR (1). These orders kept American factories and farms busy even before the USA joined the war (1).
:::

::: example Q5 In what ways (8)
In what ways were African Americans affected by the Second World War? Explain your answer. (8)

**Work and migration.** Over a million moved north and west for war jobs. After A. Philip Randolph threatened a march on Washington, Executive Order 8802 (1941) banned discrimination in defence industries.

**Military service.** About a million served, but in segregated units, often in labour and supply roles. The Tuskegee Airmen showed what Black servicemen could achieve.

**Protest.** The Double V campaign (1942) called for victory over fascism abroad and racism at home; NAACP membership grew; CORE was founded in 1942.

**Complexity (Level 4).** The effects were mixed and differed by place. In the North there were more jobs but also hostility, as in the Detroit riot of 1943; in the South Jim Crow was unchanged. Above all, fighting Nazi racism while facing segregation at home raised expectations, leading to Truman's desegregation of the forces in 1948 and the civil rights campaigns of the 1950s.
:::

::: example Q6 Which did more (12)
Which of the following did more to end the Depression in America: the New Deal; the Second World War? Explain your answer with reference to both bullet points. (12)

**The New Deal.** From 1933 the bank holiday restored confidence, the CCC, PWA and WPA gave millions work, and the AAA raised farm incomes. Unemployment fell from about a quarter of the workforce to well below that by 1937. Social Security and the Wagner Act gave lasting security. But when spending was cut, the 1937–38 recession pushed unemployment back up to nearly ten million in 1938.

**The Second World War.** Cash and carry (1939) and Lend Lease (1941) brought orders; after Pearl Harbor government spending soared. Over 15 million joined the forces and war production created labour shortages; unemployment fell to around 1 per cent by 1944, and the economy roughly doubled.

**Judgement.** The war did more, because it ended unemployment completely, which the New Deal never achieved. But the two are linked: wartime recovery rested on the New Deal's precedent of large-scale federal spending, and its infrastructure (TVA power, roads) helped production. The New Deal stopped the collapse; the war ended the Depression. (Level 4.)
:::

::: watch

- **"Women kept their jobs."** Many were dismissed in 1945; the long-term change was gradual.
- **Missing Lend Lease and exports.** They are named in the spec: explain how they helped recovery even before Pearl Harbor.
- **One-sided Q6 on recovery.** Give the New Deal its due (relief, confidence, reform) before arguing that the war ended unemployment.
- **Forgetting dates.** Cash and carry 1939; Lend Lease March 1941; Pearl Harbor 7 December 1941; Executive Order 8802 1941; Double V 1942; Detroit riot 1943.
