## What it is

This lesson covers the first bullet of Part three, **Post-war America**: **post-war American society and economy**: **consumerism and the causes of prosperity**; **the American Dream**; **McCarthyism**; and **popular culture, including rock and roll and television**.

The big idea: after 1945 most white Americans enjoyed a prosperity never seen before: a house in the suburbs, a car, a television and a growing family. This was the "American Dream". But the same years brought fear of communism at home (McCarthyism), a youth culture that alarmed parents, and the continued exclusion of African Americans and the poor from the Dream.

### 1. Causes of prosperity

| Cause | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Wartime savings** | Rationing had limited spending during the war, so Americans had large savings to spend once peace came |
| **The GI Bill (1944)** | Helped returning servicemen with college education, training and cheap loans to buy homes and start businesses |
| **The baby boom** | Births rose sharply from 1946 to the early 1960s, creating demand for houses, schools, toys, clothes and food |
| **Suburbs** | Mass-produced housing estates such as **Levittown** (from 1947) let families buy affordable homes outside cities |
| **Cars and roads** | Car ownership soared; the **Interstate Highway Act (1956)** funded a vast network of motorways, boosting construction and suburban growth |
| **Credit and advertising** | Hire purchase and the first credit cards made buying easy; television advertising created demand |
| **Government spending** | **Cold War** defence spending on weapons, aircraft and research created jobs in many states |
| **New technology and cheap energy** | New consumer goods (televisions, washing machines, freezers) and cheap oil |
| **America's position** | The USA came out of the war undamaged, with the world's strongest economy, while Europe and Japan rebuilt, buying American goods |

### 2. Consumerism

- Americans bought houses, cars, televisions, refrigerators, washing machines and new convenience foods in huge numbers.
- **Shopping centres** (malls), supermarkets and fast-food restaurants (such as McDonald's, which began franchising in the mid-1950s) appeared, many built for car-owning suburban families.
- Spending became a symbol of the American way of life, and of capitalism's superiority over communism in the Cold War.

### 3. The American Dream

**The idea:** in America anyone could achieve success and a good life through hard work: a home of one's own, a car, a secure job, a family and the chance for children to do better.

**The 1950s image:** a white suburban family, a father in a well-paid job, a mother as a full-time housewife, two or three children.

**Who was excluded:**
- **African Americans**: segregation in the South; in the North, many were refused mortgages, and early Levittown sales were for white families only.
- **The poor**: tens of millions still lived in poverty, in run-down inner cities and rural areas such as Appalachia.
- **Hispanic Americans and Native Americans**: low wages, discrimination and poverty.
- **Women**: many who had worked in the war were expected to find fulfilment as housewives, limiting their opportunities (an idea later attacked by the feminist movement).

### 4. McCarthyism

**Causes: why Americans feared communism at home**
- The **Cold War**: the USSR controlled Eastern Europe; the **USSR tested an atomic bomb in 1949**; **China became communist in 1949**; the **Korean War** began in 1950.
- **Spy cases**: **Alger Hiss**, a former State Department official, was convicted in 1950 of lying about passing documents to the Soviets; **Julius and Ethel Rosenberg** were executed in 1953 for passing atomic secrets.
- **The House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC)** investigated communists from the late 1930s; in 1947 it targeted **Hollywood**, and the **"Hollywood Ten"**, writers and directors who refused to answer its questions, were jailed for contempt of Congress. Hundreds of others were **blacklisted** by the studios.
- President Truman's **loyalty checks** on government employees (1947).

**Joseph McCarthy**
- A Republican senator from Wisconsin. In **February 1950** he claimed to have a list of communists working in the **State Department**. The numbers changed and he never proved his claims, but the accusations made him famous.
- For four years he led a campaign of accusations against government officials, academics, writers and others. Being accused could cost someone their job, even without evidence. Others were pressured to name names.
- **His fall:** in 1954 he attacked the **US Army**. The **Army–McCarthy hearings** were televised, and millions of viewers saw him bullying witnesses. The army's lawyer publicly rebuked him for his lack of decency, and the journalist **Ed Murrow** criticised him on television. In **December 1954** the **Senate censured** him. He died in 1957.

**Impact**
- **Thousands lost their jobs**: teachers, civil servants, entertainers and scientists; many were never shown to have done anything wrong.
- **Fear and conformity**: people avoided unpopular opinions, organisations and friendships; free speech was restricted.
- **The blacklist** damaged careers in Hollywood and broadcasting for years.
- Writers responded: Arthur Miller's play *The Crucible* (1953), about the Salem witch trials, was widely read as a comment on McCarthyism.
- The term **"McCarthyism"** came to mean making accusations of disloyalty without evidence.

### 5. Popular culture: rock and roll and television

**Television**
- In 1950 only a small minority of homes had a television; by 1960 nearly nine in ten did.
- Families watched comedies (such as *I Love Lucy*), westerns, quiz shows and news. Programmes often showed an idealised white suburban family.
- TV became a huge **advertising** medium, boosting consumerism, and later brought events such as the McCarthy hearings and civil rights protests into every home.

**Teenagers and rock and roll**
- With prosperity, teenagers had money from part-time jobs and allowances. A separate **teenage culture** emerged: their own clothes, music, films and slang.
- **Rock and roll** grew out of African American rhythm and blues mixed with country music. Bill Haley's *Rock Around the Clock* became a hit in 1955; **Elvis Presley** became a national star in 1956, with hits and controversial TV appearances; Black artists such as **Chuck Berry** and **Little Richard** also had huge success with white teenagers.
- Films such as *Rebel Without a Cause* (1955), starring James Dean, showed restless youth.
- **Reactions:** many parents, church leaders and politicians condemned rock and roll as immoral and rebellious; some in the South attacked it for bringing white and Black youth together. Despite this, it became a major industry.

::: terms
- **Consumerism**: a way of life and economy based on buying ever more goods and services.
- **GI Bill**: the 1944 law giving returning servicemen help with education, training and loans for homes and businesses.
- **Baby boom**: the sharp rise in the birth rate in the USA from 1946 to the early 1960s.
- **Suburbs**: residential areas on the edge of cities, such as Levittown, that grew rapidly after 1945.
- **American Dream**: the belief that anyone in America can achieve prosperity and success through hard work.
- **McCarthyism**: the anti-communist campaign led by Senator Joseph McCarthy; now, accusing people of disloyalty without evidence.
- **HUAC**: the House Un-American Activities Committee, which investigated alleged communists, including in Hollywood.
- **Blacklist**: a list of people refused work, such as entertainers suspected of communist sympathies.
- **Hollywood Ten**: film writers and directors jailed for refusing to answer HUAC's questions in 1947.
- **Censure**: a formal condemnation by the Senate, as given to McCarthy in December 1954.
- **Rock and roll**: the popular music of the 1950s that grew from rhythm and blues and country, popular with teenagers.
- **Teenager**: a young person aged 13–19; in the 1950s teenagers became a distinct group with their own money and culture.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 Section A at a glance.** About an hour, 40 marks: Q1 differ (4), Q2 why differ (4), Q3 more convincing (8), Q4 describe two (4), Q5 in what ways (8), Q6 which bullet (12). AQA asked in 2023: "Describe two problems faced by Americans because of McCarthyism." Other favourites: interpretations on the 1950s as a golden age; "in what ways were Americans affected by McCarthyism?".

1. **Q1 (4): content only.** E.g. one says the 1950s were a golden age, the other that many were excluded.
2. **Q2 (4): provenance + context.** A suburban housewife, an African American family refused a home, a blacklisted screenwriter, a government official: explain how experience, time and purpose shape each view.
3. **Q3 (8): test both views** with precise evidence: GI Bill, baby boom, Levittown, the Interstate Highway Act, TV ownership **against** poverty, segregation and the limits on women; for McCarthy, HUAC, the Hollywood Ten, the Army hearings and the censure.
4. **Q4 (4): two features, each with a precise detail.**
5. **Q5 (8): Level 4 shows how effects differed** (Hollywood, government workers, teachers; teenagers and parents) or how one cause fed another.
6. **Q6 (12): explain both bullets, then judge** with a clear criterion (how many people, how deep, how lasting).
:::

::: example Q1 How interpretations differ (4)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a woman who moved to a new suburb in 1952, interviewed in 1990: "We had a house with a yard, a car in the drive and a television in the den. The children played in safe streets. Anyone who worked hard could have that life. It was the best time to be American." Interpretation B, also written for practice, is in the style of a historian writing in 2016: "The suburban dream was real for some and closed to many. Black families were refused mortgages and homes, millions lived in poverty in the cities and countryside, and the housewife ideal confined educated women to the home." How does Interpretation B differ from Interpretation A about the American Dream in the 1950s? (4)

A says the American Dream was **open to anyone who worked hard**, and many enjoyed homes, cars and safety (1–2). B says it was **closed to many**: African Americans, the poor and many women were excluded or limited (+1). So A sees the Dream as available to all, B as a privilege of some (+1). Level 2, 4/4.
:::

::: example Q4 Describe two (4)
Describe two problems faced by Americans because of McCarthyism. (4)

**Losing jobs.** People accused of communist sympathies could be sacked even without evidence (1). Teachers, civil servants and entertainers lost their careers, and the Hollywood blacklist stopped hundreds working in films (1).

**Fear and loss of free speech.** Because any accusation was dangerous, people avoided unpopular opinions and organisations (1). Witnesses before HUAC were pressured to name friends and colleagues, so people became suspicious of each other (1).
:::

::: example Q5 In what ways (8)
In what ways were Americans affected by McCarthyism? Explain your answer. (8)

**Careers.** After McCarthy's claims in February 1950 about communists in the State Department, accusations spread to government, universities and entertainment. Thousands lost jobs; the Hollywood Ten were jailed in 1950 after refusing to answer HUAC, and many more were blacklisted.

**Fear and conformity.** Loyalty checks and investigations made people afraid to criticise the government or join left-wing groups; free speech was restricted.

**Political and cultural response.** By 1954 the televised Army–McCarthy hearings turned opinion against him, and the Senate censured him in December 1954. Writers such as Arthur Miller criticised the hysteria in *The Crucible*.

**Complexity (Level 4).** The effects fell unevenly: people in government, education and entertainment, and those with left-wing pasts, were most at risk, while most Americans were never investigated but became more cautious. The effects also changed over time: McCarthy's power peaked in 1950–54 and collapsed after the hearings, but the blacklist and the climate of conformity lasted longer than he did.
:::

::: example Q6 Which had more impact (12)
Which of the following had more impact on American society in the 1950s: consumerism; popular culture? Explain your answer with reference to both bullet points. (12)

**Consumerism.** Wartime savings, the GI Bill and the baby boom created huge demand. Families moved to suburbs like Levittown, bought cars (helped by the Interstate Highway Act of 1956), fridges and washing machines on credit. Shopping malls and fast food changed daily life. Consumerism became part of national identity in the Cold War.

**Popular culture.** Television spread from a small minority of homes in 1950 to nearly nine in ten by 1960, shaping how families spent their time and what they wanted to buy. Teenagers, with their own money, created a youth culture around rock and roll, Elvis Presley and Chuck Berry, and films like *Rebel Without a Cause*, which alarmed parents and church leaders.

**Judgement.** Consumerism had more impact, because it changed where and how most Americans lived (suburbs, cars, homes full of goods). Popular culture was powerful but depended on consumerism: television sets and teenagers' spending money were products of prosperity, and television was mainly a vehicle for advertising. Rock and roll, though, had a longer-term social effect in bringing Black music to white teenagers. (Level 4.)
:::
