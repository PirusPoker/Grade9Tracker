## What it is

This lesson covers the third bullet of **Part four: Modern medicine**: **modern public health** (c1900 to the present). It covers the evidence of poverty from **Booth**, **Rowntree** and the **Boer War**; the **Liberal social reforms**; the impact of the **two world wars** on health, poverty and housing; the **Beveridge Report** and the **Welfare State**; the creation and development of the **NHS**; and the costs and choices facing healthcare in the 21st century.

The big idea: the 19th century made the government responsible for **clean towns**. The 20th century made it responsible for **people's health and welfare** "from the cradle to the grave". The key cause was **evidence that poverty caused ill health**, made urgent by **war**, and turned into law by **government**.

### 1. The evidence: Booth, Rowntree and the Boer War

| Evidence | Findings | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|
| **Charles Booth**, a wealthy businessman, surveyed **London** from **1886**, publishing in volumes over the following years | About **30%** of Londoners lived in poverty; he mapped streets by income level | Showed that poverty was huge and was usually caused by **low wages, unemployment, illness or old age**, not laziness |
| **Seebohm Rowntree**, of the York chocolate-making family, surveyed **York** and published in **1901** | Over a quarter of people lived in poverty, even in a smaller town; he set a **poverty line** and described a **poverty cycle** (worst in childhood, while raising children, and in old age) | Proved poverty was not just a London problem, and that people could not escape it through their own efforts |
| **The Boer War** (1899–1902) | A large proportion of volunteers from industrial towns were **rejected** as physically unfit | Alarmed the government: if men were too unhealthy to fight, Britain's power and **empire** were at risk |

Together they changed attitudes: poverty and ill health were **national problems** that needed **government action**. Fear of losing working-class voters to the new **Labour Party** (founded 1900) added political pressure.

### 2. The Liberal social reforms (1906–1914)

The Liberal government elected in **1906**, with **David Lloyd George** and **Winston Churchill** as leading reformers, passed a series of measures:

| Group | Reform |
|---|---|
| **Children** | **Free school meals** (1906; councils could provide them); **school medical inspections** (1907), later with free treatment; the **Children's Act** (1908) protected children from neglect |
| **The elderly** | **Old Age Pensions** (1908): a small weekly pension for people over 70, paid from taxes |
| **Workers** | **Labour exchanges** (1909) to help the unemployed find work; the **National Insurance Act** (**1911**): workers, employers and the state paid contributions; insured workers got **sick pay** and **free treatment from a doctor**, and some trades got unemployment pay |

**Limits:** school meals were at first optional for councils; pensions were small and only from 70; National Insurance covered the **worker but not his family**, and did not pay for hospital care. Some opposed the cost and the interference; some workers resented deductions from wages. But this was the first time the state took **direct responsibility** for the welfare of the poor: a foundation of the Welfare State.

### 3. The impact of the two world wars

**First World War (1914–18):**
- Recruitment again revealed poor health; soldiers lived in terrible trench conditions.
- Lloyd George promised **"homes fit for heroes"**. The **Ministry of Health** was set up in **1919**, and a **Housing Act** (1919) gave councils money to build **council houses**. Fewer were built than promised, as money ran short in the 1920s.
- The **Depression** of the 1930s brought high unemployment and poverty, especially in the north, Wales and Scotland.

**Second World War (1939–45):**
- **Evacuation** (from 1939) sent city children to the countryside. Many hosts were shocked by their poor health, dirty clothes, lice and malnutrition: middle-class Britain **saw** urban poverty for itself.
- **Rationing** gave everyone a fair share; the diets of the poorest **improved**, with free or cheap milk and vitamins for children.
- The **Emergency Medical Service** organised hospitals nationally to treat air-raid casualties, showing that a national system could work.
- **Bombing** destroyed huge numbers of homes, making housing a major post-war problem.
- The war created a spirit of **"fair shares"** and a belief that ordinary people deserved a better Britain after their sacrifice.

### 4. The Beveridge Report (1942)

**William Beveridge**, a civil servant and economist, was asked to review social insurance. His report (December **1942**) identified **five giants** blocking progress:

| Giant | Problem | Later solution |
|---|---|---|
| **Want** | Poverty | National Insurance, family allowances |
| **Disease** | Ill health | **National Health Service** |
| **Ignorance** | Poor education | Education Act (1944) |
| **Squalor** | Bad housing | Council house building |
| **Idleness** | Unemployment | Government aim of full employment |

He proposed a system of social security **"from the cradle to the grave"**, paid for by National Insurance contributions. The report was a **bestseller**, and the public expected it to happen. **Labour** won the **1945 election** promising to carry it out, creating the **Welfare State** (National Insurance Act 1946, family allowances, the NHS).

### 5. The creation of the NHS (1948)

**Aneurin Bevan**, Labour's Minister of Health and a former miner from Wales, set up the **National Health Service**, which began on **5 July 1948**. Its principles: **free at the point of use**, available to **everyone**, paid for through **taxation**.

**Opposition:** many doctors, led by the **British Medical Association (BMA)**, opposed it. They feared becoming **state employees**, losing **income** from private patients and losing their **independence**; some Conservatives and local authorities (who lost control of their hospitals) objected too.

**How Bevan won them over (concessions):** GPs were paid according to the **number of patients**, not a salary, and stayed independent; hospital **consultants** could keep **private patients** and use NHS hospital beds for them. By the time it opened, most doctors had joined.

**Immediate impact:** huge demand: millions of people got **free** spectacles, dentures, prescriptions and treatment they had gone without, especially **women** and the elderly, who had not been covered by National Insurance. Costs were far **higher** than expected.

### 6. The NHS's development

- **Charges:** to control costs, charges were introduced for spectacles and dentures (Bevan **resigned** in **1951** in protest) and **prescription charges** from **1952**.
- **Prevention:** national **vaccination** campaigns (for example against polio and diphtheria); screening; the **Clean Air Act** (**1956**) after London's deadly smog of **1952**; campaigns against **smoking** after scientists linked it to lung cancer in the 1950s, leading in time to the ban on smoking in enclosed public places in England (**2007**).
- **Hospitals** were built and modernised; new treatments (transplants, scanners, IVF, keyhole surgery) became available through the NHS.

### 7. Costs, choices and issues in the 21st century

| Issue | Details |
|---|---|
| **Cost** | Modern drugs, scanners and surgery are very expensive; NHS spending has risen enormously |
| **Ageing population** | People live longer, so more need care for long-term conditions such as dementia and heart disease |
| **Lifestyle diseases** | **Obesity**, type 2 diabetes, alcohol and smoking-related illness are now leading causes of ill health; government campaigns (healthy eating, exercise) try to change behaviour |
| **Choices** | A body (**NICE**, created in 1999) decides whether new treatments are cost-effective; some treatments are available in some areas but not others; waiting lists; debates about private provision and about how far government should tell people how to live |

### 8. Factors in modern public health

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Government** | Liberal reforms; Ministry of Health; the Welfare State and NHS; laws on clean air and smoking |
| **War** | Boer War recruits; "homes fit for heroes"; evacuation and rationing; the demand for a fairer Britain after 1945 |
| **Individuals** | Booth, Rowntree, Lloyd George, Beveridge, Bevan |
| **Science and technology** | Surveys and statistics; vaccines; evidence linking smoking and cancer; costly new treatments |
| **Communication** | Booth's maps, the bestselling Beveridge Report, health campaigns on TV and online |
| **Economy** | Poverty caused ill health; the cost of the NHS forces charges and hard choices |

::: terms
- **Poverty line**: Rowntree's measure of the minimum income needed to stay physically healthy.
- **Poverty cycle**: Rowntree's idea that people were most likely to be poor in childhood, while raising children and in old age.
- **Liberal reforms**: the social laws passed by the Liberal government from 1906, such as school meals, pensions and National Insurance.
- **National Insurance**: a scheme from 1911 in which workers, employers and the state paid in, giving insured workers sick pay and free GP care.
- **Evacuation**: moving children from cities to the countryside in the Second World War, which exposed urban poverty.
- **Beveridge Report**: the 1942 report that identified the five giants and proposed welfare "from the cradle to the grave".
- **Five giants**: Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor and Idleness, the problems Beveridge said blocked progress.
- **Welfare State**: the system of state support for health, income, housing and education created after 1945.
- **NHS**: the National Health Service, launched on 5 July 1948, free at the point of use and paid for through taxation.
- **BMA**: the British Medical Association, the doctors' organisation that opposed the NHS at first.
- **Lifestyle diseases**: illnesses linked to how people live, such as obesity and type 2 diabetes.
:::

::: example Q2 Significance of the Beveridge Report (8)
Explain the significance of the Beveridge Report. (8)

**At the time**, the Beveridge Report (1942) was significant because it gave a clear plan for ending the **five giants**: Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor and Idleness. It appeared during the war, when evacuation had exposed poverty and people wanted a fairer Britain after their sacrifices, so it became a **bestseller** and raised expectations.

It shaped politics: **Labour** promised to carry it out and won the **1945 election**. Its proposals led directly to the **Welfare State**: National Insurance (1946), family allowances, council housing, and above all the **NHS** in 1948.

**Over time** it is significant because it established the idea that the state should look after citizens "**from the cradle to the grave**". That principle still shapes Britain, though the rising cost of the welfare state and the NHS has led to debates about charges and what the state can afford. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Similarity (8)
Explain two ways in which public health in the 19th and 20th centuries was similar. (8)

**First, in both centuries investigations and evidence pushed the government to act.** In 1842 **Chadwick's** report showed that labourers in towns died far younger, which helped bring the 1848 Public Health Act. In the early 20th century **Booth** and **Rowntree** showed that about a quarter to a third of people lived in poverty, which helped lead to the **Liberal reforms**.

**Second, in both centuries government moved from limited, optional measures to compulsory national ones.** The **1848** Act was **permissive**, but the **1875** Act forced councils to provide clean water and sewers. Similarly, free school meals in **1906** were optional for councils, but after 1945 the government created a national **Welfare State** and the **NHS**, run and funded nationally. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Factor essay (16)
Has government been the main factor in improving the health of the British people? Explain your answer with reference to government and other factors. Use a range of examples from across your study of Health and the people: c1000 to the present day. (16)

**Government** has been the main factor in public health. In the Middle Ages there was almost no national action; during the Great Plague of 1665 the authorities ordered quarantines, but did not understand the cause. The **1875 Public Health Act** forced councils to provide clean water and sewers. In the 20th century the **Liberal reforms** (pensions in 1908, National Insurance in 1911) and above all the **NHS** (1948) gave everyone access to free treatment, while later laws on clean air (1956) and smoking (2007) prevented disease.

But government usually acted because of **other factors**. **Individuals** provided evidence: Chadwick, Snow, Booth and Rowntree. **War** created urgency: the unfit Boer War recruits led to the Liberal reforms, and evacuation and a sense of shared sacrifice in the Second World War led to the Beveridge Report and Labour's victory. **Science and technology** created the vaccines, antibiotics and treatments the NHS provides, and germ theory explained why sewers mattered. **Democracy**, such as working men's votes after 1867 and Labour's rise, made reform politically necessary.

**Judgement.** Government has been the main factor in improving health, because only it could provide clean water, welfare and healthcare for the **whole population**. But it was a **reactive** factor, usually moved to act by evidence, war and voters. (Level 4.)
