## What it is

This lesson covers **Part four: The historic environment of Norman England**. AQA names one **site** each year, and Question 4 of the exam (16 marks) is always about it. For students sitting in **June 2028** the site is **the White Tower** (the original great tower of the Tower of London). The site changes each year (Pevensey Castle in 2026, the Battle of Hastings in 2027), so check AQA's list for your exam year; the method in this lesson works for any site.

The big idea: the site question is **not** a tour of a building. It asks how the site connects to the **events and developments** of Norman England (conquest, control, government, religion, culture), and how it shows **change and continuity**. Every feature you describe must be tied to the period: "the walls are thick" earns little; "the walls are thick because a small Norman elite had to dominate a large and possibly hostile city" earns a lot. You do not need to have visited.

### 1. What AQA asks you to consider for any site

| Aspect | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Why here? What did the place control or protect? |
| **Function** | What was it for: defence, residence, government, religion, display? Did that change? |
| **Structure** | What is it built of and how? What does that tell us about resources and skills? |
| **People** | Who ordered it, designed it, built it, lived in it, was kept in it? |
| **Design** | What are the key features and why were they chosen? |
| **Culture and values** | What does the design show about the beliefs, tastes and priorities of its builders? |
| **Links to events** | Which events and developments of the depth study connect to it? |
| **Change and continuity** | How does it differ from what came before, and how did it change during the period? |

### 2. Location: why the south-east corner of London?

- **London** was England's largest and richest town, a centre of trade and the key to controlling the kingdom. William had circled it after Hastings rather than storming it, and the city submitted only after the leaders came to him at Berkhamsted.
- The tower was built in the **south-east corner of the old Roman city wall**, beside the **River Thames**. The Roman walls gave ready-made defences on two sides; new ditches and banks closed off the rest.
- From here the Normans could **watch the river**, the route by which ships and any Danish fleet would approach, and **overawe the city**: the tower stood just outside the crowded streets but dominated them.
- Close to the river, building stone and supplies could be brought in by **water**.
- Norman writers said William built fortifications in London after his coronation as a safeguard against the restless and numerous population. So location tells us about **fear** as well as power.

### 3. People: who built it and who used it

- **William I** ordered it. A first, quickly built **timber** fortification was put up in this corner after the coronation of 1066. The great stone tower was begun probably in the **1070s**.
- **Gundulf**, a monk of Bec who became **Bishop of Rochester**, is recorded as supervising the work. He was close to **Lanfranc** and also built in stone at Rochester. A bishop running a royal fortress shows how closely the Norman **Church** and **state** worked together.
- **William II** (Rufus) **completed** it, probably by about 1100. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, in its entry for 1097, complained of the burden of forced labour on works at the Tower, along with other royal building in London (Westminster Hall).
- The builders were largely **English** labourers working under Norman masters: the conquered built the symbol of their own conquest.
- **Occupants**: the king and his household when in London; a **constable** and garrison; officials and stores.
- **Prisoners**: its first recorded prisoner was **Ranulf Flambard**, William II's hated minister, locked up by Henry I in **1100**. He escaped in 1101, reportedly by a rope smuggled in with wine, climbing down from a window. This links the tower to William II's government and its unpopularity.

### 4. Structure and design

| Feature | Detail | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| **Size** | A great rectangular tower, about 27 metres (90 feet) high, the tallest building in London for its time | Power and permanence: nothing in Anglo-Saxon London compared |
| **Materials** | Walls of **Kentish ragstone** with fine **Caen stone** from Normandy for dressings, corners and the chapel | Wealth and organisation; Normandy literally built into England |
| **Walls** | Several metres thick at the base, narrowing higher up | Defence against attack and fire; very hard to undermine |
| **Entrance** | At **first-floor** level on the south side, reached by an external wooden stair that could be removed | Attackers could not reach a door at ground level |
| **Turrets** | Four corner turrets, three square and one **round**, holding spiral staircases | Lookouts; communication between floors |
| **Windows** | Small, narrow openings low down; larger windows higher up | Defence first, comfort higher up (later enlarged, centuries after) |
| **Interior** | A basement (stores, a well) and two main floors, divided by a strong **cross wall** into large rooms; the upper floor was very tall | Space for a royal household; the cross wall added strength and helped hold up the floors |
| **Comforts** | Some of the earliest **fireplaces** with flues in England; **garderobes** (latrines) built into the walls | It was a **residence** fit for a king, not just a barracks |
| **Chapel of St John the Evangelist** | On the upper levels in the south-east, with a rounded **apse** that bulges out of the outline; massive round pillars, round arches, plain carved capitals, a gallery, Caen stone | The king's private worship; Norman **Romanesque** style; religion at the heart of royal power |

**Comparisons.** Similar great towers were built in Normandy and at **Colchester** in the same period (Colchester also has an apsidal chapel and reuses a Roman site). This shows a Norman **type** of building, brought from the Continent and used to mark key places in England.

### 5. Function: what was it for?

1. **Military control**: a strongpoint and garrison base dominating London and the Thames; a refuge if the city rose. Fits the revolts of 1067–75 and the Danish threat (1069–70, 1075, 1085).
2. **Symbol of conquest**: size, height and foreign stone told Londoners that the Normans were here to stay.
3. **Royal residence and government**: fine rooms, fireplaces, a chapel; a place where the king could stay safely in London. Over time it also became a store for weapons, records and treasure.
4. **Religion and legitimacy**: the chapel showed the king as a Christian ruler blessed by God, as the papal banner had in 1066.
5. **Prison**: from 1100, beginning a long history as a prison for important captives.

The best answers ask which function was **main** and how they **combined**: control and display were intertwined, because the building controlled by **intimidating**.

### 7. Change and continuity

**From earlier periods**
- **Continuity**: London had been a Roman city with walls, and the Anglo-Saxons (from Alfred's time) used it as a fortified **burh**. The Normans **reused the Roman walls**.
- **Change**: Anglo-Saxon defences protected the **whole community**; the White Tower protected a **lord and his men against the community**. Stone great towers of this scale were new in England, as was the Romanesque style on this scale.

**During the period (c1066–c1100)**
- From a **temporary timber** fortification (1066–67) to a **permanent stone** tower (1070s–c1100): from emergency control to lasting rule.
- From William I to William II: the project outlived its founder, showing continuity of Norman kingship; new outer works and walls were added.
- Function widened: from fortress to fortress, residence, store and (from 1100) prison.

**Later changes (beyond the period, useful for contrast)**: in the thirteenth century it was whitewashed, which gave it the name "White Tower", and it was surrounded by further walls; its windows were enlarged much later.

### 8. Planning the 16-mark site essay

1. **Introduction**: answer the question directly: "The White Tower supports this statement to a large extent, because… but…".
2. **For the statement**: two features of the site, each tied to an event or development, with precise knowledge.
3. **Against or qualifying**: one or two other functions or factors shown by the site, again tied to context.
4. **Change/continuity or consequence**: show how the site changed or what it led to.
5. **Judgement**: which matters most, and why, carried through the essay, not bolted on.

::: terms
- **Historic environment**: a specified real place studied to understand how it connects to the events and developments of the period.
- **Great tower**: a large, strong stone tower at the heart of a Norman castle, sometimes called a keep.
- **Caen stone**: fine pale limestone from Normandy, shipped to England for important Norman buildings.
- **Ragstone**: a hard local stone from Kent, used for the main walls of the White Tower.
- **First-floor entrance**: a door above ground level, reached by a removable stair, making attack harder.
- **Cross wall**: a strong internal wall dividing the White Tower into separate rooms on each floor.
- **Garderobe**: a latrine built into the thickness of a castle wall.
- **Apse**: the rounded east end of a chapel or church, like that of the chapel of St John.
- **Romanesque**: the Norman style of round arches, thick walls and massive round pillars.
- **Constable**: the officer in charge of a royal castle and its garrison when the king was absent.
:::

::: method
**Paper 2 Section B, Question 4 (16).** "'[Statement].' How far does a study of [site] support this statement? Explain your answer. You should refer to [site] and your contextual knowledge."

1. Read the statement and find its **key concept** (purpose, control, change, consequence, culture). Decide your judgement first.
2. Plan two or three paragraphs, each starting with a **site feature** (location, walls, entrance, chapel, materials, people) and moving to **contextual knowledge** (events, developments, other places).
3. Write at least one paragraph that **challenges or qualifies** the statement.
4. Use the language of **change, continuity, causation and consequence**.
5. Make the judgement explicit in every paragraph and in a short conclusion that explains **why** one view is stronger.
6. Do not just describe the building, and do not just write about the Normans without the site: Level 4 needs both, woven together.
:::

::: example Site essay: control of London (16)
"The main purpose of the White Tower was to control London." How far does a study of the White Tower support this statement? Explain your answer. You should refer to the White Tower and your contextual knowledge. (16)

**The location strongly supports the statement.** The White Tower was built in the south-east corner of London's Roman walls, beside the Thames, the first stone version of fortifications put up after William's coronation in 1066. London was the largest and richest town in England and had submitted only after William circled it. Norman writers said these defences were built because of the city's large and restless population. The thick walls, first-floor entrance and garrison show a building designed to hold out against a hostile city and to watch the river, the route any Danish fleet would take, as in 1069–70 and 1075.

**But the tower's design shows other purposes too.** It was a **royal residence**, with fireplaces, garderobes and great rooms, where the king and his household could stay. Its **chapel of St John**, built in Caen stone in the Romanesque style, displayed the king as a Christian ruler, just as Lanfranc's cathedrals linked Norman power to God. Its height and foreign stone made it a **symbol** for the whole kingdom, not only London: it told the English that conquest was permanent.

**Change during the period** supports a wider view. Completed under William II and used from 1100 as a prison for Ranulf Flambard, it became a centre of royal power in general.

**Judgement.** Controlling London was the first and most important purpose, as the location and defences show. But the White Tower controlled by **impressing** as much as by force, and its residence and chapel show it served Norman kingship as a whole. So the statement is largely, but not entirely, supported. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Site essay: change (16)
"The White Tower shows that the Normans brought great change to England." How far does a study of the White Tower support this statement? Explain your answer. You should refer to the White Tower and your contextual knowledge. (16)

**The White Tower shows major change.** Nothing like it existed in Anglo-Saxon England: a stone great tower about 27 metres high, faced with Caen stone shipped from Normandy, built in the Romanesque style. Anglo-Saxon defences, such as London's burh, protected the whole community, but the White Tower protected a small ruling class **against** the community. This matches wider change: hundreds of castles, the replacement of the English aristocracy by Norman tenants-in-chief, and Normans in every bishopric.

**Its people show change too.** It was ordered by William I, supervised by the Norman bishop Gundulf, finished by William II and built with forced English labour, the conquered building the symbol of their conquest. The Church that Gundulf served was being transformed by Lanfranc, and the chapel of St John reflects the same style as the new cathedrals.

**But it also shows continuity.** It was built **inside the Roman walls**, reusing defences that the Anglo-Saxons had also used, and it stood in London, already the key city under Edward the Confessor. William kept London's privileges and much of Anglo-Saxon government: sheriffs, writs and coinage. For many ordinary people beyond London, farming life went on much as before.

**Judgement.** The White Tower strongly supports the statement for those who held power, and for how power was displayed and enforced. But its reuse of Roman walls and London's continuing importance show the Normans built on what already existed. The change was greatest at the top. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Explain what was important (8)
Explain what was important about the chapel of St John in the White Tower. (8)

The chapel was important first for **royal religion**. It gave the king and his household a private place of worship inside the fortress, showing that a Norman king was expected to be pious, as William presented himself when he carried the papal banner in 1066.

Second, it was important as **display**. Built of fine Caen stone, with massive round pillars and round arches in the Romanesque style, its apse even bulges out of the building's outline. It showed the wealth and culture of the conquerors and linked the White Tower to the great cathedrals rebuilt under Lanfranc.

Third, it shows that **Church and state worked together**. The tower was supervised by Bishop Gundulf, and the chapel at the heart of a fortress expressed the Norman idea that their rule was God's will, which made rebellion seem sinful. (Level 4.)
:::

::: watch
- **Describing the building without the period.** Every feature must be linked to an event or development.
- **Ignoring the statement.** Keep returning to the key concept (purpose, change, control).
- **No counter-argument.** Level 4 needs a balanced, sustained judgement.
- **Inventing exact dates.** Building began probably in the 1070s and finished around 1100; say "about".
- **Using later features as Norman.** The whitewash and large windows came long after 1100.
- **Forgetting people.** William I, Gundulf, William II, English labourers and Flambard all connect the site to the period.
:::
