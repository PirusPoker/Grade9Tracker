## What it is

This lesson covers the first bullet of Part one, **Germany and the growth of democracy**: Kaiser Wilhelm II and the difficulties of ruling Germany between 1890 and 1914. You need five things: the **growth of parliamentary government**, the influence of **Prussian militarism**, **industrialisation**, **social reform and the growth of socialism**, and the **domestic importance of the Navy Laws**.

The big idea: Germany had a modern economy and an elected parliament, but power stayed with an emperor, an aristocratic army and the state of Prussia. Every problem Wilhelm faced came from that tension between a fast-changing society and an old-fashioned system of power.

### 1. Kaiser Wilhelm II and the constitution

**Wilhelm II** became Kaiser (emperor) in **1888** and ruled until he abdicated in **November 1918**. In **1890** he forced out the Chancellor, **Otto von Bismarck**, who had built the German Empire in 1871, because he wanted to rule himself. Wilhelm was intelligent and energetic but impulsive and tactless; he loved the army, uniforms and parades, and believed he was answerable to God, not the people. Historians often link his insecurity to a difficult childhood and a damaged left arm from birth, but keep that as background: the exam wants his **aims and actions**.

| Part of the system | Who | Powers |
|---|---|---|
| **Kaiser** | Wilhelm II, also King of Prussia | Appointed and dismissed the **Chancellor** and ministers; commanded the **armed forces**; ran **foreign policy**; could call and dissolve the Reichstag |
| **Chancellor** | Chief minister (Caprivi, Hohenlohe, Bülow, then Bethmann Hollweg from 1909) | Ran the government and proposed laws; answerable to the **Kaiser, not the Reichstag** |
| **Bundesrat** | Representatives of the 25 states | Had to agree to laws; **Prussia** had enough votes to block changes to the constitution |
| **Reichstag** | Elected by all **men over 25** | Debated and **passed or rejected laws and the budget**; could not appoint or remove the government |

So Germany was a **semi-democracy**: elections were fair and turnout high, but the government was not chosen by the parliament.

### 2. The growth of parliamentary government

The Reichstag's weapon was money. New taxes and the military budget needed its vote, so Chancellors had to build majorities among the parties. As the parties grew, especially the socialists, ruling without the Reichstag became harder.

- **The Daily Telegraph affair (1908).** Wilhelm gave an interview to a British newspaper in which he made clumsy claims about Germany's friendship with Britain. It caused uproar in both countries. The Reichstag criticised him openly, Chancellor **Bülow** failed to defend him, and Wilhelm promised to be more careful in public. It showed the Kaiser could be embarrassed and restrained by public and parliamentary opinion; Bülow lost office in 1909.
- **The Zabern affair (1913).** In Zabern, in Alsace, a young army officer insulted local people, and the army then arrested civilians illegally. The Reichstag passed a **vote of no confidence** in Chancellor **Bethmann Hollweg** by a huge majority, but he stayed in office because only the Kaiser could dismiss him, and Wilhelm backed the army. It showed both the Reichstag's growing confidence and the **limits of its power**.

### 3. Prussian militarism

**Prussia** made up about two-thirds of the empire's land and population, its king was the Kaiser, and its capital, Berlin, was the national capital. Prussia's culture of **militarism** shaped Germany:
- The **army** was answerable to the Kaiser, not the Reichstag, and officers swore loyalty to him personally.
- The officer corps came mainly from the **Junkers**, Prussian landowning aristocrats who were conservative and hostile to democracy and socialism.
- The army had huge **prestige**: uniforms were admired, being a reserve officer helped a man's career, and the military was treated as above criticism.
- Prussia's own parliament was elected on a **three-class voting system** that gave the rich far more weight, so conservatives dominated it.

### 4. Industrialisation

Germany became Europe's leading industrial power:
- **Coal, iron and steel**: by 1914 German steel production had overtaken Britain's. Firms such as **Krupp** became giants.
- **New industries**: **chemicals** (dyes, fertilisers, medicines) and **electrical** goods (firms such as Siemens and AEG) led the world.
- **Population** rose by over a third between 1890 and 1914, and cities such as Berlin and the Ruhr towns grew rapidly. Most Germans now lived in towns.

**Consequences.** Industrialisation created a large **working class** living in crowded housing with long hours and few rights. Workers joined **trade unions** (millions of members by 1914) and voted for the **Social Democratic Party (SPD)**. Meanwhile wealthy industrialists wanted influence, and Junkers feared that cheap imported grain and urban growth threatened their way of life. The economy was modern; the political system was not.

### 5. Social reform and the growth of socialism

The SPD was a **Marxist** party in theory (its 1891 programme called for the end of capitalism), though in practice most of its leaders sought reform through the Reichstag. Wilhelm and the elites saw it as a threat to the monarchy.

| Election | SPD position |
|---|---|
| 1890 | The anti-socialist law lapsed; the SPD won the most votes, though not the most seats |
| **1912** | **110 seats: the largest party in the Reichstag**, with about a third of the vote |

The government tried two approaches:
- **Reform to undercut socialism ("killing socialism with kindness").** Wilhelm kept Bismarck's insurance against **sickness**, **accidents** and **old age**, and added measures such as the **Workers' Protection Act (1891)**, which banned Sunday work and limited the hours of women and children, and industrial courts to settle disputes. Later laws extended insurance to more workers.
- **Hostility.** Wilhelm talked of using the army against socialists and refused to treat the SPD as a normal party.

Neither approach worked: the SPD kept growing, and by 1912 no government could ignore it.

### 6. The domestic importance of the Navy Laws

**Admiral Tirpitz**, in charge of the navy from 1897, persuaded the Reichstag to pass the **Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900**, which committed Germany to building a large battle fleet; further laws followed (1906, 1908, 1912). This was part of **Weltpolitik**, Wilhelm's "world policy" of colonies and naval power. The specification asks for the **domestic** importance:
- **Popularity and nationalism.** The navy was hugely popular. The **Navy League** (1898) had hundreds of thousands of members and campaigned for more ships. A big navy gave Germans a shared national symbol and rallied them behind the Kaiser.
- **Uniting the elites.** Industrialists (steel, shipbuilding) profited from contracts; the middle classes were proud of it. The policy was meant to unite conservatives and nationalists against the socialists.
- **Jobs.** Shipbuilding and its suppliers employed many workers.
- **Cost and conflict.** The fleet was enormously expensive, especially after Britain launched HMS Dreadnought (1906). Debt grew, and new taxes needed Reichstag votes, which gave the parties, including the SPD, more leverage. Budget battles weakened governments.

### Judgement: how difficult was Germany to rule?

The Kaiser kept the key powers, but his position was weakening. The Reichstag could not appoint governments, yet its control of money, the rise of the SPD and episodes such as the Daily Telegraph and Zabern affairs show that **parliamentary government was growing**. Militarism and the Navy Laws were the elites' answer: national pride to unite Germans. By 1914 the system was under strain, which is one reason the war in 1914 was welcomed by some leaders as a way to unite the nation.

::: terms
- **Kaiser**: the German emperor, who appointed the Chancellor and controlled the army and foreign policy.
- **Chancellor**: the head of the German government, appointed by and answerable to the Kaiser rather than the Reichstag.
- **Reichstag**: the German parliament, elected by men over 25, which passed laws and the budget but could not choose the government.
- **Bundesrat**: the federal council of the German states, in which Prussia had enough votes to block constitutional change.
- **Prussian militarism**: the dominance of Prussian army values, with a powerful army answerable to the Kaiser and led by Junker officers.
- **Junkers**: the conservative landowning aristocracy of Prussia, who supplied most army officers and opposed democracy.
- **SPD**: the Social Democratic Party, the socialist party of the workers, which became the largest party in the Reichstag in 1912.
- **Weltpolitik**: "world policy", Wilhelm's aim of making Germany a world power through colonies and a large navy.
- **Navy Laws**: the laws of 1898 and 1900 (and later additions) promoted by Tirpitz to build a large German battle fleet.
- **Navy League**: a mass pressure group founded in 1898 to build popular support for a bigger navy.
:::

3. **Q3 "Which interpretation gives the more convincing opinion…?" (8).** Test **each** interpretation's main argument against precise knowledge (Daily Telegraph affair, Zabern, the SPD's 110 seats). 4. **Q4 "Describe two…" (4).** Two short paragraphs: name a feature, then add a precise supporting detail. No evaluation needed.
5. **Q5 "In what ways were… affected by…?" (8).** Explain two or three effects with detail. For Level 4, show how effects **differed** between groups (workers, Junkers, industrialists) or **changed over time**.

::: example Q1 How interpretations differ (4)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a Prussian army officer's memoir, published in 1925: "Before the war the Kaiser was master in his own house. Ministers came and went at his word, the army looked to him alone, and the chatter in the Reichstag changed nothing that mattered. Germany was strong because it was ruled from the top." Interpretation B, also written for practice, is in the style of a historian writing in 2015: "By 1914 the Kaiser could no longer govern as he pleased. The Reichstag controlled the money, the socialists were its largest party, and even a vote of no confidence showed how far opinion had moved against personal rule." How does Interpretation B differ from Interpretation A about the Kaiser's power before 1914? Explain your answer based on what it says in Interpretations A and B. (4)

Interpretation A argues the Kaiser had **complete control**: ministers were appointed and dismissed by him and the Reichstag "changed nothing that mattered" (1–2). Interpretation B argues the opposite, that his power was **being limited**, because the Reichstag controlled the budget and the socialists were the largest party (+1). So A sees a strong personal ruler while B sees a ruler whose power was being eaten away by parliament (+1). Level 2, 4/4: a developed difference supported from both.
:::

::: example Q3 Which is more convincing (8)
Using Interpretations A and B above: which interpretation gives the more convincing opinion about the Kaiser's power before 1914? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretations A and B. (8)

**A is convincing in part.** The constitution gave the Kaiser the power to appoint and dismiss the Chancellor, and the army answered to him, not the Reichstag. The Zabern affair (1913) proves it: the Reichstag passed a vote of no confidence in Bethmann Hollweg, but he stayed in office because Wilhelm backed him and the army.

**B is also convincing.** The Reichstag had to approve the budget, and the cost of the Navy Laws and army meant governments needed its votes. In 1912 the SPD won 110 seats and became the largest party, so no Chancellor could ignore it. The Daily Telegraph affair (1908) showed the Kaiser could be openly criticised and forced to promise restraint.

**Judgement.** B is more convincing about the *direction* of change: the Reichstag's power over money and the rise of the SPD were growing. But A is right about *who held formal power*: even after Zabern, the Reichstag could not remove a Chancellor. The best view combines them: the Kaiser still ruled, but by 1914 he could not govern without the Reichstag's money. (Level 4: both evaluated with precise knowledge and a sustained judgement.)
:::

::: example Q4 Describe two (4)
Describe two problems Kaiser Wilhelm II faced in ruling Germany before 1914. (4)

**The growth of socialism.** Industrialisation created a large working class that voted for the SPD (1). By 1912 the SPD had 110 seats and was the largest party in the Reichstag, which alarmed the Kaiser, who saw socialism as a threat to the monarchy (1).

**Paying for the navy and army.** Tirpitz's Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900 committed Germany to a huge, expensive fleet (1). New taxes needed the Reichstag's approval, so the cost gave the parties more leverage over the government (1).
:::

::: example Q5 In what ways (8)
In what ways were the lives of German workers affected by industrialisation, 1890–1914? Explain your answer. (8)

**Living and working conditions.** Industrialisation drew millions from the countryside into fast-growing cities such as Berlin and the Ruhr towns. Many workers lived in overcrowded housing and worked long hours in coal mines, steelworks and factories, with the risk of injury.

**Welfare.** At the same time workers gained some protection. Insurance against sickness, accidents and old age continued, and the Workers' Protection Act of 1891 banned Sunday work and limited hours for women and children. The state's aim was to win workers away from socialism.

**Organisation and politics.** Workers joined trade unions, which had millions of members by 1914, and voted for the SPD, which became the largest party in 1912. Industrialisation gave workers a political voice, even though the Reichstag could not choose the government.

**Complexity (Level 4).** The effects pulled in different directions: harsher conditions pushed workers towards socialism, while the state's reforms tried to pull them back to loyalty. Skilled workers in the new chemical and electrical industries were better paid than miners, so experience varied. Reform did not stop the SPD's growth, which shows conditions and the lack of political rights mattered more than insurance schemes.
:::

::: example Q6 Which was more important (12)
Which of the following was the more important problem for Kaiser Wilhelm II in ruling Germany, 1890–1914: the growth of socialism; the Navy Laws? Explain your answer with reference to both bullet points. (12)

**Socialism.** Rapid industrialisation created a huge working class. The SPD, officially Marxist, grew steadily and in 1912 won 110 seats to become the largest party. This was a problem because the Kaiser and the Junkers saw it as a threat to the monarchy, while welfare reforms such as the Workers' Protection Act (1891) failed to win workers back. A socialist majority would make the budget, and so government itself, harder to manage.

**The Navy Laws.** Tirpitz's laws of 1898 and 1900 were meant to solve problems: the Navy League and industrialists supported them, and they united nationalists behind the Kaiser. But they created a new problem: cost. After the Dreadnought (1906) the fleet became far more expensive, debt rose, and new taxes needed Reichstag votes, which gave the parties leverage.

**Judgement.** Socialism was the more important problem, because it was the root of the others: the Navy Laws were partly a response to it, an attempt to rally Germans behind nationalism rather than class. And the financial problem the navy created mattered precisely because the Reichstag, with the SPD as its largest party, had to vote the money. (Level 4: both bullets explained with precise detail; the judgement links them.)
:::
