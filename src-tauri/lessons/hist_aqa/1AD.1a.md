## What it is

This lesson covers the first two bullets of Part one, **American people and the "Boom"**: the **Boom** (its benefits, advertising and the consumer society, hire purchase, mass production including Ford and the motor industry, inequalities of wealth, Republican government policies and the stock market boom) and **social and cultural developments** (entertainment, including cinema and jazz, and the position of women, including flappers).

The big idea: in the 1920s the USA became the richest country in the world, and many Americans enjoyed a new consumer lifestyle. But the boom was **uneven**: farmers, workers in old industries, African Americans and the poor were left behind, and the prosperity rested on credit and speculation.

### 1. Causes of the Boom

| Cause | How it helped |
|---|---|
| **The First World War** | The USA lent money and sold food and weapons to the Allies, joined the war late (1917) and suffered little; American firms took over markets Europe could not supply |
| **Natural resources** | Plentiful coal, oil, iron, timber and land |
| **Mass production** | Goods made faster and cheaper, so more people could afford them |
| **New industries and electricity** | Most urban homes were connected to electricity by the late 1920s, creating demand for radios, fridges, vacuum cleaners and washing machines |
| **Hire purchase** | "Buy now, pay later": customers paid a deposit and weekly or monthly instalments |
| **Advertising** | Billboards, magazines, radio and cinema persuaded people they needed new goods; mail-order catalogues and chain stores spread them |
| **Republican policies** | Low taxes, high tariffs and little regulation encouraged business (see below) |
| **Confidence** | Rising share prices and wages made people optimistic and willing to spend and borrow |

### 2. Ford and the motor industry

- **Henry Ford** introduced the **moving assembly line** at his Highland Park factory in **1913**. The car moved along a line and each worker did one simple task. Production time for a **Model T** fell from over twelve hours to about an hour and a half.
- The **Model T** ("any colour so long as it's black", as Ford reputedly said) became cheaper every year, falling to under 300 dollars by the mid-1920s; about 15 million were sold by 1927.
- Ford paid high wages (**five dollars a day** from 1914) so workers could afford the products and would stay in boring, repetitive jobs.
- **Knock-on effects:** the car industry used huge amounts of **steel, rubber, glass, oil and leather**, so it boosted those industries. New **roads**, petrol stations, motels and roadside diners appeared; **suburbs** grew because people could commute.
- The number of cars on the roads roughly **trebled** during the 1920s.

### 3. Republican government policies

The Republican presidents **Harding** (1921–23), **Coolidge** (1923–29) and **Hoover** (1929–33) believed in **laissez-faire**: government should leave business alone. Coolidge is remembered for saying that the business of America was business.
- **Low taxes**, especially on the rich and on businesses, so they would invest and spend.
- **High tariffs**: the **Fordney–McCumber Tariff (1922)** taxed imported goods, protecting American firms from foreign competition.
- **Little regulation**: few controls on business; large firms (trusts) dominated industries.
- **Rugged individualism**: people should succeed by their own efforts, not government help.

### 4. The stock market boom

- As firms grew, their **shares** rose in value. More and more ordinary people bought shares hoping to get rich: **speculation**.
- **Buying on the margin**: investors paid a small part of the share price (often about 10 per cent) and borrowed the rest from brokers, planning to repay when they sold at a profit.
- Share prices rose especially fast in **1927–29**, far above the real value of the firms. This only worked while prices kept rising.

### 5. Inequalities of wealth

| Group | Why they missed out |
|---|---|
| **Farmers** | After the war, European demand fell; with new machinery, farmers **overproduced**. Prices fell all decade, incomes dropped and many lost their farms to the banks. Between a quarter and a third of Americans lived on farms |
| **Old industries** | **Coal** faced competition from oil and electricity; **textiles** from new artificial fibres and cheaper Southern mills; **railways** from cars and lorries. Wages and jobs fell |
| **African Americans** | Most were poor sharecroppers in the South; those who moved north got the lowest-paid jobs |
| **Native Americans** | Most lived in poverty on reservations |
| **Immigrants and the unskilled** | Low wages, long hours, poor housing in city tenements |

Wealth was concentrated: the richest few per cent received a large share of all income, while many families earned too little to buy the new goods. This **underconsumption** helped cause the crash in 1929.

### 6. Social and cultural developments: entertainment

- **Cinema.** **Hollywood** became the centre of the world film industry. Tens of millions of tickets were sold every week; stars such as **Charlie Chaplin** and **Rudolph Valentino** were idolised. **The Jazz Singer (1927)** was the first major film with synchronised speech, the start of the **"talkies"**. Films spread new fashions and attitudes, and critics worried they encouraged immorality.
- **Jazz.** Developed by African American musicians in the South, especially **New Orleans**, and spread to Chicago and New York with the Great Migration. **Louis Armstrong** and **Duke Ellington** became stars; Harlem's clubs were famous. New dances such as the **Charleston** shocked the older generation. The decade is called the **"Jazz Age"**.
- **Radio.** Commercial broadcasting began in 1920; by the end of the decade millions of homes had sets, bringing music, sport, news and advertising.
- **Sport.** Baseball stars such as **Babe Ruth** and boxers such as Jack Dempsey drew huge crowds.

### 7. The position of women

- **Gains:** the **19th Amendment (1920)** gave women the **vote** in all states. During the war many had done men's jobs. More women worked in offices, shops and telephone exchanges; labour-saving electrical goods gave some middle-class women more free time; divorce became easier and more common.
- **Flappers:** young, mostly urban, middle-class women who wore short skirts and short hair, used make-up, smoked and drank in public, drove cars, danced to jazz and went out without chaperones. They symbolised a new independence.
- **Limits:** most women's lives changed little, especially in rural areas and among the poor. Women were paid less than men and mostly in low-status jobs; very few entered politics or the professions. Religious and older Americans disapproved of flappers, and groups campaigned against the new morals. Flappers were a visible minority, not typical of all women.

::: terms
- **Boom**: a period of rapid economic growth, as in the USA in the 1920s.
- **Mass production**: making large numbers of identical goods quickly and cheaply, for example on an assembly line.
- **Assembly line**: a production method where the product moves past workers who each do one task, introduced by Ford in 1913.
- **Hire purchase**: buying goods by paying a deposit and then regular instalments.
- **Consumer society**: a society in which buying goods is central to people's lives and the economy.
- **Laissez-faire**: the belief that governments should not interfere in business and the economy.
- **Tariff**: a tax on imported goods, such as the Fordney–McCumber Tariff of 1922.
- **Speculation**: buying shares hoping to sell them quickly for a profit, rather than for long-term investment.
- **Buying on the margin**: paying a small part of a share's price and borrowing the rest, to be repaid when the share was sold.
- **Overproduction**: producing more goods than can be sold, which pushed down farm prices in the 1920s.
- **Flapper**: a young woman of the 1920s who rejected traditional behaviour with short skirts, short hair, smoking and dancing.
- **Talkies**: films with synchronised sound, starting with The Jazz Singer in 1927.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 Section A at a glance.** About an hour, 40 marks: Q1 differ (4), Q2 why differ (4), Q3 more convincing (8), Q4 describe two (4), Q5 in what ways (8), Q6 which bullet (12). AQA has asked "which had more impact on America in the 1920s: the economic boom / social and cultural changes?" and "in what ways were women affected?".

1. **Q1 (4): content only.** One overall difference with a detail from each, e.g. one says the boom made everyone richer, the other that it left millions behind.
2. **Q2 (4): provenance + context.** A car salesman in 1928, a Kansas farmer, a flapper writing her memoirs, a historian with income statistics: say how position, time and purpose shaped the view.
3. **Q3 (8): test both.** Use Ford, hire purchase, the trebling of car numbers, radio and electricity **against** farmers' falling prices, coal and textiles, and unequal incomes. Judge which describes more Americans.
4. **Q4 (4): two features with precise detail.**
5. **Q5 (8): several effects; Level 4 shows how they differed** (urban versus rural women; young versus old) or how they connect.
6. **Q6 (12): explain both bullets, then compare**, e.g. mass production made the goods but Republican policy and credit sold them.
:::

::: example Q1 How interpretations differ (4)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a Chicago car salesman's memoir, published in 1950: "In the twenties everybody was buying. A man with a steady job could drive home in a new Ford, pay a few dollars a month, and have a radio in the parlour. It was the best time this country ever had." Interpretation B, also written for practice, is in the style of a historian writing in 2014: "For millions the boom never arrived. Farm families watched prices fall year after year, miners and textile workers saw wages cut, and in the cities many families earned too little to buy the goods advertised everywhere." How does Interpretation B differ from Interpretation A about who benefited from the boom? (4)

A says **almost everyone benefited**: ordinary workers could buy cars and radios using credit (1–2). B says **millions were left out**: farmers, miners, textile workers and the urban poor could not afford the new goods (+1). So A sees a shared prosperity, B an unequal one (+1). Level 2, 4/4.
:::

::: example Q3 Which is more convincing (8)
Using Interpretations A and B above: which interpretation gives the more convincing opinion about who benefited from the boom? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretations A and B. (8)

**A is convincing for many city dwellers.** Mass production made the Model T cheaper every year, and the number of cars roughly trebled in the 1920s. Hire purchase let people pay in instalments; electricity reached most urban homes, so radios, fridges and vacuum cleaners sold in millions.

**B is convincing for large groups.** Farmers overproduced after the war and prices fell all decade, so many lost their land. Coal suffered from competition with oil and electricity, and textiles from new fibres and cheaper Southern mills. Most African Americans were poor sharecroppers, and income was concentrated at the top.

**Judgement.** B is more convincing because it describes the experience of a larger share of the population: farm families alone were between a quarter and a third of Americans. A describes the urban middle class and skilled workers accurately, but even their prosperity depended on credit, and underconsumption by the many was one reason the boom ended in 1929. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Describe two (4)
Describe two features of the consumer society in 1920s America. (4)

**Hire purchase.** People could buy goods such as cars and radios by paying a deposit and then regular instalments (1). This let families buy goods they could not afford outright, so demand grew fast (1).

**Advertising.** Firms advertised on billboards, in magazines and on the new commercial radio stations (1). Adverts persuaded people they needed the latest products, such as fridges and vacuum cleaners (1).
:::

::: example Q5 In what ways (8)
In what ways were the lives of American women affected by the changes of the 1920s? Explain your answer. (8)

**Political rights.** The 19th Amendment (1920) gave women the vote in all states, though few women were elected to office.

**Work and home.** More women worked as secretaries, typists, shop assistants and telephonists. Electrical goods such as vacuum cleaners and washing machines reduced housework for those who could afford them.

**Social freedom.** Flappers wore short skirts and hair, smoked, drank, drove and danced to jazz; cinema spread their image. Divorce became more common.

**Complexity (Level 4).** The changes were limited to some women. Flappers were young, urban and mostly middle-class; rural and poor women, African American women and immigrant women saw little change. Women still earned less than men and were kept out of most professions. And the changes caused a backlash from religious and older Americans, showing how divided the decade was.
:::

::: example Q6 Which was more important (12)
Which of the following was more important in causing the boom: mass production; Republican government policies? Explain your answer with reference to both bullet points. (12)

**Mass production.** Ford's moving assembly line (1913) cut the time to build a Model T to about an hour and a half, so the price fell to under 300 dollars by the mid-1920s. Cheaper goods meant more buyers. The car industry boosted steel, rubber, glass and oil, and created roads, suburbs and service jobs.

**Republican policies.** Harding and Coolidge cut taxes, so the rich invested and spent more. The Fordney–McCumber Tariff (1922) protected American industry from imports. Laissez-faire left business free to grow, merge and use credit, and little regulation allowed the stock market boom.

**Judgement.** Mass production was more important, because it actually made goods cheap enough for millions to buy, and drove growth in other industries. Republican policies created favourable conditions, but they did not make goods; and their weaknesses (tariffs that hurt exports, no regulation of speculation) helped end the boom. (Level 4.)
:::
