## What it is

This lesson covers **Part two: Life in Elizabethan times**: **a "Golden Age"** (living standards and fashions, growing prosperity and the rise of the **gentry**, the **theatre** and attitudes to it); **the poor** (why poverty increased, attitudes and responses, why the government acted and how serious the problem was); and **English sailors** (**Hawkins** and **Drake**, the **circumnavigation of 1577–1580**, voyages and trade, and the role of **Raleigh**).

The big idea: "Golden Age" is an **interpretation**, not a fact. For the rich and the rising gentry, and for London's theatres and sailors, it was an age of wealth, confidence and achievement. For the growing numbers of poor, it was an age of rising prices, hunger and harsh laws. The best answers ask **for whom** it was golden, and use precise evidence on both sides.

### 1. A Golden Age? Prosperity and the rise of the gentry

**Growing prosperity**: the **population** grew strongly, raising demand for food, cloth and goods; **trade** expanded and London grew rapidly; landowners and merchants selling food and wool at **rising prices** grew rich.

**The gentry** were landowners below the nobility: knights, esquires and gentlemen, living on rents and farming. Their numbers and wealth grew because:
- they bought **former monastery lands** sold off since the 1530s;
- **rising food prices** and **enclosure** for sheep farming boosted their incomes;
- many entered the **law**, trade or royal service, and were educated at **grammar schools**, universities and the Inns of Court;
- they served as **Justices of the Peace** and **MPs**, giving them local power and a voice in Parliament.

**Great houses and living standards**
- The wealthy built grand **prodigy houses** such as **Longleat**, **Burghley House**, **Wollaton Hall** and **Hardwick Hall** (famous for its huge windows), often partly to host the queen on **progress**.
- Features: **symmetry** (often E- or H-shaped plans), large **glass windows** (glass was costly, so it showed wealth), **long galleries** for exercise and portraits, plasterwork, chimneys and formal **gardens**.
- The better off enjoyed more furniture, pewter, meat, sugar and spices, and more **privacy**.

**Fashion**: costly silks, velvets and lace, jewels, padded doublets, starched **ruffs** and **farthingales** showed **rank** and won attention at court. **Sumptuary laws** (renewed by royal proclamations) limited which ranks could wear certain fabrics and colours, to keep **social order** visible and limit spending on imports; they were hard to enforce.

### 2. The theatre and its achievements

**Before** purpose-built theatres, actors toured, performing in inn yards, great houses and town halls.

**The first playhouses**
- **The Theatre** (1576), built in **Shoreditch** by **James Burbage**, just outside the City of London.
- Others followed: **the Curtain** (1577), **the Rose** (1587, on Bankside, Southwark, owned by **Philip Henslowe**), **the Swan** (1590s) and **the Globe** (**1599**), built on Bankside using timbers from the dismantled Theatre, home of Shakespeare's company.
- Design: round or many-sided open-air buildings, a raised **stage** jutting into the yard, **groundlings** standing in the yard for about a penny, galleries with seats for wealthier spectators at higher prices.

**Companies and patrons**: acting companies needed a noble **patron** to be legal, because unlicensed travelling players counted as vagabonds. Examples: **Leicester's Men** (Robert Dudley), the **Lord Chamberlain's Men** (Shakespeare's company) and the **Admiral's Men** (with the star actor Edward Alleyn). Companies played at **court**, and the queen's enjoyment gave the theatre protection.

**Achievements**: a burst of new writing by **Christopher Marlowe** (Tamburlaine, Doctor Faustus), **Thomas Kyd** (The Spanish Tragedy) and **William Shakespeare** (histories, comedies and tragedies in the 1590s); plays that appealed to **all classes** at once and spread a shared culture and language. Boys played women's parts.

**Attitudes to the theatre**

| Supporters | Opponents |
|---|---|
| Elizabeth and the court enjoyed plays; nobles acted as patrons | **Puritans** saw plays as sinful: lies, cross-dressing, bad behaviour, and drawing people from church on Sundays |
| Ordinary Londoners flocked to them for cheap entertainment | The **City of London** authorities feared crowds would spread **plague**, cause disorder and crime (pickpockets, prostitution) and keep apprentices from work |
| Plays could celebrate England and the Tudor monarchy | Playhouses were **closed** in times of plague (for long periods in the early 1590s) |

Because the City authorities controlled the City itself, theatres were built **just outside** its jurisdiction, in Shoreditch and Southwark.

### 3. The poor

**Reasons for the increase in poverty**

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Population growth** | More people competing for work, land and food; wages fell behind |
| **Inflation** | Prices, especially of food, rose sharply while wages did not; earlier **debasement** of the coinage under Henry VIII had added to it |
| **Enclosure** | Landowners fenced off open fields and common land for sheep, which needed fewer workers; some villagers lost land and work |
| **Rack-renting** | Landlords raised rents steeply to keep up with prices |
| **Bad harvests** | Especially a run of failed harvests in the mid-1590s, causing hunger and high bread prices |
| **Decline in the cloth trade** | Disruption of exports (e.g. through war and trade disputes) threw spinners and weavers out of work |
| **Dissolution of the monasteries** | Monasteries, closed in the 1530s, had given charity to the poor; that safety net was gone |
| **War and disbanded soldiers** | Returning soldiers and sailors, often unpaid or injured, added to the wandering poor in the 1590s |

**Attitudes**
- Elizabethans divided the poor into the **deserving poor** (the **impotent poor**: old, sick, disabled, orphans; and the honest unemployed) and the **undeserving poor** (**sturdy beggars** or **vagabonds**: able-bodied people thought to be idle).
- Vagabonds were **feared**: they were blamed for crime, spreading disease and disorder, and seen as a threat to social order. Popular pamphlets described tricksters such as the "counterfeit crank" (faking illness).
- There was also a **Christian duty** of charity, and many people and towns gave generously.

**Responses: local and national**
- **Towns** led the way with their own schemes: some made a **census of the poor** (Norwich in 1570), raised a compulsory local rate, gave relief and found work; London used **Bridewell** as a house of correction.
- **Laws**:
  - **1563**: those who refused to contribute to poor relief could be forced to pay.
  - **1572 Vagabonds Act**: harsh punishment of vagabonds (whipping, and boring through the ear); but also a compulsory **poor rate** in every parish, collected by officials, to care for the deserving poor. This was the first national compulsory poor rate.
  - **1576 Act for Setting the Poor on Work**: towns to provide materials (such as wool) for the able-bodied poor to work on; **houses of correction** for those who refused.
  - **1597–98 Acts**: each parish to appoint **Overseers of the Poor** to collect the rate and organise relief; harsh penalties for rogues.
  - **1601 Poor Law**: brought these together. Each parish was responsible for its own poor, funded by a compulsory **poor rate**. The impotent poor received relief (money, food, or places in almshouses); the able-bodied were set to **work**; pauper children were **apprenticed**; the idle were punished in houses of correction. It lasted, with changes, for over two centuries.

**Reasons for government action**
- **Fear of disorder** and rebellion, especially in hard years like the 1590s.
- The **scale** of the problem grew and private charity could not cope.
- **Local schemes** (Norwich, Ipswich, York, London) showed what worked.
- Christian **duty** and the belief that the government should keep the social order.
- Gentry MPs and JPs saw the problem in their own areas.

**How serious was the problem?** It was most severe in the **1590s** with bad harvests, war and plague; the poorest could face starvation, and there were local riots over food. But England avoided major revolts over poverty, and the system of parish relief was a real achievement. Judge seriousness both by the **numbers and hardship** and by the government's **fears**.

### 4. English sailors

**John Hawkins**
- In the **1560s** he led voyages that seized or bought **enslaved Africans** in West Africa and sold them to Spanish colonists in the Caribbean, against Spanish law. These were among the first English slaving voyages.
- In **1568** his fleet was attacked by the Spanish at **San Juan de Ulúa** in Mexico; many men and ships were lost (Drake escaped). This increased hostility to Spain.
- Later, as **Treasurer of the Navy** (from 1577), he improved the fleet: new **race-built galleons** that were lower, longer, faster and better armed, important in 1588.

**Francis Drake**
- A Protestant from Devon, Hawkins's relative, who saw Spain as a religious and commercial enemy.
- In the early **1570s** he raided Spanish treasure routes in **Panama**, returning rich.
- **Circumnavigation, 1577–1580**: he left Plymouth in 1577 with several ships; only his ship, renamed the **Golden Hind**, completed the voyage. He sailed through the Strait of Magellan, raided Spanish ports and treasure ships along the Pacific coast of South America, claimed land on the west coast of North America (which he named **New Albion**), crossed the Pacific, traded for spices in the East Indies, and returned in **1580** with huge treasure. He was the first Englishman to sail round the world.
- **Elizabeth knighted him** aboard the Golden Hind at **Deptford** in **1581**, openly approving his raids despite Spanish anger. She was an investor and took a large share.
- Later: raids on Spanish colonies in the Caribbean (1585–86), **Cadiz** (1587), and vice-admiral against the Armada (1588).

**Importance of the voyages and trade**: **wealth** for investors, including the queen; new **joint-stock companies** such as the Muscovy and **Levant** Companies and the **East India Company** (**1600**); better **navigation** and maps, and searches for a northern passage to Asia (Frobisher); growing **rivalry with Spain**; and **national pride**, part of the confidence of the "Golden Age".

**Walter Raleigh**
- A courtier and favourite in the 1580s, rewarded with monopolies and estates.
- He organised attempts to found an English **colony** at **Roanoke** (in modern North Carolina), naming the land **Virginia** after the queen. Elizabeth did not let him go himself.
- The first settlers (1585) struggled and returned home. A second group, including women and children, landed in **1587**, but supply ships were delayed by the Armada war; when they arrived in 1590 the settlers had **vanished** (the "Lost Colony").
- **Importance**: the colonies failed, but brought back knowledge and made **tobacco** fashionable, and paved the way for later colonies.

::: terms
- **Golden Age**: a later description of Elizabeth's reign as a time of prosperity, achievement and confidence.
- **Gentry**: landowners below the nobility, such as knights and gentlemen, who grew in wealth and power.
- **Prodigy house**: a very large, showy Elizabethan country house built to display wealth and host the queen.
- **Sumptuary laws**: laws limiting which ranks could wear certain fabrics and colours.
- **Groundlings**: the poorest playgoers, who paid about a penny to stand in the yard.
- **Patron**: a noble who protected and sponsored an acting company, making it legal.
- **Inflation**: a general rise in prices, which outpaced wages in Elizabethan England.
- **Enclosure**: fencing off open fields or common land, often for sheep farming.
- **Impotent poor**: people who could not work, such as the old, sick, disabled and orphans.
- **Vagabond**: an able-bodied person wandering without work, seen as idle and dangerous.
- **Poor rate**: a compulsory local tax to pay for relief of the poor, made national in 1572.
- **Circumnavigation**: sailing all the way round the world, as Drake did in 1577–80.
:::

::: example Q1 Interpretation (8)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a historian writing a social history of Tudor England in 2006. It says: "To call Elizabeth's reign a Golden Age is to look only at the top of society. For the labourer whose wages bought less every year, and the vagrant whipped from parish to parish, it was an age of iron." How convincing is Interpretation A about Elizabethan England? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretation A. (8)

The interpretation is convincing about the **poor**. Population growth and **inflation** meant food prices rose faster than wages; **enclosure** and **rack-renting** cost some villagers their land; and the bad harvests of the mid-1590s brought hunger. The **1572 Vagabonds Act** ordered vagabonds to be whipped and bored through the ear, so "whipped from parish to parish" is accurate.

It is also right that the Golden Age was enjoyed mainly at the **top**. The gentry grew rich on rising prices and monastery lands and built **prodigy houses** such as Hardwick Hall with its expensive glass, while fashion and sumptuary laws displayed rank.

But it is too one-sided. The **theatre** was enjoyed by groundlings paying a penny, so its achievements reached ordinary Londoners. The government **did** respond to poverty: the compulsory **poor rate** (1572) and the **1601 Poor Law** gave relief to the deserving poor in every parish. So the interpretation is convincing about hardship, but understates both the shared culture and the care shown to the deserving poor. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Explain what was important (8)
Explain what was important about the voyages of Drake. (8)

Drake's voyages were important first for **wealth**. His raids on Spanish treasure in Panama in the 1570s and his **circumnavigation** of 1577–80 brought back huge treasure; the queen, an investor, took a large share. This encouraged more investment in voyages and **trade**, such as the Levant and East India Companies.

Second, they were important for **relations with Spain**. Drake attacked Spanish ports and ships in the Pacific, and Elizabeth **knighted him** aboard the Golden Hind in 1581, openly approving his piracy. Philip II saw this as provocation, so the voyages were one cause of the war that led to the Armada, where Drake was vice-admiral.

Third, they were important for **national pride and knowledge**. He was the first Englishman to sail round the world, his voyages added to English navigation and maps, and he became a Protestant hero. This confidence is part of why the reign is called a Golden Age. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of the ways in which the government's treatment of the poor changed during Elizabeth's reign. (8)

Early in the reign, relief depended on **charity**, but because poverty was growing through population rise, inflation and enclosure, an Act of **1563** let officials force people to contribute to poor relief.

As vagabonds were feared as a threat to order, the **1572 Vagabonds Act** punished them harshly with whipping and ear-boring. Yet it also introduced the first national compulsory **poor rate**, showing a new acceptance that each parish must care for the deserving poor.

Because towns such as **Norwich** had shown that the able-bodied would work if given materials, the **1576 Act** told towns to provide wool and similar materials and set up **houses of correction** for those who refused.

The crisis of the **1590s**, with bad harvests and returning soldiers, then led to the **1597–98 Acts**, which created **Overseers of the Poor**, and the **1601 Poor Law**, which made each parish responsible for relief, work, apprenticeships and correction. So policy moved from punishment and charity towards an organised, rate-funded system. (Level 4.)

- **Confusing the poor laws.** 1572: vagabonds punished, compulsory rate; 1576: work and houses of correction; 1601: the parish system.
