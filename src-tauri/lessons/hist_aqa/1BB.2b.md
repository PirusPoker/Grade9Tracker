## What it is

This lesson covers the third bullet of Part two: **the collapse of the League**: the **effects of the Depression**; the **Manchurian and Abyssinian crises and their consequences**; and the **failure of the League to avert war in 1939**.

The big idea: the League's weaknesses had been hidden in the 1920s because no great power was set on aggression. The **Depression** changed that. It pushed Japan and Italy, two permanent members of the Council, to seek empires by force, and it made Britain and France even less willing to pay for sanctions or risk war. **Manchuria (1931–33)** showed the League was slow and powerless far from Europe; **Abyssinia (1935–36)** showed that its two leading members would betray it. After 1936 collective security was dead, and the League stood aside while Europe moved towards war.

### 1. The effects of the Depression

The **Wall Street Crash (October 1929)** led to a world economic depression.

| Effect | How it damaged the League |
|---|---|
| **World trade collapsed**; countries raised **tariffs** to protect their own industries | Economic **sanctions**, the League's main weapon, became harder to use: members did not want to lose more trade |
| **Mass unemployment** and poverty | Governments focused on problems at home, not international cooperation; little money for military action |
| **Nationalist and militarist governments** gained support, promising strength and recovery | Hitler came to power in Germany (1933); the army gained control of Japan's policy; Mussolini sought glory abroad |
| Countries looked for **raw materials and markets** through expansion | Japan and Italy turned to conquest, the very aggression the League existed to stop |
| Disarmament talks stalled; the **Disarmament Conference** (1932–34) failed and Germany walked out in 1933 | The League's aim of general disarmament collapsed |

### 2. The Manchurian crisis, 1931–33

**Background.** Japan was badly hit by the Depression: the USA, its main customer, stopped buying Japanese silk. Japan had few raw materials and a fast-growing population. It already controlled the **South Manchurian Railway** in north-east China and kept troops there. Manchuria was rich in coal, iron and farmland. Japan's army leaders were increasingly independent of the government.

| Stage | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Mukden incident (September 1931)** | An explosion damaged the railway near Mukden. The Japanese army blamed Chinese soldiers (most historians believe Japanese officers staged it) and used it as an excuse to invade and occupy Manchuria |
| **China appealed** to the League | The League ordered Japan to withdraw. The Japanese army ignored it; Japan's government could not, or would not, control it |
| **Manchukuo (1932)** | Japan set up a **puppet state**, Manchukuo, with the former Chinese emperor **Puyi** as its figurehead |
| **The Lytton Commission** | The League sent a commission led by **Lord Lytton** to investigate. It took months to travel there and report; its report (published in **1932**) said Japan had acted wrongly and Manchuria should be returned to Chinese control |
| **Japan leaves (1933)** | In February 1933 the Assembly accepted the Lytton Report, with only Japan voting against. Japan **left the League** in **1933** and soon pushed further into northern China |

**Why the League failed over Manchuria**
- Japan was a **great power** and a **permanent member** of the Council.
- Manchuria was **far away**; only Britain and the USA had navies strong enough to act, and the USA was not a member.
- **Sanctions** were not imposed: members feared losing trade in the Depression, and Japan's main trading partner, the USA, was outside the League.
- Some in Britain had sympathy with Japan, seeing China as chaotic and Japan as a barrier to communism.
- The League was **slow**: the Lytton Report took a year, by which time Japan was firmly in control.

**Consequences:** an aggressor had defied the League and lost nothing; Japan simply left. Other dictators, especially Mussolini and Hitler, drew the lesson that the League could be ignored.

### 3. The Abyssinian crisis, 1935–36

**Background.** **Mussolini** wanted a new Roman empire and glory to distract Italians from economic problems. **Abyssinia** (Ethiopia), ruled by Emperor **Haile Selassie**, was one of the few independent African states and lay between two Italian colonies, Eritrea and Italian Somaliland. Italy had been humiliatingly defeated there at **Adwa in 1896**, and Mussolini wanted revenge. Abyssinia was a **League member**.

| Stage | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Wal Wal incident (December 1934)** | Italian and Abyssinian troops clashed at the **Wal Wal oasis**, which lay inside Abyssinian territory. Mussolini demanded compensation and an apology and began preparing for war. Haile Selassie appealed to the League |
| **The Stresa Front (April 1935)** | Britain, France and Italy agreed to stand together against German breaches of Versailles. Britain and France wanted to keep Mussolini as an ally against Hitler, so they **avoided** confronting him over Abyssinia |
| **Invasion (October 1935)** | Italy invaded with a large modern army, using aircraft, tanks and later **poison gas** against poorly armed Abyssinian forces |
| **Sanctions** | The League condemned Italy and imposed **economic sanctions**: no arms sales, no loans, and a ban on importing Italian goods. But they did **not include oil, coal or steel**, which Italy needed for the war, and Britain did **not close the Suez Canal**, through which Italian troops and supplies passed |
| **The Hoare-Laval Pact (December 1935)** | The British Foreign Secretary **Samuel Hoare** and the French Prime Minister **Pierre Laval** secretly agreed to offer Italy about two-thirds of Abyssinia. The plan was **leaked** to the press; there was outrage in Britain, and Hoare resigned. The plan was dropped, but the damage was done |
| **Conquest (May 1936)** | Italian forces captured the capital, **Addis Ababa**; Haile Selassie went into exile and spoke to the League in Geneva, warning that small nations would be next |
| **Sanctions dropped (1936)** | The League lifted sanctions in mid-1936. **Italy left the League in 1937** |

**Why sanctions were so weak**
- An **oil** ban was delayed for fear it would not work because the USA, outside the League, could sell oil to Italy, and for fear Mussolini would go to war with Britain and France.
- Closing the **Suez Canal** risked war with Italy, which the British government wanted to avoid.
- Britain and France put **keeping Italy as an ally against Germany** ahead of collective security.
- The Depression made members reluctant to lose trade with Italy.

### 4. Consequences of Abyssinia: why it was the death blow

| Consequence | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **The League discredited** | Its two leading members had secretly tried to reward the aggressor: the Hoare-Laval Pact showed they would betray the League's principles |
| **Collective security finished** | Small states saw that the League would not protect them, and stopped relying on it |
| **Hitler encouraged** | While attention was on Abyssinia, Hitler **remilitarised the Rhineland (March 1936)**; Britain and France did nothing |
| **Italy and Germany drew together** | The Stresa Front collapsed; Mussolini moved towards Hitler, forming the **Rome-Berlin Axis (1936)** |
| **Britain and France turned to appeasement** | They now dealt with dictators by negotiation outside the League |

**Manchuria and Abyssinia compared:** Manchuria showed the League was **slow and powerless far from Europe**. Abyssinia was more damaging because it happened **close to Europe**, involved a **weaker** aggressor whom sanctions could have hurt, and showed **Britain and France themselves** undermining the League.

### 5. The failure to avert war in 1939

- After 1936 the League was **sidelined**. It played no real part in the Spanish Civil War (1936–39), the **Anschluss** (1938), the **Sudeten crisis and Munich** (1938) or the German occupation of Czechoslovakia and invasion of Poland (1939). These were handled, if at all, by direct diplomacy among the great powers.
- Membership kept shrinking: Germany and Japan had left in 1933, Italy in 1937.
- Its last significant act was to **expel the USSR** in December 1939 for attacking Finland, after the Second World War had already begun.

**Why the League failed overall (bringing it together)**

| Long-term weaknesses | Short-term triggers |
|---|---|
| USA never a member; USSR and Germany only briefly | The Depression encouraged aggression and made sanctions unpopular |
| Unanimity, veto, slow procedures | Manchuria showed aggression could succeed |
| No army; depended on Britain and France | Abyssinia and the Hoare-Laval Pact destroyed trust |
| Tied to the Versailles settlement, which Germany hated | Hitler's challenge to Versailles from 1933 was met by appeasement, not the League |

::: terms
- **Depression**: the world economic slump that followed the Wall Street Crash of October 1929, bringing mass unemployment and collapsing trade.
- **Tariff**: a tax on imported goods, used during the Depression to protect home industries.
- **Mukden incident**: the explosion on the South Manchurian Railway in September 1931 that Japan used as an excuse to invade Manchuria.
- **Manchukuo**: the puppet state set up by Japan in Manchuria in 1932, with Puyi as its figurehead.
- **Lytton Report**: the League's 1932 report into Manchuria, which condemned Japan's actions; Japan left the League when it was accepted.
- **Puppet state**: a state that appears independent but is controlled by another power.
- **Wal Wal incident**: the clash at an oasis in Abyssinia in December 1934 that Mussolini used as an excuse for war.
- **Haile Selassie**: the Emperor of Abyssinia, who appealed to the League against the Italian invasion.
- **Hoare-Laval Pact**: the secret plan of December 1935 by the British and French foreign leaders to give Italy most of Abyssinia.
- **Suez Canal**: the waterway in Egypt, under British control, through which Italian supplies passed to Abyssinia; Britain kept it open.
- **Stresa Front**: the 1935 agreement between Britain, France and Italy to resist German breaches of Versailles, which collapsed over Abyssinia.
- **Collective security**: the principle that members act together against an aggressor, which failed in the 1930s.
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of the Manchurian crisis, 1931–33. (8)

The crisis grew out of the **Depression**. Japan's silk exports to the USA collapsed, unemployment rose, and the army argued that Japan needed Manchuria's coal, iron and land. **Because** Japan already guarded the South Manchurian Railway, its army was in position to act.

In **September 1931** an explosion on the railway near Mukden gave the army its excuse. It blamed Chinese saboteurs and **occupied Manchuria**, ignoring Japan's own government. **As a result**, China appealed to the League, which ordered Japan to withdraw. Japan ignored the order and in 1932 set up the puppet state of **Manchukuo** under Puyi.

**Because** the League could only act by investigation, it sent the **Lytton Commission**. This took many months, so by the time its report condemned Japan in 1932, Manchukuo was a fact. When the Assembly accepted the report in February 1933, Japan **walked out of the League**. No sanctions followed, because members feared losing trade in the Depression and the USA, Japan's chief trading partner, was not a member.

**The consequence** was that the League had been openly defied by a permanent member of its Council, which paid no price. This showed Mussolini and Hitler that aggression could succeed, and Japan went on to push further into China. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Two sources (12)
Source B, written for this practice question, is in the style of Haile Selassie's speech to the League Assembly in June 1936, warning that if the League abandons Abyssinia, no small nation will be safe and that it will be the turn of others tomorrow. Source C, also written for practice, is a description of an Italian poster from 1936 showing Italian soldiers bringing schools, roads and hospitals to grateful Abyssinians. How useful are Sources B and C to an historian studying the Abyssinian crisis? (12)

**Source B** is useful because it shows the **consequence** of the crisis for collective security. By June 1936 Addis Ababa had fallen, sanctions without oil had failed and the League was about to drop them. Its **provenance** gives it great weight: the emperor himself, speaking at Geneva to the members who had failed him. He has a clear purpose, to shame them into acting, but his warning was proved right when Hitler, who had already remilitarised the Rhineland, went on to the Anschluss and Czechoslovakia.

**Source C** is useful in a different way. Its content is misleading: the invasion used bombing and poison gas against a poorly armed people. But as **Italian propaganda** of 1936, it shows how Mussolini justified the war at home, presenting it as a civilising mission and building a new Roman empire, which helps explain why he could ignore the League.

**Judgement.** B is more useful for understanding the crisis's impact on the League, because it records the moment collective security was seen to fail. C is useful mainly for Mussolini's motives and methods of persuasion. Together they show both the victim's and the aggressor's views. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Essay (16)
"The main reason for the failure of the League of Nations in the 1930s was the Depression." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. (16)

**The Depression.** It turned the 1930s into a decade of aggression. Japan, hit by the collapse of its silk trade, invaded Manchuria in 1931 for raw materials and land. Mussolini looked for glory abroad, and Hitler came to power in 1933 on the back of mass unemployment. It also weakened the League's response: members who were raising tariffs and fighting unemployment would not impose sanctions that cost them trade, which is why nothing was done against Japan and why oil was left out of the sanctions on Italy.

**Structural weaknesses.** The USA's absence meant sanctions could be undermined: oil could still reach Italy, and Japan's main trading partner was outside the League. Unanimity and the lack of an army made it slow and toothless, as the year-long Lytton inquiry showed.

**Britain and France's self-interest.** In Abyssinia, a weaker aggressor close to Europe, the League could have acted. But Britain and France wanted Mussolini as an ally against Hitler after the Stresa Front, so they left the Suez Canal open and drew up the Hoare-Laval Pact. That betrayal, more than the Depression, destroyed trust in the League.

**Judgement.** The Depression created the conditions for failure by producing aggressors and making sanctions unpopular, but it was not the main reason. The decisive factor was that the League's leading members put their own interests first; Abyssinia shows the League failed even when it could have succeeded. (Level 4.)
