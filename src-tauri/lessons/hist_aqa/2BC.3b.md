## What it is

This lesson covers the third bullet of **Part three: Troubles at home and abroad**: **conflict with Spain**: the **reasons** for it, the **events**, **naval warfare** (including **tactics** and **technology**), and the **defeat of the Spanish Armada** in 1588.

The big idea: England and Spain drifted from uneasy friendship into open war over **religion**, the **Netherlands**, **trade and privateering**, and finally **Mary, Queen of Scots**. The Armada of 1588 was Philip II's attempt to end the problem by invasion. Its defeat is usually explained by a mix of **English tactics and ships**, **Spanish problems** (planning, supplies, leadership) and the **weather**. High marks come from weighing these, not listing them, and from remembering that the war did not end in 1588.

### 1. Reasons for conflict

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Religion** | **Philip II** of Spain was the leading Catholic monarch in Europe; Elizabeth was a Protestant and, after 1570, excommunicated. Philip saw restoring Catholicism in England as a duty |
| **Personal and political** | Philip had been Mary I's husband and king of England; he had proposed to Elizabeth in 1559 and been refused. At first he protected her against France, but relations soured |
| **The Netherlands** | The Netherlands belonged to Spain. Protestant Dutch rebels rose against Spanish rule from the late 1560s; Philip sent the **Duke of Alba** with a large army. An army just across the Channel worried England; Elizabeth secretly helped the rebels with money and allowed English volunteers to fight |
| **Trade and privateering** | Spain claimed a monopoly of trade with its American colonies. **Hawkins** traded enslaved Africans there illegally; **Drake** raided Spanish treasure; Elizabeth **knighted** Drake in 1581. In 1568 England also seized Spanish money (borrowed from Genoese bankers) being shipped to pay Alba's army |
| **Plots** | Spain backed plots to replace Elizabeth with Mary: the **Ridolfi Plot** (1571) and the **Throckmorton Plot** (1583), after which the Spanish ambassador was expelled |
| **Treaty of Nonsuch (1585)** | After the Dutch leader William of Orange was assassinated (1584) and Spain advanced, Elizabeth agreed in **1585** to send an **English army**, led by **Robert Dudley**, to help the Dutch. To Philip this was **open war** |
| **Mary's execution (1587)** | Mary had left her claim to Philip; her execution removed the chance of a Catholic queen without war and gave him a cause |

**Turning point.** Before the mid-1580s both sides avoided full war. The **Treaty of Nonsuch** and **Mary's execution** made it unavoidable.

### 2. Drake at Cadiz, 1587

In **April 1587** Drake led a raid on the Spanish port of **Cadiz**, where ships and supplies for the invasion were being gathered. He destroyed or captured a large number of ships, and burned a great quantity of **barrel staves** (seasoned wood for making the barrels to store food and water). He called it "singeing the King of Spain's beard".
- **Consequence**: the Armada was **delayed by about a year**, and its barrels had to be made of **unseasoned wood**, which later leaked and let food and water rot.

### 3. The Armada: plan and commanders

- **Philip's plan**: a great fleet (the "Most Fortunate Armada") of about **130 ships** would sail up the **English Channel** to the Netherlands, meet the **Duke of Parma**'s army there, and protect barges carrying it across to invade England, probably landing in Kent and marching on London.
- **Commanders**: the experienced **Marquis of Santa Cruz** died in early 1588; Philip replaced him with the **Duke of Medina Sidonia**, a great noble and able administrator with little experience at sea, who did not want the command.
- **The English fleet**: commanded by **Lord Howard of Effingham**, with **Drake** as vice-admiral and **Hawkins** and **Frobisher** commanding squadrons. It was a mix of royal ships and armed merchant ships.

**Weaknesses of the plan**
- No **deep-water port** where the Armada could shelter and meet Parma; Parma's barges were flat-bottomed and vulnerable.
- **Communication** between Medina Sidonia and Parma was slow; Parma was not ready when the Armada arrived.
- Dutch rebel ships (**flyboats**) blockaded the coast, so Parma's barges could not safely come out.

### 4. Events, summer 1588

| Stage | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Delay** | The Armada left Lisbon in May 1588 but storms forced it into **Corunna** to repair and re-supply |
| **Sighted** | It was sighted off **Cornwall** in July; **beacons** carried the news across England. The English fleet left **Plymouth** and got **upwind** of the Spanish (the "weather gauge"), so they could choose when to attack |
| **Up the Channel** | The Armada sailed in a tight **crescent** formation, protecting weaker ships. Several long-range skirmishes off **Plymouth**, **Portland** and the **Isle of Wight** did little damage either way, though two Spanish ships were lost (one captured by Drake, one damaged by an explosion). The English used up much ammunition |
| **Calais** | The Armada **anchored off Calais** to wait for Parma, who was not ready |
| **Fireships** | At night the English sent **eight fireships** (old ships set on fire, packed with flammable material) towards the anchored fleet. In panic, Spanish captains **cut their anchor cables** and scattered, breaking the crescent |
| **Gravelines** | The next day the English attacked the scattered Spanish ships at close range off **Gravelines**. Several Spanish ships were sunk or driven aground; many more were badly damaged. A change of wind saved the Armada from being driven onto the sandbanks |
| **The voyage home** | Unable to return through the Channel against the wind and the English fleet, the Armada sailed **north around Scotland** and down the west of **Ireland**. Fierce **storms** wrecked many ships on the Scottish and Irish coasts; many survivors who came ashore in Ireland were killed. Perhaps a third or more of the fleet, and far more of the men, never returned |
| **Tilbury** | Fearing that Parma might still invade, Elizabeth visited her army at **Tilbury** in August and made a famous speech saying she had the "heart and stomach of a king" |

### 5. Naval warfare: tactics and technology

**English ships**
- New **race-built galleons**, promoted by **Hawkins** as Treasurer of the Navy: lower, longer and narrower, without the tall castles at bow and stern, so they were **faster** and **more manoeuvrable**, and could sail closer to the wind.
- Armed with many long-range guns (**culverins**). Many historians argue English guns were mounted on compact **truck carriages** (small wheeled gun carriages) that made reloading quicker in a confined gun deck.
- Experienced crews, many trained in privateering, who knew local waters.

**Spanish ships**
- Large, tall ships, many converted **merchant ships**, built to carry **soldiers** and supplies; slower and harder to manoeuvre.
- Spanish **tactics** relied on getting close, **grappling** and **boarding**, so their soldiers could fight as on land. Their guns were mostly heavier and short-range, and many were on carriages designed for land use, making reloading slow; there is evidence from wrecks that some cannonballs were poorly made.

**Tactics in 1588**
- The English **stayed at a distance** in the Channel, refusing to be boarded, and used their speed and the **weather gauge**.
- The **crescent formation** protected the Armada until the **fireships** broke it.
- At **Gravelines** the English came **closer**, having seen that long range did little damage, and inflicted the heaviest losses when the Spanish were scattered.

### 6. Why was the Armada defeated?

| Factor | Evidence | Weighing |
|---|---|---|
| **English ships and guns** | Race-built galleons, faster reloading, culverins; damage at Gravelines | Decisive at Gravelines, but long-range fire in the Channel did little |
| **English tactics and leadership** | Weather gauge, refusing to be boarded, fireships at Calais; Howard and Drake | The **fireships** were the turning point: they broke the formation that had protected the Armada |
| **Spanish planning** | No deep-water port; Parma not ready; poor communication; Dutch blockade | A deep flaw: even an undefeated Armada could not easily carry Parma across |
| **Spanish leadership** | Medina Sidonia inexperienced, though he kept discipline well | Less important than the plan itself |
| **Supplies** | Unseasoned barrels after Cadiz; food and water rotted; sickness | Weakened the crews, especially on the long voyage home |
| **Weather and luck** | Storms at Corunna; the wind that drove the Armada north; storms off Scotland and Ireland | Destroyed most of the ships that were lost, but only after the battle had already been decided |

**Judgement.** The English did not destroy most of the Armada in battle: most ships were lost to **storms** on the way home. But the English **prevented** the invasion: the fireships and Gravelines broke the formation and drove the Armada past Parma. Most historians see the defeat as a combination, with the **flawed plan** making failure likely and English **tactics** ensuring it. Elizabethans themselves called the wind a "Protestant wind", seeing God's hand.

### 7. Impact and the war after 1588

- **Impact**: a huge boost to English **confidence** and Protestant identity; Elizabeth's prestige as Gloriana grew (the "Armada Portrait"); England was safe from invasion that year. English Catholics had stayed **loyal**.
- **But the war went on until 1604.** An English counter-attack (the "English Armada") in **1589** failed. Spain rebuilt its navy and sent further armadas in the **1590s** (scattered by storms). English forces raided **Cadiz** again in **1596** (Essex). Drake and Hawkins both died on a failed Caribbean expedition in the mid-1590s. Spain supported rebels in **Ireland**, landing troops at **Kinsale** in 1601. The war was **costly** and contributed to the problems of the 1590s.

### 8. If you sit in June 2027: the Armada as the "site"

For June 2027 AQA's specified site for this option is **the Spanish Armada**. A site question asks how the **geography** and setting shaped events.
- **The Channel**: prevailing **westerly winds** pushed the Armada east; the English, leaving Plymouth, worked **upwind** and could attack at will.
- **Calais**: an open anchorage, not a sheltered harbour, so the fleet was exposed to fireships; Parma's ports further north were shallow and blockaded by the Dutch.
- **Gravelines and the Flemish banks**: the shallow sandbanks threatened to wreck the scattered Spanish ships.
- **Around Scotland and Ireland**: a long route in unfamiliar, stormy northern waters, with poor charts, damaged ships and short supplies; many ships were wrecked on the rocky west coast of Ireland, and some wrecks have been found and studied by archaeologists, giving evidence about Spanish guns and supplies.
- **On land**: beacons along the coast, defences and the army at **Tilbury** on the Thames, show how England prepared for invasion.

::: terms
- **Privateering**: attacks on enemy ships by privately owned armed ships with the monarch's approval, such as Drake's raids.
- **Treaty of Nonsuch**: Elizabeth's 1585 agreement to send an English army to help the Dutch rebels against Spain.
- **Singeing the King of Spain's beard**: Drake's description of his 1587 raid on Cadiz, which delayed the Armada.
- **Armada**: the Spanish fleet of about 130 ships sent in 1588 to escort an invasion of England.
- **Race-built galleon**: a lower, longer, faster English warship without tall castles, promoted by Hawkins.
- **Culverin**: a long-range gun used on English ships.
- **Weather gauge**: being upwind of the enemy, which lets a fleet choose when and how to attack.
- **Crescent formation**: the Armada's defensive formation, with strong ships protecting weaker ones in the centre.
- **Fireship**: an old ship set alight and sent towards an enemy fleet, as at Calais.
- **Protestant wind**: the Elizabethan name for the winds and storms seen as God's help against the Armada.
:::

::: example Q1 Interpretation (8)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a historian writing a naval history in 2008. It says: "The Armada was beaten by the weather, not by the English. Their guns did little damage in the Channel, and it was the gales off Scotland and Ireland that sank most of Philip's ships." How convincing is Interpretation A about the defeat of the Spanish Armada? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretation A. (8)

Interpretation A is convincing in parts. In the **Channel** the English kept their distance and their long-range fire did little damage to the Armada's **crescent** formation, and only two Spanish ships were lost there. It is also right that **storms** on the voyage **around Scotland and Ireland** destroyed most of the ships that were lost, many wrecked on the Irish coast.

But it is unconvincing to say the English did not beat the Armada. The **fireships** at **Calais** made the Spanish cut their cables and scatter, breaking the formation that had protected them, and at **Gravelines** the English closed in and sank or crippled several ships. This, with the wind, drove the Armada **past Parma**, so the invasion was already defeated before the storms. The Spanish plan was also flawed, with no deep-water port and Parma's barges blockaded by the Dutch.

So the interpretation is convincing about how the ships were lost, but not about why the invasion failed. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Explain what was important (8)
Explain what was important about Drake's raid on Cadiz in 1587. (8)

The raid was important first because it **delayed the Armada**. Drake destroyed or captured many ships and supplies being gathered at Cadiz, and the invasion had to be put off by about a year, giving England more time to prepare.

Second, it was important for the Armada's **supplies**. Drake burned a huge stock of seasoned barrel staves, so the barrels later made for the Armada used **unseasoned wood**. These leaked, and food and water rotted, which weakened crews and added to the suffering on the long voyage home.

Third, it was important for **morale and the war**. Drake boasted of "singeing the King of Spain's beard": it showed English sailors could strike at Spain in its own harbours, boosting confidence, but it also made Philip even more determined to invade. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of the ways in which relations between England and Spain broke down between 1568 and 1588. (8)

In **1568** relations worsened when the Spanish attacked Hawkins at **San Juan de Ulúa** and England seized Spanish money meant for Alba's army in the **Netherlands**. Because Alba's army just across the Channel looked threatening, Elizabeth began secretly helping the **Dutch rebels**.

Spain responded by backing **plots**: the **Ridolfi Plot** (1571) and the **Throckmorton Plot** (1583), after which the Spanish ambassador was expelled. Meanwhile **Drake** raided Spanish treasure and, after his **circumnavigation**, was **knighted** in 1581, so Philip saw Elizabeth as supporting piracy.

When William of Orange was assassinated in 1584, Elizabeth signed the **Treaty of Nonsuch** (1585) and sent an army under Dudley, which Philip treated as war. The **execution of Mary** in 1587 gave him a Catholic cause, Drake's Cadiz raid delayed him, and in **1588** the **Armada** sailed. (Level 4.)
