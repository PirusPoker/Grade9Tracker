## What it is

This lesson covers the first two bullets of Part two, **Bust: Americans' experiences of the Depression and New Deal**: **American society during the Depression** (unemployment, farmers, businessmen, Hoover's responses and unpopularity, and Roosevelt's election as president), and **the effectiveness of the New Deal on different groups in society** (successes and limitations, opposition from the **Supreme Court**, **Republicans** and **radical politicians**, **Roosevelt's contribution** as president, and **popular culture**).

The big idea: the Wall Street Crash turned into the worst depression in American history. Hoover's limited response made him deeply unpopular; Roosevelt's New Deal used the federal government on an unprecedented scale. It brought relief and hope and changed the role of government permanently, but it did not end the Depression and helped some groups far more than others.

### 1. The Wall Street Crash, October 1929

- **Causes:** overproduction and **underconsumption** (unequal incomes meant many could not buy goods); **speculation** and **buying on the margin**; weak, unregulated **banks**; falling exports because of tariffs; and a sudden **loss of confidence**.
- **Events:** prices began to fall in September and October 1929. On **Black Thursday (24 October)** panic selling began; on **Black Tuesday (29 October)** a record number of shares were sold and prices collapsed. Margin buyers could not repay their loans; many investors and banks were ruined.

### 2. American society during the Depression

| Group | Experience |
|---|---|
| **The unemployed** | Unemployment rose from about 1.5 million in 1929 to about **12–13 million by 1933**, roughly **a quarter of the workforce**. Many more were on short time or reduced wages. There was little unemployment benefit; people relied on charity and **soup kitchens** |
| **The homeless** | Families evicted from their homes lived in shanty towns of shacks, nicknamed **"Hoovervilles"**. Hundreds of thousands of men travelled the country on freight trains looking for work ("hobos") |
| **Farmers** | Prices for crops collapsed further; many could not pay their mortgages and their farms were repossessed. From the early 1930s drought and soil erosion created the **Dust Bowl** on the Great Plains; thousands of families (often called "Okies") moved west to California |
| **Businessmen and banks** | Thousands of banks failed between 1929 and 1933, wiping out savers. Businesses lost customers and credit; many went bankrupt. Even the rich lost fortunes on the stock market |
| **African Americans** | Often "last hired, first fired"; unemployment was higher than for white workers |

### 3. Hoover's response and unpopularity

**President Herbert Hoover** (1929–33) believed in **rugged individualism** and **voluntarism**: charities and local government, not federal handouts, should help the poor, and the economy would right itself. He did more than his reputation suggests:
- Asked businesses to keep wages and jobs.
- Cut taxes (1929) and supported public works such as the **Hoover Dam** (begun in 1931).
- The **Federal Farm Board** bought surplus crops; the **Reconstruction Finance Corporation (1932)** lent money to banks, railways and businesses.

But:
- The **Hawley–Smoot Tariff (1930)** raised tariffs further; other countries retaliated and trade collapsed.
- He refused direct federal relief for the unemployed and kept saying recovery was near.
- **The Bonus Army (1932).** Thousands of First World War veterans marched to Washington to ask for early payment of a bonus due in 1945, camping in a shanty town. In July 1932 troops under **General MacArthur** cleared them with tear gas, bayonets and tanks and burned the camp. Hoover looked heartless.

**The 1932 election.** Franklin D. Roosevelt (Democrat) promised a **"New Deal"** for the American people, and won a landslide, carrying all but six states.

### 4. The New Deal

**Roosevelt (FDR)** took office in **March 1933**. In the **Hundred Days** Congress passed a flood of laws. His aims are often summed up as the **three Rs: relief, recovery and reform**.

| Measure | What it did |
|---|---|
| **Emergency Banking Act (1933)** | A **bank holiday** closed all banks; only sound ones reopened, under government supervision. Confidence returned and deposits flowed back |
| **Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC, 1933)** | Work for young unemployed men in the countryside: planting trees, flood control, national parks; most of their pay was sent home to their families |
| **Agricultural Adjustment Act (AAA, 1933)** | Paid farmers to **produce less**, so that prices would rise |
| **Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA, 1933)** | Built dams on the Tennessee River across several states: flood control, cheap **electricity**, jobs in one of the poorest regions |
| **National Recovery Administration (NRA, 1933)** | Codes for fair wages, hours and prices; firms that joined displayed a **blue eagle** symbol |
| **Federal Emergency Relief Administration (FERA, 1933)** | Federal money for state relief schemes |
| **Public Works Administration (PWA, 1933)** | Large building projects: schools, hospitals, bridges |
| **The Second New Deal (1935)**: **Works Progress Administration (WPA)** | Millions of jobs on roads, buildings and airports, and for artists, writers and actors |
| **Social Security Act (1935)** | **Old-age pensions**, **unemployment insurance** and help for disabled people and dependent children |
| **Wagner Act (1935)** | Gave workers the right to join **trade unions** and bargain collectively |

### 5. Effectiveness for different groups

| Group | Successes | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **The unemployed** | Millions given work by the CCC, PWA and WPA; unemployment fell substantially by 1937 | It rose again in the **1937–38 recession** when FDR cut spending; it was still near ten million in 1938. Full employment came only with the Second World War |
| **Farmers** | AAA payments raised prices and incomes; loans saved many farms | Big landowners gained most; many **sharecroppers and tenants**, often African American, were evicted when land was taken out of production; the Dust Bowl continued |
| **Workers** | Wagner Act and union growth; NRA codes; Social Security | Many low-paid groups (farm labourers, domestic servants) were excluded from Social Security at first |
| **African Americans** | Some gained jobs and relief; Eleanor Roosevelt spoke out against discrimination; FDR's informal "Black Cabinet" of advisers | Agencies were often **segregated** or paid less; the AAA hurt Black sharecroppers; FDR would not back an anti-lynching law for fear of losing Southern Democrats |
| **Women** | **Frances Perkins** became the first woman cabinet member (Labour Secretary) | Some NRA codes allowed lower pay for women; few places in the CCC |
| **Native Americans** | The Indian Reorganisation Act (1934) gave tribes more control over their land | Poverty remained severe |

### 6. Opposition to the New Deal

- **The Supreme Court.** Conservative judges ruled that parts of the New Deal broke the Constitution by giving the federal government too much power: the **NRA was declared unconstitutional in 1935** and the AAA in 1936. In 1937 FDR proposed adding up to six new judges to the Court (**"court packing"**); it was widely condemned and failed, but the Court afterwards upheld New Deal laws such as Social Security.
- **Republicans and businessmen.** They said the New Deal was too expensive, raised taxes and debt, interfered with business, strengthened unions and was a step towards **socialism** or dictatorship. Wealthy critics formed the **American Liberty League** (1934).
- **Radical politicians** said it did **not go far enough**:
  - **Huey Long**, senator for Louisiana: the **"Share Our Wealth"** plan to take money from the rich and give every family a guaranteed income. He was assassinated in 1935.
  - **Father Charles Coughlin**, the "radio priest", attacked bankers and FDR to millions of listeners.
  - **Dr Francis Townsend** proposed generous pensions for everyone over 60 who would spend the money; his clubs helped push FDR towards Social Security.

### 7. Roosevelt's contribution and popular culture

- **Roosevelt's contribution:** energy and willingness to experiment; restoring **confidence** (his inaugural address told Americans they had nothing to fear but fear itself); his **fireside chats**, radio talks explaining his policies directly to the public; disabled by polio, he projected strength; he was re-elected in **1936** by a landslide, and again in 1940 and 1944. **Eleanor Roosevelt** travelled widely and championed the poor, women and African Americans.
- **Popular culture in the 1930s:** cinema offered **escapism**; tens of millions still went every week to see musicals, comedies (the Marx Brothers), child star Shirley Temple, gangster films and later epics such as *Gone with the Wind* and *The Wizard of Oz* (both 1939). **Radio** brought comedies, serials, swing music and FDR's chats into homes. **Swing** and big bands (such as Benny Goodman's) became popular, and comic-book heroes such as Superman (1938) appeared.

::: terms
- **Wall Street Crash**: the collapse of share prices on the New York Stock Exchange in October 1929.
- **Depression**: a long, severe economic slump with mass unemployment, as in the USA from 1929 to the late 1930s.
- **Hooverville**: a shanty town of homeless people during the Depression, named mockingly after President Hoover.
- **Rugged individualism**: Hoover's belief that people should help themselves rather than rely on government.
- **Bonus Army**: the First World War veterans who marched on Washington in 1932 and were driven out by the army.
- **Dust Bowl**: the area of the Great Plains where drought and soil erosion destroyed farms in the 1930s.
- **New Deal**: Roosevelt's programme of relief, recovery and reform from 1933.
- **Hundred Days**: the first months of Roosevelt's presidency in 1933, when many New Deal laws were passed.
- **Alphabet agencies**: the New Deal organisations known by their initials, such as the CCC, AAA, TVA and NRA.
- **Social Security Act**: the 1935 law creating old-age pensions and unemployment insurance.
- **Fireside chats**: Roosevelt's radio broadcasts explaining his policies directly to the American people.
- **Court packing**: Roosevelt's failed 1937 plan to add judges to the Supreme Court.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 Section A at a glance.** About an hour, 40 marks: Q1 differ (4), Q2 why differ (4), Q3 more convincing (8), Q4 describe two (4), Q5 in what ways (8), Q6 which bullet (12). AQA asked in 2023: "In what ways were the lives of American people affected by the Depression?" Other favourites: interpretations on the success of the New Deal, and "Supreme Court or radical critics: which was the greater problem for Roosevelt?".

1. **Q1 (4): content only.** E.g. one says the New Deal saved America, the other that it failed to end unemployment.
2. **Q2 (4): provenance + context.** A CCC worker, a businessman paying higher taxes, a Black sharecropper evicted under the AAA, a historian with unemployment figures: explain how experience and purpose shape each view.
3. **Q3 (8): test both.** Bank holiday, CCC, TVA, WPA, Social Security, Wagner Act, FDR's landslide in 1936 **against** the 1937–38 recession, unemployment near ten million in 1938, segregation and the Supreme Court. Judge using a criterion: relief, recovery or reform?
4. **Q4 (4): two features with precise detail.**
5. **Q5 (8): Level 4 shows how effects differed** between groups (unemployed, farmers, businessmen, African Americans) or how one effect led to another.
6. **Q6 (12): explain both bullets with evidence, then judge** with a clear reason.
:::

::: example Q1 How interpretations differ (4)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a man who joined the CCC in 1933, interviewed in 1978: "Roosevelt gave us our self-respect back. I planted trees in Montana and sent money home every month. My mother cried when the first cheque came. That man saved this country." Interpretation B, also written for practice, is in the style of a historian writing in 2012: "The New Deal gave relief but not recovery. Unemployment was still in the millions in 1938, and it took the war, not Roosevelt's agencies, to put America back to work." How does Interpretation B differ from Interpretation A about the New Deal? (4)

A says the New Deal **saved the country**, giving people work, money and self-respect (1–2). B says it **failed to bring recovery**: it gave relief, but unemployment stayed in the millions until the war (+1). So A judges it by its effect on individuals' lives and morale, B by whether it ended the Depression (+1). Level 2, 4/4.
:::

::: example Q3 Which is more convincing (8)
Using Interpretations A and B above: which interpretation gives the more convincing opinion about the New Deal? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretations A and B. (8)

**A is convincing about relief and confidence.** The bank holiday restored confidence; the CCC gave millions of young men work and sent wages home; the WPA employed millions more; Social Security (1935) created pensions and unemployment insurance. Roosevelt's fireside chats and his landslide re-election in 1936 show how many Americans felt he had saved them.

**B is convincing about recovery.** Unemployment fell by 1937 but rose again in the 1937–38 recession when FDR cut spending, and was still near ten million in 1938. Full employment came only with war production after 1941. Some groups gained little, such as sharecroppers evicted under the AAA.

**Judgement.** B is more convincing as an economic verdict, because the New Deal's own aim of recovery was not achieved before the war. But A is more convincing about what the New Deal changed for good: it ended starvation, saved the banks and democracy, and created a permanent welfare system. The New Deal succeeded in relief and reform, and only partly in recovery. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Describe two (4)
Describe two problems faced by farmers during the Depression. (4)

**Falling prices.** Crop prices, already low in the 1920s, collapsed after 1929 (1). Many farmers could not repay mortgages and their farms were repossessed by the banks (1).

**The Dust Bowl.** In the 1930s drought and over-farming turned parts of the Great Plains into dust (1). Topsoil blew away in huge dust storms, ruining farms, and thousands of families moved west to California looking for work (1).
:::

::: example Q5 In what ways (8)
In what ways were the lives of American people affected by the Depression? Explain your answer. (8)

**Unemployment.** By 1933 about 12–13 million, a quarter of the workforce, were unemployed, with little government help. Families relied on charity and soup kitchens.

**Homes and savings.** Thousands of banks failed, wiping out savings; families were evicted and lived in Hoovervilles; men rode freight trains looking for work.

**Farmers.** Prices collapsed, farms were repossessed, and the Dust Bowl drove families west.

**Complexity (Level 4).** The effects varied. Industrial workers lost jobs; farmers lost land; businessmen and investors lost fortunes; African Americans, last hired and first fired, suffered most. And economic hardship had political effects: the Bonus Army's treatment in 1932 turned many against Hoover, leading to Roosevelt's landslide.
:::

::: example Q6 Which was the greater problem (12)
Which of the following was the greater problem for Roosevelt's New Deal: the Supreme Court; radical critics? Explain your answer with reference to both bullet points. (12)

**The Supreme Court.** It declared the NRA unconstitutional in 1935 and the AAA in 1936, striking down two central agencies. FDR's attempt to add judges in 1937 failed and damaged his reputation, splitting his own party.

**Radical critics.** Huey Long's Share Our Wealth movement attracted millions and could have split the Democratic vote in 1936; Father Coughlin attacked FDR on radio to millions; Townsend's pension campaign was popular. They argued the New Deal did too little.

**Judgement.** The Supreme Court was the greater problem, because it could legally destroy New Deal laws, and the court-packing row weakened FDR politically. The radicals were a threat mainly in 1934–35, and FDR answered them with the Second New Deal (Social Security, the WPA); Long's assassination in 1935 removed the most dangerous. After 1937 the Court upheld the New Deal, but by then its damage was done. (Level 4.)
