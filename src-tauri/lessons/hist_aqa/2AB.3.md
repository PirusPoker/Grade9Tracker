## What it is

This lesson covers **Part three: Reform and reformers** (c1780 to c1900): **radical protest** and **Peterloo**; the **Great Reform Act** (1832) and later reform; **Chartism**; campaigning groups (the **anti-slavery movement**, the **Anti-Corn Law League**, **factory and social reformers**); and **workers' movements**, from the **GNCTU** and the **Tolpuddle Martyrs** to **New Model Unions** and the **match girls' and dockers' strikes**.

The big idea: industrialisation created new classes, the **industrial middle class** and the **urban working class**, who had little or no voice in Parliament. They used **peaceful pressure**: petitions, newspapers, mass meetings, boycotts and unions. Governments first **repressed** them, then gradually **reformed**. Many campaigns failed at the time but their aims were later achieved.

### 1. Radical protest before 1832

- **Thomas Paine's** **Rights of Man** (1791–92), inspired by the **French Revolution**, argued that government should rest on the people's consent. It sold widely; the government, fearing revolution, banned it and suppressed radical societies.
- After the wars with France ended in **1815**, **unemployment** and dear bread (the **Corn Laws**, 1815) fuelled demands for reform.
- **Peterloo** (**16 August 1819**): a huge, peaceful crowd gathered at **St Peter's Field, Manchester**, to hear the radical speaker **Henry Hunt** call for reform. The local **yeomanry** (volunteer cavalry) charged to arrest him; around fifteen or more people were killed and hundreds injured. The name mocked the victory at Waterloo.
- The government responded with the **Six Acts** (1819), restricting public meetings and radical newspapers. Repression was the first answer to protest.

### 2. The Great Reform Act (1832)

**The old system:** seats were not linked to population. **Rotten boroughs** (such as Old Sarum, with almost no voters) elected MPs, while big industrial towns such as **Manchester** and **Birmingham** had **none**. **Pocket boroughs** were controlled by a landowner. Voting was public, so bribery and intimidation were common.

**Causes of reform:**
- The **industrial middle class** wanted political power to match its wealth.
- **Political unions** (such as Thomas Attwood's **Birmingham Political Union**) organised mass support.
- The **Whigs**, under **Earl Grey**, came to power in **1830** promising reform; revolution in France in 1830 raised fears.
- When the **House of Lords** rejected the Reform Bill (1831), there were **riots** (for example in Bristol and Nottingham). In the **Days of May** (1832), **King William IV** agreed to create enough new Whig peers, so the Lords gave way.

**Terms:** many **rotten boroughs** lost their MPs; seats went to new **industrial towns** and to counties; in boroughs, men occupying property worth **£10 a year** got the vote.

**Impact:** the **middle class** gained the vote, but the electorate was still only a minority of men (about one in five in England and Wales); the **working class** and **all women** were excluded; there was no secret ballot. Workers felt **betrayed**, which fed **Chartism**. But it proved that the system **could be changed**, opening the door to more reform.

**Further reform:**

| Act | Change |
|---|---|
| **1867 Second Reform Act** | Vote for male **householders** in the boroughs, including many skilled working men; the electorate roughly doubled |
| **1872 Ballot Act** | **Secret ballot**, reducing bribery and intimidation |
| **1884 Third Reform Act** | Household vote extended to the **counties**, so many agricultural labourers could vote; a majority of men now had the vote |

### 3. Chartism (1838 to c1850)

**Causes:** disappointment with **1832**; the harsh **Poor Law Amendment Act** (**1834**), which forced the poor into **workhouses**; economic **depression** and unemployment in the late 1830s and 1840s; anger at the treatment of the Tolpuddle Martyrs.

**The People's Charter** (1838), drafted by **William Lovett** of the London Working Men's Association, had **six points**:
1. a vote for every man over 21;
2. a **secret ballot**;
3. no **property qualification** for MPs;
4. **payment** of MPs;
5. **equal constituencies**;
6. **annual** parliaments.

**Actions:**
- Huge **petitions** to Parliament in **1839**, **1842** and **1848**, each with millions of signatures claimed. All were rejected. The 1848 petition, after a mass meeting at **Kennington Common**, was mocked because many signatures were found to be fake.
- **Moral force** (Lovett): education, petitions and persuasion. **Physical force** (**Feargus O'Connor**, whose newspaper the **Northern Star** spread the movement): threats of violence if peaceful methods failed.
- The **Newport Rising** (**1839**): Chartists marched on Newport in Wales; soldiers fired on them, killing many, and the leaders were transported.

**Why it failed:** divided leaders; a strong government response (arrests, troops moved by **railway**); the economy **improved** in the later 1840s; the middle class backed the Anti-Corn Law League instead; the 1848 petition's fake names.

**Long-term impact:** **five of the six points** later became law: no property qualification (1858), the secret ballot (1872), payment of MPs (1911), votes for all men (1918) and roughly equal constituencies. Only annual parliaments never came. Chartism showed that working people could organise nationally.

### 4. Campaigning groups

**The anti-slavery movement**
- The **Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade** (1787), with **Thomas Clarkson** and **Granville Sharp**, gathered evidence; **William Wilberforce** led the campaign in **Parliament**.
- Methods: **petitions**, public meetings, a **sugar boycott**, books and pamphlets, and powerful images such as **Josiah Wedgwood's** medallion of a kneeling, chained African.
- **Olaudah Equiano**, a formerly enslaved man, published his life story (1789), which gave a first-hand account of the trade.
- **Enslaved people's resistance**, such as the revolution in **Haiti** (from 1791) and the large rising in **Jamaica** (1831–32), showed that slavery could not last and made planters fear further revolts.
- **Results:** the **slave trade** was abolished in the British Empire in **1807**; **slavery** itself by the **Slavery Abolition Act** (**1833**). Slave owners received large **compensation**; the formerly enslaved got none, and many had to work as "apprentices" for some years.

**The Anti-Corn Law League** (from 1838–39)
- Led by **Richard Cobden** and **John Bright**, Manchester businessmen. The **Corn Laws** kept the price of grain and bread high, protecting landowners.
- A **middle-class**, well-funded and well-organised campaign: lecturers travelled by **railway**; pamphlets went out by the new **penny post** (1840); it ran newspapers, held meetings and won seats in Parliament.
- **Repeal** came in **1846**, when Prime Minister **Robert Peel**, persuaded by the League's arguments and by the **Irish famine**, split his own Conservative Party to get it through.

**Factory and social reformers**
- **Richard Oastler**, **Michael Sadler** and **Lord Ashley** (later the **Earl of Shaftesbury**) campaigned against long hours and child labour in factories and mines, using evidence from parliamentary inquiries.
- Laws included the **1833 Factory Act** (limits on child labour in textile mills, with **inspectors**), the **1842 Mines Act** (no women or young children underground) and the **Ten Hours Act** (**1847**), limiting women and young people in textile mills to ten hours a day.
- **Social reformers** campaigned for those without a voice: Shaftesbury backed **ragged schools** for poor children, and **Elizabeth Fry** worked to improve prisons.

### 5. Workers' movements and trade unions

| Development | Key points |
|---|---|
| **GNCTU** (1834) | The **Grand National Consolidated Trades Union**, linked to **Robert Owen**, aimed to unite all workers in one union. It grew fast but **collapsed** within the year: poor organisation, employers forcing workers to sign "the document" giving up union membership, and government hostility |
| **Tolpuddle Martyrs** (1834) | Six farm labourers in **Dorset**, led by **George Loveless**, formed a union and were sentenced to **seven years' transportation** to Australia for swearing a secret oath. A mass **petition** and a London **march** led to their **pardon** (1836); they became heroes of the union movement |
| **New Model Unions** (from 1851) | Unions of **skilled** workers, such as the **Amalgamated Society of Engineers**, with high subscriptions, paid officials, sickness and unemployment **benefits**, and a preference for **negotiation** over strikes. The **TUC** was founded in 1868; laws in the 1870s gave unions legal protection |
| **New unionism**: the **match girls' strike** (1888) | Women workers at **Bryant and May** in London, angered by low pay, fines and **"phossy jaw"** (bone disease from phosphorus), struck after an article by **Annie Besant** exposed conditions. Public support won better conditions |
| **The dock strike** (1889) | London dockers, led by **Ben Tillett**, **John Burns** and **Tom Mann**, struck for a wage of "the dockers' tanner" (sixpence an hour). Donations, including from Australian workers, helped them win. **Unskilled** workers' unions grew rapidly afterwards |

**Impact:** unions moved from being treated as criminal conspiracies to **legal, respected** organisations; unskilled workers organised; unions helped found the **Labour Party** (1900), giving workers a political voice.

### 6. Factors in Part three

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **The economy** | Industrialisation created the middle and working classes; depressions fuelled Peterloo and Chartism; the Corn Laws; wages and conditions drove unions |
| **Ideas** | Paine's rights; democracy and representation; humanitarian and Christian ideas behind abolition; free trade |
| **Communication** | Pamphlets, the Northern Star, the penny post, railways, petitions, mass meetings, Wedgwood's medallion |
| **Government** | Repression (Six Acts, Tolpuddle, Newport) then reform (1832, 1867, 1884, factory acts, repeal) |
| **Individuals** | Paine, Grey, Lovett, O'Connor, Wilberforce, Cobden, Shaftesbury, Besant |
| **War** | Wars with France brought fear of revolution and post-war hardship |

::: terms
- **Rotten borough**: a constituency with very few voters that still elected MPs, abolished in 1832.
- **Peterloo**: the 1819 killing of peaceful reform protesters by yeomanry at St Peter's Field, Manchester.
- **Great Reform Act**: the 1832 law that redistributed seats to industrial towns and gave the vote to middle-class men.
- **People's Charter**: the Chartists' six demands of 1838, including votes for all men and a secret ballot.
- **Moral force**: the Chartist strategy of peaceful persuasion, education and petitions, led by William Lovett.
- **Physical force**: the Chartist strategy, associated with Feargus O'Connor, of threatening violence if peaceful methods failed.
- **Abolition**: ending the slave trade (1807) and then slavery itself (1833) in the British Empire.
- **Anti-Corn Law League**: the middle-class pressure group led by Cobden and Bright that won repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846.
- **Ten Hours Act**: the 1847 law limiting women and young people in textile mills to ten hours' work a day.
- **Tolpuddle Martyrs**: six Dorset labourers transported in 1834 for swearing a union oath, pardoned in 1836.
- **New Model Unions**: skilled workers' unions from 1851 offering benefits and favouring negotiation over strikes.
- **New unionism**: the growth of unions for unskilled workers after the match girls' (1888) and dockers' (1889) strikes.
:::

::: example Q1 Source utility (8)
Source A, written for this practice question, is a description of a print published by a radical publisher in Manchester in late August 1819. It shows cavalrymen with raised swords riding into a crowd of men, women and children in their best clothes, holding banners reading "Reform" and "Liberty". How useful is Source A to an historian studying protest about the vote in the early 19th century? Explain your answer using Source A and your contextual knowledge. (8)

Source A is useful because it shows the **Peterloo Massacre** of **16 August 1819**, when the yeomanry charged a crowd at St Peter's Field who had come to hear **Henry Hunt** demand **parliamentary reform**. The banners show the crowd's aims, and the best clothes and children suggest a **peaceful** meeting, which fits accounts that the crowd was unarmed.

Its **provenance** makes it useful in a different way. It was published by a **radical** within days, so it is **propaganda** designed to turn opinion against the authorities, and it may exaggerate the soldiers' brutality. But that tells the historian how radicals used Peterloo to win **sympathy**, and how angry many people were. The government, fearing revolution, responded with the **Six Acts**.

So the source is very useful for the **aims** of reformers and the **outrage** at Peterloo, though an historian would need official sources to see why the authorities acted as they did. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Significance of the Great Reform Act (8)
Explain the significance of the Great Reform Act. (8)

**At the time**, the 1832 Act was significant because it was the first major reform of Parliament. It removed many **rotten boroughs** and gave seats to industrial towns such as **Manchester** and **Birmingham**, and the **£10** property qualification gave the vote to much of the **middle class**. Riots and the Days of May showed that outside pressure could work.

But its significance at the time was **limited**: only about one in five men in England and Wales could vote, there was no secret ballot, and the working class was excluded. This sense of betrayal led directly to **Chartism**.

**Over time**, it was significant because it broke the idea that the system could not change. It began a process that led to the **1867** and **1884** Reform Acts, the **secret ballot** (1872) and eventually votes for all adults in **1918** and **1928**. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Difference (8)
Explain two ways in which Chartism and the Anti-Corn Law League were different. (8)

**First, they had different supporters and resources.** The **Anti-Corn Law League** was a **middle-class** movement, led by Manchester businessmen **Cobden and Bright**, with plenty of money to pay lecturers, print pamphlets and send them by the **penny post**. **Chartism** was mainly a **working-class** movement, with little money, relying on mass petitions, meetings and O'Connor's **Northern Star**.

**Second, they had different results.** The League had **one clear aim**, repeal of the Corn Laws, and it succeeded in **1846**, when Peel was persuaded to repeal them. The Chartists had **six demands**, which many MPs saw as dangerously democratic; their petitions of **1839, 1842 and 1848** were all rejected, and the movement was divided between moral and physical force. Chartism failed at the time, though most of its points were achieved later. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Factor essay (16)
Has the economy been the main factor in the extension of rights in Britain? Explain your answer with reference to the economy and other factors. Use a range of examples from across your study of Power and the people: c1170 to the present day. (16)

**The economy** has often driven demands for rights. The Black Death's labour shortage and the poll tax caused the **Peasants' Revolt**, and serfdom declined because labour was scarce. **Industrialisation** created a wealthy middle class which demanded the vote, leading to **1832**, and a working class whose hardship fuelled **Chartism** and the **trade unions**. The **dockers' strike** (1889) and the **General Strike** (1926) were about wages.

But other factors were as important. **Ideas** of equality and representation inspired many campaigns: Magna Carta's idea of consent, the **Levellers**, **Paine's** Rights of Man and the Chartists' six points. **Government** decided when rights were granted: it repressed Peterloo and the Tolpuddle Martyrs, but passed the 1832, 1867 and 1884 Acts. **War** mattered most in the 20th century: the **First World War** helped bring votes for women in 1918. **Individuals** such as Wilberforce and the Pankhursts organised campaigns.

**Judgement.** The economy was the main factor in **creating** demands for rights, because economic change produced new classes and grievances. But it rarely won rights alone: **ideas** gave campaigns their aims, and rights were extended only when **government** was persuaded or pressured to act. (Level 4.)
