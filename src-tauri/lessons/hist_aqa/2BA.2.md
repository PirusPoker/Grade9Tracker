## What it is

This lesson covers **Part two: Life under the Normans**. The first bullet is **feudalism and government**: roles, rights and responsibilities; landholding and lordship; land distribution and patronage; Anglo-Saxon and Norman government and aristocracies; military service; **justice and the legal system** (ordeals, **murdrum**); inheritance; and the **Domesday Book**. The second is **economic and social change**: towns, villages, buildings, work, food, roles and seasonal life, and **forest law**.

The big idea: for those at the top, 1066 was a **revolution**: an entire aristocracy was replaced and land was reorganised around the king. For most ordinary people, daily life in the fields changed much less: the farming year, food and work continued, but under a **new lord** who often demanded more. Answers that weigh **change against continuity**, and say **for whom** things changed, reach the top levels.

### 1. Anglo-Saxon society and government before 1066

Society: the **king** (advised by the **Witan**); a few great **earls** ruling huge earldoms (Wessex, Mercia, Northumbria) who could challenge the king, as the Godwins did; several thousand **thegns** (local lords owing military service); free peasants (**ceorls**); and a significant number of **slaves**.

Government was advanced for its time: **shires** (each with a **shire reeve** or sheriff) and smaller **hundreds**, each with a court; royal **writs** carrying the king's orders; a land tax, the **geld**; a high-quality silver **coinage**; and the **fyrd** for defence.

### 2. Feudalism under the Normans

After 1066 William declared that **all land belonged to the king**. He kept some as his own royal **demesne** and granted the rest in return for service. This system is usually called **feudalism** (a later word, but a useful one).

| Level | Holds land from | Owes |
|---|---|---|
| **King** | God | Protection and justice to his people |
| **Tenants-in-chief** (barons and bishops, a couple of hundred great landholders) | The king | **Homage** and **fealty** (loyalty sworn on oath), a fixed number of **knights**, advice at court, payments |
| **Knights** (under-tenants) | A tenant-in-chief | Military service, usually for a set number of days a year; guarding castles |
| **Villeins** (unfree peasants) | A lord, as part of a **manor** | **Labour service** on the lord's land (week work), payments in kind, fees; could not leave the manor without permission |
| **Cottars, bordars and slaves** | A lord | The poorest labour; slavery declined after 1066 |

**Roles, rights and responsibilities** worked both ways: a lord owed his men **protection** and **justice**, and they owed him **service** and loyalty. The oath of **homage** was a personal bond.

**Land distribution and patronage**
- Land taken from those who fought at Hastings or rebelled went to William's followers. Rewarding loyalty with land and office is **patronage**: what the king gave he could take away.
- Great estates were usually **scattered** across many shires, so no baron had a compact power base like the old earldoms, except the frontier **marcher earldoms**.
- By **1086** only a tiny share of land was still held by English lords; the Church held a large share, perhaps around a quarter.
- **Knight service** gave William an army without paying wages and spread castles across England.

**The Norman aristocracy** was small, French-speaking, held land on both sides of the Channel and owed its position to the king. The Anglo-Saxon aristocracy was almost **wiped out**: killed at Hastings, dispossessed, or emigrated.

**The Norman system of government** kept Anglo-Saxon foundations but changed who ran them. **Kept**: shires, hundreds and their courts, sheriffs, writs, the geld and the coinage. **Changed**: the old great earldoms disappeared (except on frontiers); sheriffs became **Normans**; writs moved into **Latin**; the **Witan** gave way to the king's **Great Council** of tenants-in-chief; new lords' (**honorial**) courts and separate **Church courts** appeared.

At **Salisbury** in **1086** William took an **oath of loyalty** from major landholders, including under-tenants, binding them directly to the king, not just to their own lords.

### 3. Justice and the legal system

**Much continued.** The Normans kept the **shire and hundred courts**, the **tithing** (groups of about ten men responsible for each other's behaviour) and the **hue and cry** (everyone must chase a criminal when the alarm was raised). The English legal system worked, and keeping it helped William look like Edward's heir.

**Changes and additions**
- **Trial by ordeal** continued (the accused carried hot iron or plunged a hand into boiling water, and the wound was checked for healing after some days; or was thrown into cold water to see if they floated). It was overseen by priests, as a judgement of **God**.
- **Trial by combat** was introduced by the Normans: the accuser and accused (or champions) fought, and God was thought to give victory to the innocent. It was mainly for nobles.
- **Murdrum**: if a Norman was found murdered and the killer was not handed over, the whole **hundred** paid a heavy fine. This protected the outnumbered Normans by making local people police each other.
- **Punishments**: the Anglo-Saxon practice of **wergild** (compensation paid to the victim's family) gave way to more punishments by the king; William was said to prefer **mutilation** to execution.
- **Church courts** were separated from secular courts, so clergy were tried by the Church (see 2BA.3).
- **Lords' courts**: barons held their own courts for their tenants, which gave them extra control.

**Forest law.** The Normans loved hunting. William created large **royal forests** (areas under special forest law, which included villages and farmland, not just woods), most famously the **New Forest** in Hampshire, where villages were cleared.
- Forbidden: hunting deer and boar, carrying bows or dogs, cutting wood, clearing land, without permission.
- Punishments were **harsh**, including blinding or mutilation.
- Forest law was **resented** by the English, who lost access to food, fuel and grazing. William II's promise of fairer forest laws in 1088 shows how unpopular it was. Chroniclers saw his death while hunting in the New Forest (1100) as divine justice.

**Inheritance.** Heirs paid the lord a fee (a **relief**) to inherit, and lords controlled the marriage of heiresses and the wardship of children. The Normans increasingly favoured passing an estate whole to the **eldest son** (**primogeniture**), keeping estates big enough to supply knights; Anglo-Saxon custom more often divided land among heirs.

### 4. The Domesday Book, 1086

**What it was.** At **Christmas 1085**, at **Gloucester**, William ordered a survey of England. Royal commissioners rode out in groups covering regional **circuits**; local juries in each hundred answered questions on oath. The results were written up in Latin in the **Domesday Book** (in two volumes: Great Domesday and Little Domesday, covering East Anglia and Essex in more detail).

**What it recorded.** For each manor: who held it in the time of King Edward ("TRE") and in 1086; its value then and now; the number of ploughs, villeins, cottars and slaves; mills, woodland, meadow and fisheries. The far north, London and Winchester were not fully covered.

**Why it was made**
- **Tax**: to know what the geld could raise, especially as a **Danish invasion** threatened in **1085** and William had brought a large army of mercenaries to England that had to be paid and housed.
- **Who held what**: to settle the many **land disputes** after twenty years of seizures, and to know what each tenant owed the king.
- **Control**: a record of the kingdom's resources and of the **new Norman landholding**, giving it the look of legal, permanent fact.

**Its significance**
- At the time, a display of royal **power**: the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle grumbled that not even an ox was left out, and the English name "Domesday" (the Day of Judgement) suggested there was no appeal against it.
- For historians, the best **evidence** for 1066–86: the replacement of the English aristocracy, Yorkshire "waste", slaves, mills and towns. Its limits: it records land and resources, not people's lives, and some areas are missing.

### 5. Economic and social change

**Villages and work.** Most people (perhaps nine in ten) lived in villages and worked on the land. Many villages had **open fields** divided into strips, worked in rotation; peasants shared ploughs and oxen. Villeins worked several days a week on the lord's **demesne**. **Continuity**: the farming methods, crops and tools barely changed. **Change**: a new Norman lord, often demanding **more labour and rent**; some free peasants were pushed down into villeinage; **slavery declined**, partly because Norman lords found it cheaper to use villeins and the Church disapproved.

**The farming year (seasonal life)**

| Season | Main work |
|---|---|
| Autumn | Ploughing and sowing winter wheat or rye; autumn slaughter of animals that could not be fed, meat salted |
| Winter | Repairing tools and buildings, threshing, hedging; the hungriest time |
| Spring | Ploughing and sowing spring crops (barley, oats, peas, beans); lambing |
| Summer | Haymaking, sheep shearing, then the **harvest**, when all hands, including women and children, were needed |

**Food.** Peasants ate **pottage** (a thick stew of vegetables, grains and peas), dark bread and ale, with little meat. Normans and the rich ate more meat and fish, and wine. Forest law reduced peasants' access to game. A bad harvest meant hunger.

**Roles and buildings.** Men did most ploughing; women brewed, spun, wove, kept animals and joined the harvest; children worked young. Peasant houses were wooden and thatched, often shared with animals in winter. The great change was **stone**: castles, cathedrals and parish churches rebuilt in the Romanesque style.

**Towns.** England already had large towns (London, York, Winchester, Norwich, Lincoln).
- **Castles** were built inside many towns, sometimes demolishing many houses (as at Lincoln and York), and towns were **damaged** in revolts.
- Over time the Normans **encouraged trade**: links with Normandy and France grew; new towns grew beside castles and abbeys; **markets** and fairs were licensed; **Jewish** communities came from Normandy to London and offered credit; cathedrals moved to larger towns (2BA.3), bringing building work and pilgrims.
- In the long run towns gained most from Norman rule; by 1100 many were growing again.

::: terms
- **Feudalism**: land held from a lord in return for service, with the king at the top.
- **Tenant-in-chief**: a baron or bishop who held land directly from the king, owing knights and loyalty.
- **Homage**: the ceremony and oath by which a man became the vassal of a lord and promised loyalty.
- **Villein**: an unfree peasant who worked the lord's land and could not leave the manor without permission.
- **Demesne**: land kept by the king or a lord for his own use, worked by villeins.
- **Patronage**: rewarding loyal followers with land, office and favours, which bound them to the giver.
- **Murdrum fine**: a heavy fine on a whole hundred if a Norman was found murdered and the killer was not produced.
- **Trial by ordeal**: a test by hot iron, hot water or cold water in which God was believed to show guilt or innocence.
- **Trial by combat**: a Norman method in which the two sides fought and God was believed to give victory to the innocent.
- **Forest law**: harsh laws protecting the king's hunting grounds, banning hunting, wood-cutting and clearing.
- **Primogeniture**: passing a whole estate to the eldest son, increasingly favoured by the Normans.
- **Domesday Book**: the 1086 survey of land, resources and landholders ordered by William I at Gloucester.
:::

::: example Q1 Interpretation (8)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a historian writing a textbook on medieval law in 2010. It says: "The Normans did not sweep away English justice. They found a working system and largely kept it. What they added was designed above all to protect a small ruling class living among a hostile people." How convincing is Interpretation A about the legal system under the Normans? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretation A. (8)

Interpretation A is convincing that the Normans **kept** English justice. The **shire and hundred courts**, the **tithing** and the **hue and cry** all continued, and trial by **ordeal** was still used. Keeping them made sense: the system worked, and continuity supported William's claim to be Edward's lawful heir.

It is also convincing that the **additions** protected the Normans. The **murdrum fine** made a whole hundred pay if a Norman was murdered and the killer not found, which forced the English, who greatly outnumbered the Normans, to police themselves. **Trial by combat** suited a warrior aristocracy.

But "above all to protect" is too narrow. **Forest law** was about the king's **hunting** and royal power, not safety, and it caused great resentment. **Church courts** were separated as part of Lanfranc's reforms, and the growth of **lords' courts** and the eldest son's inheritance served **landholding** and control. So the interpretation is mostly convincing, but Norman changes served power and wealth as well as protection. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Explain what was important (8)
Explain what was important about the Domesday Book. (8)

The Domesday Book was important first for **tax and defence**. In 1085 a Danish invasion threatened and William had brought a large mercenary army to England. The survey showed what each manor was worth and what geld it could pay, so William knew the resources he could call on.

Second, it was important for **control of land**. After twenty years of seizures there were many disputes. By recording who held each manor in King Edward's time and in 1086, Domesday turned Norman landholding into a written, legal record, and showed what each **tenant-in-chief** owed the king. Together with the **oath at Salisbury** in 1086, it bound landholders to the crown.

Third, it is important as **evidence**. It shows how few English lords remained, how many slaves there were, and how much of Yorkshire was still "waste" after the Harrying. Its size, and the English name "Domesday", show how the survey was a display of royal **power** that no one could appeal against. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of the ways in which life in villages changed after the Norman Conquest. (8)

After 1066 most villages received a **new Norman lord**, because land was taken from English thegns and granted to William's followers. Because these lords owed the king **knights** and payments, they often demanded **more labour service** and rent from villeins, and some free peasants were pushed down into **villeinage**.

At the same time much stayed the same: the open fields, strips, ploughing and harvest of the **farming year** continued, as did pottage, bread and ale. Over time **slavery declined**, partly because lords found villeins cheaper and the Church opposed the slave trade, which changed the lives of the poorest.

In some areas change was far harsher. The **Harrying of the North** left many Yorkshire villages "waste", and **forest law** cleared villages in places such as the New Forest and banned hunting and wood-gathering, so peasants lost food and fuel. Villages also saw new **stone churches** replace wooden ones. So change came mainly through lordship and law, while daily work continued. (Level 4.)
:::

::: watch
- **Writing about feudalism in the abstract.** Use names and examples: tenants-in-chief, knight service, Domesday, the Salisbury oath.
- **Confusing the legal and feudal systems.** A question on law wants courts, ordeals, murdrum and forest law, not the feudal pyramid.
- **Saying ordinary life was transformed.** For most peasants work, food and the farming year continued; the new lord and his demands were the main change.
- **Thinking trial by ordeal was Norman.** It was Anglo-Saxon and continued; trial by combat was the Norman addition.
- **Treating Domesday as only a tax record.** It was also about landholding, disputes and royal power.
