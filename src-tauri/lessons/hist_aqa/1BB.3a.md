## What it is

This lesson covers the first two bullets of Part three. **The development of tension**: Hitler's aims and Allied reactions; the **Dollfuss affair**; the **Saar**; German **rearmament**, including **conscription**; the **Stresa Front**; the **Anglo-German Naval Agreement**. **The escalation of tension**: the **remilitarisation of the Rhineland**; **Mussolini, the Axis and the Anti-Comintern Pact**; **Anschluss**; the **reasons for and against appeasement**; the **Sudeten crisis and Munich**; and the **ending of appeasement**.

The big idea: Hitler set out to overturn the Treaty of Versailles step by step. Each step was a gamble, and each time Britain and France chose not to stop him, his confidence and his power grew. Britain and France followed a policy of **appeasement**: giving way to reasonable-seeming demands to avoid another war. By the Munich Agreement of September 1938 appeasement had reached its peak; within six months it had collapsed.

### 1. Hitler's aims and Allied reactions

Hitler had set out his ideas in *Mein Kampf* (written in the 1920s). His aims in foreign policy:

| Aim | What it meant |
|---|---|
| **Overturn Versailles** | Rearm, remilitarise the Rhineland, and recover lost land such as the Saar, Danzig and the Polish Corridor |
| **Unite all German speakers** | A "Greater Germany", including Austria (Anschluss) and the Germans of Czechoslovakia and Poland |
| **Lebensraum** ("living space") | Expand into Eastern Europe, especially Poland and the USSR, to gain land and resources for Germans |
| **Destroy communism** | He saw the USSR as Germany's natural enemy |

**Allied reactions in 1933–35**
- **Britain:** many thought Versailles had been **too harsh** and that some German demands were fair. Britain was in economic difficulty, its people feared another war, and its rearmament was only just beginning.
- **France:** deeply alarmed but politically divided; it relied on the defensive **Maginot Line** and on alliances, signing a pact with the **USSR in 1935**.
- **Italy:** Mussolini at first distrusted Hitler, especially over Austria.

**First moves (1933):** Hitler **left the Disarmament Conference and the League of Nations** in October 1933, claiming that Germany was being denied equal treatment. He also signed a **non-aggression pact with Poland (1934)** to reassure his neighbours.

### 2. The development of tension, 1934–35

| Event | What happened | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **The Dollfuss affair (July 1934)** | Austrian Nazis, encouraged by Hitler, murdered the Austrian Chancellor **Engelbert Dollfuss** in a failed attempt to seize power. **Mussolini** moved Italian troops to the Austrian border to protect Austria's independence | Hitler **backed down** and denied involvement. It showed he was not yet strong enough to act against opposition, and that Italy was then an obstacle to Anschluss |
| **The Saar plebiscite (January 1935)** | Under Versailles the Saar had been run by the League for 15 years; now its people voted. **Over 90 per cent** chose to return to Germany | A legal, peaceful triumph; a huge **propaganda** boost that seemed to show Germans wanted to be united |
| **Rearmament and conscription (March 1935)** | Hitler announced that Germany had an **air force** (the Luftwaffe) and reintroduced **conscription**, aiming for an army of about half a million: both clear breaches of Versailles. Rearmament had been going on secretly since 1933 | Britain and France protested but did nothing. Rearmament also reduced German unemployment, making Hitler popular |
| **The Stresa Front (April 1935)** | **Britain, France and Italy** met at Stresa and condemned German rearmament, agreeing to resist further breaches of the treaties and to protect Austrian independence | Appeared to be a united front against Hitler, but it had no firm commitments |
| **The Anglo-German Naval Agreement (June 1935)** | Britain agreed that Germany could build a navy up to **35 per cent** of the size of Britain's surface fleet, and allowed it submarines | Britain **approved a breach of Versailles** without consulting France or Italy. This **broke the Stresa Front** only two months after it was formed |
| **Abyssinia (from October 1935)** | Italy's invasion and the League's sanctions turned Mussolini against Britain and France | Finished off the Stresa Front and pushed Mussolini towards Hitler |

### 3. The escalation of tension, 1936–38

**The remilitarisation of the Rhineland (March 1936)**
- On **7 March 1936** German troops marched into the demilitarised Rhineland. Hitler claimed the new **Franco-Soviet pact** threatened Germany.
- It was a **gamble**: the German army was still weak, and its commanders had orders to withdraw if the French resisted.
- **France** was in the middle of an election campaign and would not act without Britain. **Britain** saw it as Germany moving troops into its own territory and would not risk war. The **League** was preoccupied with Abyssinia.
- **Results:** Hitler's gamble paid off, and his confidence in his own judgement over his generals grew; he began fortifying the western border; France's alliances in Eastern Europe were weakened, because France could no longer easily attack Germany from the west to help them.

**Mussolini, the Axis and the Anti-Comintern Pact**
- **The Spanish Civil War (1936–39):** Hitler and Mussolini both sent forces to help General **Franco's** Nationalists against the left-wing Republic. German planes bombed **Guernica (1937)**. It gave German forces combat practice and drew the dictators together; Britain and France followed **non-intervention**.
- **The Rome-Berlin Axis (1936):** an agreement of friendship and cooperation between Italy and Germany.
- **The Anti-Comintern Pact (1936):** Germany and **Japan** agreed to work together against international communism (the Comintern); **Italy joined in 1937**. The three aggressive powers were now linked, and Italy left the League in 1937.

**Anschluss (March 1938)**
- Austrian Nazis, backed from Berlin, stirred up unrest. In February 1938 Hitler bullied the Austrian Chancellor **Kurt Schuschnigg** into appointing a Nazi, **Arthur Seyss-Inquart**, to his government.
- Schuschnigg called a **plebiscite** on Austrian independence. Hitler, fearing it would go against him, demanded it be cancelled; Schuschnigg resigned, and on **12 March 1938** German troops entered Austria unopposed.
- Hitler then held his own plebiscite in April, in which **over 99 per cent** voted in favour, under heavy Nazi pressure.
- **Mussolini**, now Hitler's ally, did nothing. Britain and France protested but took no action; many British people saw it as Germans uniting with Germans.
- **Results:** a direct breach of Versailles and Saint-Germain; Germany gained Austria's people, army, gold and iron; Czechoslovakia was now surrounded on three sides.

### 4. Appeasement: reasons for and against

**Appeasement** was the policy of avoiding war by giving way to an aggressor's demands, provided they seemed reasonable. It is linked above all with **Neville Chamberlain**, British Prime Minister from 1937.

| Reasons for appeasement (at the time) | Reasons against (critics then and since) |
|---|---|
| Memories of the **First World War**: no one wanted another bloodbath | It encouraged Hitler to make more demands, each larger than the last |
| Many believed **Versailles was unfair**, so some German demands were justified | It broke trust: Czechoslovakia was sacrificed, and the USSR concluded Britain and France could not be relied on |
| Britain was **not ready** for war; it needed time to **rearm**, especially to build fighters and radar | Germany was rearming faster; Britain might have done better to fight earlier, for example over the Rhineland when Germany was weak |
| The **Depression**: Britain could not afford a major war | Hitler's own writings showed his aims went far beyond Versailles |
| **Communism** was seen by some as a bigger danger, with a strong Germany as a barrier against the USSR | It gave away Czechoslovakia's strong army, fortifications and arms factories |
| **France** would not act alone; the **USA** was isolationist; the **Dominions** did not want war | Critics such as **Winston Churchill** warned that Hitler could not be trusted |

### 5. The Sudeten crisis and Munich (1938)

**The Sudetenland** was the border region of **Czechoslovakia** where about **three million German speakers** lived. Czechoslovakia was a democracy with a well-equipped army, strong border defences in the Sudeten mountains, the large **Skoda** arms works, and alliances with France and the USSR.

| Date (1938) | Stage |
|---|---|
| Spring–summer | Hitler encouraged the **Sudeten German Party** under **Konrad Henlein** to demand more rights and stir up trouble; he prepared plans to invade |
| September | Hitler demanded the Sudetenland. Fighting broke out in the region, and war seemed close |
| **15 September** | Chamberlain flew to meet Hitler at **Berchtesgaden**. Hitler demanded the parts of the Sudetenland with a German majority. Britain and France then pressured the Czechs to accept |
| **22 September** | At **Bad Godesberg** Hitler raised his demands: German troops to occupy the whole Sudetenland immediately. Chamberlain refused. Britain mobilised its fleet, gas masks were issued and trenches were dug in London parks |
| **29–30 September: Munich** | At Mussolini's suggestion, **Hitler, Chamberlain, Daladier (France) and Mussolini** met at Munich. **Czechoslovakia and the USSR were not invited.** They agreed that Germany would take the Sudetenland in stages during October, and that the rest of Czechoslovakia would be guaranteed |
| 30 September | Chamberlain and Hitler also signed a declaration that their countries would never go to war again. Chamberlain returned to a hero's welcome, claiming he had secured peace |

**Results of Munich**
- Czechoslovakia lost its border defences, much industry and a large part of its population; Poland and Hungary also took slices of its territory.
- The Czechs felt **betrayed**; **Stalin** concluded that Britain and France would not stand up to Hitler, which pushed him later towards a deal with Germany.
- Britain gained a year to rearm, though Germany also grew stronger.
- **Hitler** became convinced Britain and France would never fight.

**Munich: success or failure?** Supporters said it prevented war and bought time for rearmament; Britain greatly increased its production of fighter aircraft in the following year. Critics said it was a shameful surrender that handed Hitler a strong state without a shot and encouraged him to go further.

### 6. The ending of appeasement

- **Kristallnacht** (November 1938), the violent attacks on Jews across Germany, shocked British opinion and undermined faith in Hitler's good intentions.
- Britain stepped up its **rearmament**.
- In **March 1939** Germany occupied the rest of Czechoslovakia, which was clearly not about uniting Germans. This finally ended appeasement, and Britain and France guaranteed **Poland** (covered in the next lesson).

**How each step led to the next**

| Step | What it taught Hitler or changed |
|---|---|
| Dollfuss (1934) | Italy could block him, so he must win Mussolini over |
| Saar (1935) | Germans outside Germany wanted to join; plebiscites made great propaganda |
| Conscription and Naval Agreement (1935) | Britain would accept, even approve, breaches of Versailles; the Stresa Front was hollow |
| Abyssinia (1935–36) | The League was finished; Italy was moving to his side |
| Rhineland (1936) | Britain and France would not fight even when he was weak |
| Axis and Anti-Comintern Pact (1936–37) | He had allies |
| Anschluss (1938) | Mussolini would no longer protect Austria; Czechoslovakia was exposed |
| Munich (1938) | Britain and France would give way even on a democracy with allies |

::: terms
- **Lebensraum**: "living space"; Hitler's aim to win land in Eastern Europe for the German people.
- **Rearmament**: building up armed forces and weapons; for Germany a breach of Versailles, announced openly in March 1935.
- **Conscription**: compulsory military service, reintroduced in Germany in 1935.
- **Dollfuss affair**: the murder of the Austrian Chancellor by Austrian Nazis in July 1934, after which Mussolini sent troops to the border.
- **Plebiscite**: a vote by the people of an area on a single question, as in the Saar in 1935.
- **Stresa Front**: the 1935 agreement of Britain, France and Italy to oppose German breaches of the peace treaties.
- **Anglo-German Naval Agreement**: the 1935 deal allowing Germany a navy up to 35 per cent of Britain's surface fleet.
- **Remilitarisation**: returning troops to an area that had been demilitarised, as Germany did in the Rhineland in March 1936.
- **Rome-Berlin Axis**: the 1936 agreement of friendship between Mussolini's Italy and Hitler's Germany.
- **Anti-Comintern Pact**: the 1936 agreement between Germany and Japan against international communism, joined by Italy in 1937.
- **Anschluss**: the union of Germany and Austria, carried out by Hitler in March 1938.
- **Appeasement**: the policy of avoiding war by giving way to an aggressor's demands that seemed reasonable.
- **Munich Agreement**: the September 1938 agreement by Germany, Britain, France and Italy to give the Sudetenland to Germany.
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of how Hitler's actions in 1935–36 increased tension in Europe. (8)

In **January 1935** the Saar plebiscite returned the Saar to Germany by a huge majority. **This encouraged** Hitler, and in **March 1935** he openly announced an air force and reintroduced **conscription**, breaking the military terms of Versailles.

**As a result**, Britain, France and Italy formed the **Stresa Front** in April 1935 to resist further breaches. However, in June Britain signed the **Anglo-German Naval Agreement**, approving a German navy of 35 per cent of Britain's. **This meant** Britain had itself accepted a breach of Versailles, and the Stresa Front was undermined after only two months, angering France and Italy.

The front collapsed completely when Italy invaded **Abyssinia** in October 1935. Britain and France's attention was on Mussolini and the League, **which gave Hitler his opportunity**. On **7 March 1936** he sent troops into the **Rhineland**. France, in the middle of an election, would not act without Britain, and Britain would not risk war.

**The consequence** was a major rise in tension: Hitler had broken Versailles by force and got away with it, his trust in his own gambles grew, and France's eastern allies such as Czechoslovakia saw that France would not protect them. In 1936 he also drew closer to Mussolini through the Rome-Berlin Axis. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Two sources (12)
Source B, written for this practice question, is in the style of a British newspaper front page from 1 October 1938, with the headline "Peace!" above a photograph described as showing cheering crowds welcoming Chamberlain home from Munich. Source C, also written for practice, is in the style of a speech by a British MP in the House of Commons on 5 October 1938, arguing that Britain has suffered a total defeat, that Czechoslovakia has been abandoned, and that this is only the beginning. How useful are Sources B and C to an historian studying attitudes to the Munich Agreement? (12)

**Source B** shows the **widespread relief and support** for Munich. Context explains why: after the Bad Godesberg demands Britain had expected war, gas masks had been issued and trenches dug, so the agreement felt like a rescue. Its **provenance** as a newspaper on the day after Chamberlain's return means it captures the immediate mood, though papers also chose headlines to sell copies and many supported the government.

**Source C** shows that **not everyone agreed**. It reflects critics such as Churchill, who argued that Czechoslovakia had been betrayed: excluded from the conference, it lost its border defences and the Skoda works. Its **provenance**, a speech in Parliament during the debate on Munich, means it was meant to persuade MPs, and its prediction was borne out in March 1939 when Hitler took the rest of Czechoslovakia.

**Judgement.** Together they are very useful because they show that attitudes were divided: most of the public welcomed Munich, while a minority in Parliament condemned it. B is useful for popular opinion at the time; C is useful for the arguments against appeasement that later became the dominant view. (Level 4.)

::: example Q4 Essay (16)
"German rearmament was the main cause of increased tension in Europe by 1938." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. (16)

**Rearmament.** Hitler's public announcement of an air force and conscription in March 1935 broke Versailles and alarmed Britain and France into forming the Stresa Front. Rearmament mattered because it made every later action possible: without a growing army Hitler could not have threatened the Rhineland, Austria or Czechoslovakia, and Britain's fear of the Luftwaffe was one reason for appeasement at Munich.

**Hitler's aims and actions.** But rearmament was a means, not an end. Tension rose because Hitler used his new strength to pursue his aims of uniting German speakers and gaining Lebensraum: the Rhineland (1936), Anschluss (1938) and the Sudeten crisis each brought Europe closer to war.

**Appeasement.** Britain's response encouraged him. The Naval Agreement (1935) actually approved rearmament, and the failure to act over the Rhineland convinced him he could take risks. At Munich, Britain and France gave him the Sudetenland.

**The Axis.** Mussolini's switch from blocking Hitler over Dollfuss (1934) to the Axis (1936) and the Anti-Comintern Pact removed a key obstacle and made Anschluss possible.

**Judgement.** Rearmament was a necessary cause but not the main one. Tension rose because Hitler was determined to overturn Versailles and expand, and because appeasement and the collapse of Stresa told him he could. Rearmament provided the tool; Hitler's aims and the Allies' reluctance to stop him explain how it was used. (Level 4.)
