## What it is

This lesson covers **Part one: Medicine stands still** (c1000 to c1500): medieval ideas about disease, **Hippocrates and Galen**, the medieval doctor, **Christianity**, **Islamic medicine and surgery**, medieval surgery, and **public health** in towns and monasteries, including the **Black Death**.

The big idea: medieval medicine was not stupid, but it was **stuck**. Doctors had a complete, logical system inherited from the ancient world, the Church supported it, and nobody was able to test it. So for about 500 years ideas about the causes and treatment of disease barely changed. That **continuity** is the starting point for every later change in this thematic study.

### 1. Ideas about the causes of disease

| Explanation | Belief | Response |
|---|---|---|
| **Religious** | Disease was sent by **God** as punishment for sin or a test of faith | Prayer, fasting, **pilgrimage** to shrines, touching **relics** |
| **Astrological** | The positions of the **planets and stars** affected the body | Consulting star charts; timing bleeding by the zodiac |
| **Royal touch** | The monarch's God-given power could cure **scrofula** ("the King's Evil") | Being touched by the king |
| **Four Humours** | Illness came from an **imbalance** of the body's four fluids | Bleeding, purging, diet, rest |
| **Miasma** | Bad air from rotting matter carried disease | Removing filth, carrying sweet herbs, lighting fires |

People mixed natural and supernatural ideas freely: a physician might bleed a patient, check the stars for the right day, and tell him to pray.

### 2. Hippocrates and Galen

**Hippocrates** (Greek, around 400 BC) taught that disease had **natural causes** and stressed **clinical observation**: watch the patient, record symptoms and compare cases. He is linked to the **Four Humours** (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile): health is balance, so treatment restores balance through rest, diet, bleeding or purging. The **Hippocratic Oath** shaped medical ethics for centuries.

**Galen** (Greek, working in Rome in the 2nd century AD) developed the **Theory of Opposites**: treat an illness with its opposite, so a cold (too much cold, wet phlegm) was treated with something hot such as pepper. He wrote a vast number of books. Human dissection was banned in Rome, so he dissected **animals**, which caused **errors**, for example that the lower jaw had two bones and that blood passed through holes in the septum of the heart. He believed the body was designed by **one creator**, which suited the Church.

**Why their ideas lasted over 1,000 years:** they formed a complete, logical system; the **Church** backed Galen and controlled the universities; training meant **reading** texts, not experimenting; dissection was rare, and differences from Galen were blamed on the body; and no technology could reveal the real causes of disease.

### 3. The medieval doctor

**Physicians** were few, expensive and served mainly the rich. They trained for years at **universities** (Oxford, Cambridge, and famous schools such as Salerno and Montpellier), reading Hippocrates, Galen and later Ibn Sina. They diagnosed by examining **urine** against a **urine chart**, taking the pulse and consulting **astrological charts**; some carried a folding handbook of charts (a **vademecum**). They often aimed to **predict** an illness more than cure it.

Most people never saw a physician. **Barber-surgeons** let blood, pulled teeth and did simple surgery; **apothecaries** mixed remedies; **wise women** and housewives treated families at home with herbal remedies passed down by word of mouth. Treatments included **bleeding** (cutting or leeches), **purging**, herbal remedies (some, like honey on wounds, genuinely helped), charms, prayer and pilgrimage.

### 4. Christianity: help and hindrance

| How the Church **helped** | How the Church **held medicine back** |
|---|---|
| Monks **copied and preserved** medical texts | Promoted Galen as correct; the friar **Roger Bacon**, who urged observation and experiment, was disciplined by his order |
| Monastery **infirmaries** and herb gardens cared for the sick | Taught that disease was **God's punishment**, so prayer was a treatment |
| Founded and ran most **hospitals**, giving free care | Controlled the **universities**, where training was from approved books |
| Christian duty encouraged charity to the sick | **Discouraged dissection**, so anatomy could not improve |

**Medieval hospitals** gave **care rather than cure**: rest, food, warmth and prayer. Many refused infectious or incurable patients; **leper houses** stood outside towns. **St Bartholomew's** in London was founded in the 12th century.

### 5. Islamic medicine and surgery

- In Baghdad the **House of Wisdom** translated Greek medical texts into Arabic, **preserving** them. Islamic teaching encouraged the search for cures.
- **Hospitals** (bimaristans) in Baghdad, Cairo and Damascus had separate wards, aimed to **cure**, and **trained** doctors.
- **Al-Razi** (c900) stressed observation, distinguished **smallpox from measles**, and was willing to **question Galen**.
- **Ibn Sina** (c1000) wrote the **Canon of Medicine**, a huge encyclopaedia that, translated into Latin, was a standard European university textbook for centuries.
- **Ibn al-Nafis** (13th century) described blood passing from the heart **through the lungs**, contradicting Galen, but his work was not known in Europe at the time.
- **Al-Zahrawi** (c1000) wrote a major work on **surgery** and its instruments.

**Importance for Britain:** knowledge arrived through translations made in **Spain and Sicily** and contact during the **Crusades**. It preserved Greek medicine and improved university textbooks, but it also **reinforced** Galen, because the Canon was built around him.

### 6. Surgery

Surgery was a **craft**, below physicians in status, learned by **apprenticeship** or on the **battlefield**. Surgeons faced three problems, none solved before the 19th century:
- **Pain**: **dwale**, a mixture that could include opium and **hemlock**, which could kill.
- **Infection**: some cleaned wounds with **wine** or **honey**, which have antiseptic qualities, without knowing why; many thought pus was a healthy sign.
- **Bleeding**: **cauterising** with a hot iron.

**John of Arderne**, a 14th-century English surgeon who had served with the army, developed an operation for **anal fistula** and wrote about his methods. In **1403** Prince Henry (later **Henry V**) was hit in the face by an arrow at the Battle of Shrewsbury; the surgeon **John Bradmore** designed an instrument to remove it and cleaned the wound with honey and wine, and the prince survived. Skilled surgery existed, but it depended on rare individuals.

### 7. Public health

**Towns** had no sewers and little clean water: waste went into streets and rivers, butchers dumped remains, **cesspits** leaked into wells. Some action was taken: **rakers** cleaned streets, **gongfermers** emptied cesspits, London piped fresh water through **conduits**, councils fined offenders, and in **1388** Parliament passed an act against throwing filth into rivers and ditches. But money, knowledge and enforcement were lacking.

**Monasteries** were much cleaner: piped fresh water, separate **latrines** over running water and rules valuing cleanliness. They were **wealthy**, **organised** and chose sites near water.

**The Black Death (1348–49)** reached England in **1348** and may have killed **between a third and a half** of the population. It was mainly **bubonic plague** (fleas on rats) and **pneumonic plague** (coughing).

| | Beliefs and actions |
|---|---|
| **Causes** | **God's** punishment; an unlucky **alignment of planets**; **miasma**; imbalance of the humours |
| **Treatment** | Prayer, bleeding, purging, lancing buboes, herbal mixtures, sitting near a fire or a sewer to drive out bad air |
| **Prevention** | Prayer and fasting; **flagellants** whipping themselves in repentance; carrying sweet herbs; cleaning streets (the king ordered London's cleaned); fleeing towns |

Nothing worked, because nobody knew about germs or fleas: the Black Death shows medieval medicine's **limits**.

### 8. Factors in Part one

| Factor | Effect c1000–c1500 |
|---|---|
| **Religion and superstition** | Mainly **hindered** ideas (God's punishment, backing Galen) but **helped** care (hospitals, infirmaries, preserving texts) |
| **Government** | Weak: a few rules on filth and water, little money or knowledge |
| **War** | Surgical experience (Arderne, Bradmore); the **Crusades** brought contact with Islamic medicine |
| **Communication** | Hand-copied books spread ideas slowly and preserved old ones; translations brought Islamic knowledge |
| **Science and technology** | Very limited: no microscope, little dissection, no experimental method |
| **Individuals** | Hippocrates and Galen **inhibited** change by becoming unquestionable; Ibn Sina and al-Razi developed medicine |
| **Chance** | The Black Death was beyond anyone's control or understanding |

::: terms
- **Four Humours**: the ancient theory that the body contains blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile, and that illness comes from an imbalance between them.
- **Theory of Opposites**: Galen's idea that an illness should be treated with its opposite, such as a cold with something hot.
- **Clinical observation**: diagnosing by carefully watching and recording a patient's symptoms, as Hippocrates taught.
- **Miasma**: the belief that disease was caused by bad air from rotting matter, dirt or corpses.
- **Physician**: a university-trained doctor who diagnosed using texts, urine, the pulse and astrology.
- **Barber-surgeon**: a craftsman who cut hair and also performed bloodletting, tooth-pulling and simple surgery.
- **Urine chart**: a chart of urine colours used by physicians to diagnose which humour was out of balance.
- **Canon of Medicine**: Ibn Sina's medical encyclopaedia, used as a textbook in European universities for centuries.
- **Bimaristan**: a hospital in the medieval Islamic world, which treated, cured and trained doctors.
- **Dwale**: a medieval anaesthetic mixture that could include opium and the poison hemlock.
- **Black Death**: the plague of 1348–49 that killed perhaps a third to a half of England's population.
:::

::: example Q1 Source utility (8)
Source A, written for this practice question, is a description of an English manuscript illustration from about 1400. A physician in a long gown holds a glass flask of urine up to the light. Beside him, a chart shows twenty circles of different colours, and a book lies open on a desk. A patient lies in bed while a monk kneels in prayer at the bedside. How useful is Source A to an historian studying medieval methods of diagnosis and treatment? Explain your answer using Source A and your contextual knowledge. (8)

Source A is useful because it shows the main diagnostic method of a medieval physician: examining **urine** against a **urine chart**. The colours were matched to the Four Humours, so a dark or cloudy sample would suggest an imbalance which could then be treated by bleeding or purging. The open book is also revealing, because physicians were trained at universities by **reading** Hippocrates, Galen and later Ibn Sina, not by dissection or practical work.

The kneeling monk shows that **natural and supernatural** treatment went side by side. Most people believed disease could be a punishment from God, so prayer was a treatment in its own right, and many patients were cared for by monks or nuns in infirmaries and hospitals.

The **provenance** adds to its value: an illustration from about 1400, made for an educated reader, shows how physicians wanted to be seen, learned and scientific. That is its limit too. Physicians were few and expensive, so this is the medicine of the **rich**; most people were treated at home or by barber-surgeons.

Overall, Source A is very useful for the **ideal** of medieval diagnosis, combining the humours, book learning and religion in one scene, though an historian would need other evidence about how ordinary people were treated. (Level 4, 8/8.)

::: example Q2 Significance of Galen (8)
Explain the significance of Galen. (8)

**At the time**, Galen turned the Four Humours into a full system of treatment through the **Theory of Opposites**, giving doctors a logical method, and his many books became the basis of medical training.

**Over time** his ideas lasted **more than 1,000 years**. The Church approved of his belief in a single creator and controlled the universities, so students learned Galen as unquestionable truth; Ibn Sina's Canon, built around him, reinforced his authority when it reached Europe.

His significance was also **negative**. Because he dissected animals, his anatomy had errors, such as holes in the septum of the heart. Since challenging him was discouraged, these errors survived until **Vesalius** corrected them in the 16th century and **Harvey** overturned his ideas about blood. Galen is significant both for building medicine's foundations and for **holding back** progress. (Level 4, 8/8.)
:::

::: example Q4 Factor essay (16)
Has religion been the main factor in the development of medicine? Explain your answer with reference to religion and other factors. Use a range of examples from across your study of Health and the people: c1000 to the present day. (16)

**Religion in the Middle Ages** was the strongest single influence on medicine, but mostly as a force for **continuity**. The Church backed Galen because his idea of a single creator fitted Christianity, and it controlled the universities, so physicians learned from books, not dissection. Disease was explained as God's punishment, so prayer, pilgrimage and flagellants were responses to the Black Death. Yet religion also **developed** care: monks preserved texts, monasteries had infirmaries and clean water, and the Church founded hospitals. In the Islamic world, too, religious teaching encouraged the search for cures.

**After 1500 religion's influence declined.** Vesalius and Harvey challenged Galen, and religious objections to chloroform faded once Queen Victoria used it. Modern medicine, from germ theory to penicillin, owed little to religion.

**Science and technology** drove the biggest changes: printing spread Vesalius's drawings; better microscopes let Pasteur and Koch identify microbes; X-rays and keyhole surgery transformed diagnosis and operations. It worked with other factors: **war** gave surgeons experience (Paré, Gillies) and pushed governments to fund penicillin's mass production; **government** turned discoveries into public health, from the 1875 Public Health Act to the NHS in 1948; **individuals** such as Jenner, Lister and Fleming made breakthroughs, with **chance** playing a part in Fleming's.

**Judgement.** Religion was the main factor in the medieval period, but chiefly because it **kept medicine the same**. Over the whole period, science and technology was more important in developing medicine, especially when government and war provided the money and urgency to apply it. Religion explains why medicine stood still; science, with government, explains why it moved. (Level 4.)
