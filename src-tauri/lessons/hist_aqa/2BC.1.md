## What it is

This lesson covers **Part one: Elizabeth's court and Parliament**. The first bullet is **Elizabeth I and her court**: her background and character, court life including **patronage**, and her key **ministers**. The second is **the difficulties of a female ruler**: relations with **Parliament**, the problem of **marriage and the succession**, and the **strength of her authority at the end of her reign**, including **Essex's rebellion in 1601**.

The big idea: Elizabeth ruled in a world that expected monarchs to be men and queens to marry. She survived by **controlling her court** through patronage and balance between factions, by choosing able **ministers**, by managing (not obeying) **Parliament**, and by turning her single status into a political image. The late 1590s tested all of this. Top answers judge how **strong** her authority really was, and avoid treating the period as either a golden success or a slow collapse.

### 1. Background and character

- Born in **1533**, daughter of **Henry VIII** and **Anne Boleyn**. Her mother was executed in **1536** and Elizabeth was declared **illegitimate**, though later restored to the line of succession by Henry's will.
- Very well **educated** by humanist tutors (such as Roger Ascham): she spoke and read Latin, French and Italian and studied Greek, history and theology. She was clever, quick and well read.
- Brought up **Protestant**. Under her Catholic half-sister **Mary I** she was suspected of involvement in Wyatt's rebellion (1554) and held in the **Tower of London**, then under house arrest: she learned caution and how dangerous succession politics could be.
- Became queen in **November 1558**, aged 25.

**Character**: intelligent, a skilled speaker and performer, proud of her Tudor blood; **cautious** and often **indecisive** (she delayed decisions on marriage, Mary, Queen of Scots and war); capable of sharp **temper**; vain about her looks and image; frugal with money but generous with favour; very skilled at managing people.

### 2. The court

The **court** was wherever the monarch was: her household, servants, nobles, ministers, ambassadors and those seeking favour. It was the centre of **politics**, **fashion** and **culture**.

- The **Privy Council**: her main advisers, meeting very often, who ran government day to day: finance, defence, law and order, religion, foreign policy. Elizabeth chose its members and did not have to follow its advice.
- The **Privy Chamber**: her personal rooms and servants. Under a queen, these were mostly **women**, which kept the inner circle more separate from politics than under Henry VIII.
- **Progresses**: in summer the court travelled to stay at nobles' and gentry houses. They let her be **seen** by her people, saved her money (hosts paid), and tested and rewarded loyalty. Hosts spent heavily to impress her, as Robert Dudley did at **Kenilworth** in 1575.
- **Image**: portraits, poems, pageants and ceremonies presented her as **Gloriana**, the **Virgin Queen**, married to her kingdom. Courtiers competed to praise her.

**Patronage.** Elizabeth controlled a huge supply of rewards: **titles**, **offices** at court and in government, **lands**, **pensions**, licences and **monopolies** (the sole right to make or sell a product).
- Courtiers sought her favour; in return they gave **loyalty** and service.
- She spread favour between rival men and **factions** (groups at court), so no single faction dominated and all depended on her.
- Patronage was cheap for the Crown: monopolies cost Elizabeth nothing but raised prices for consumers, which caused anger later.
- If patronage dried up or seemed unfair, disappointed men could become dangerous, as **Essex** showed.

### 3. Key ministers and favourites

| Person | Role | Importance |
|---|---|---|
| **William Cecil, Lord Burghley** | Secretary of State from 1558; Lord Treasurer from 1572; created Lord Burghley in 1571; died 1598 | Her chief minister for 40 years: cautious, hard-working, a moderate Protestant; managed finance and Parliament; pressed her to deal with Mary, Queen of Scots |
| **Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester** | Master of the Horse; Privy Councillor; Earl of Leicester from 1564; died 1588 | Her closest favourite and possible husband in the 1560s; a patron of Puritans and of the theatre; led the English army in the Netherlands (1585–87); given **Kenilworth** in 1563 |
| **Sir Francis Walsingham** | Secretary of State from 1573; died 1590 | Strong Protestant; built a **spy network** that uncovered Catholic plots (Throckmorton, Babington); pressed for war with Spain and for Mary's execution |
| **Sir Christopher Hatton** | Courtier, Privy Councillor, Lord Chancellor from 1587; died 1591 | Loyal favourite, a key manager of Parliament |
| **Robert Cecil** | Burghley's son; Secretary of State from 1596 | Dominated government in the last years; rival of Essex; secretly prepared the succession of **James VI** |
| **Robert Devereux, Earl of Essex** | Leicester's stepson; favourite from the late 1580s | Ambitious, popular soldier; his fall led to rebellion in 1601 |

**Factions.** In the early decades Cecil and Leicester often disagreed (for example on how hard to push Protestant policy abroad), but Elizabeth kept both. In the 1590s the rivalry between **Robert Cecil** and **Essex** was much more bitter, partly because the older generation had died.

### 4. Elizabeth and Parliament

**What Parliament was.** The **House of Lords** (nobles and bishops) and the **House of Commons** (elected MPs, mostly gentry and townsmen). It met only when the monarch **summoned** it, mainly to grant **taxes** and pass **laws**, and sessions were short and infrequent.

**The royal prerogative.** Elizabeth insisted some matters were hers alone: **religion**, **foreign policy**, her **marriage** and the **succession**. MPs could discuss what she put before them.

**Freedom of speech.** MPs claimed the right to speak freely; Elizabeth accepted this only within limits.
- **Peter Wentworth**, a Puritan MP, argued strongly for free speech (1576) and was sent to the **Tower**; he was imprisoned again later for raising the succession and died in the Tower in 1597.
- Puritan MPs tried to change the Church through bills (for example on the Prayer Book in the 1580s); Elizabeth blocked them.

**How she managed Parliament.** Privy Councillors sat in both Houses and steered business; the **Speaker** was chosen by the Crown; she could **veto** bills and **dissolve** Parliament; she used flattering, carefully judged **speeches**; she gave way gracefully on points that did not matter. Most sessions passed the taxes she needed.

**Monopolies and the Golden Speech.** By the late 1590s many monopolies covered everyday goods (such as salt and starch) and pushed up prices. In **1597** and **1601** MPs protested strongly. In **1601** Elizabeth promised to cancel the worst monopolies and let the courts judge the rest, then gave her **Golden Speech** (November 1601), thanking MPs and saying her greatest glory was her subjects' love. She kept her authority by **conceding** gracefully.

### 5. Marriage and the succession

**Why marriage was a problem**
- People expected a queen to marry and to produce an **heir**. Without one, a disputed succession could mean **civil war**.
- The heir by blood was the Catholic **Mary, Queen of Scots**, who had a strong claim through Henry VIII's sister Margaret.
- But marriage was risky. A **foreign** husband might drag England into his wars or religion (Mary I's marriage to Philip II was unpopular). An **English** husband would raise one faction above the others. A husband would expect to share power, and childbirth was dangerous.

**Suitors**
- **Philip II** of Spain (soon after 1558), refused.
- **Archduke Charles** of Austria (1560s), a Catholic: talks failed over religion.
- **Robert Dudley**: clearly her favourite, but after his wife **Amy Robsart** died after a fall in **1560**, the scandal made marriage to him impossible.
- **Francis, Duke of Anjou** (late 1570s to early 1580s): the most serious later courtship; it was very unpopular with Protestants, and it ended.

**Pressure from Parliament and Council.** After Elizabeth nearly died of **smallpox** in **1562**, Parliament petitioned her to marry or name an heir (1563, 1566). She was angry and refused to be pressured, though she sometimes made vague promises. By the 1580s it was clear she would not marry.

**Outcome.** She never named an heir openly. In her last years **Robert Cecil** secretly corresponded with **James VI of Scotland** (Mary's Protestant son), who succeeded peacefully in **March 1603**. Her single status, once a danger, had become part of her **image** as the Virgin Queen married to England.

### 6. Authority at the end of the reign: the 1590s and Essex

**Problems of the 1590s**
- **War** with Spain continued, plus a costly rebellion in **Ireland** (the Nine Years' War led by the Earl of **Tyrone**): heavy taxes.
- **Bad harvests** in the mid-1590s, high prices, hunger and unrest; plague.
- **Monopolies** angered MPs and the public.
- The old guard died (Leicester 1588, Walsingham 1590, Hatton 1591, Burghley 1598), leaving a younger, more divided court.
- An ageing queen with no named heir.

**Essex's rebellion, February 1601**
1. **Rise**: Essex was charming, ambitious and popular as a soldier; he led the successful raid on **Cadiz** (1596) and craved military glory and power over patronage.
2. **Clashes**: he quarrelled openly with Elizabeth (on one occasion she struck him) and with Robert Cecil.
3. **Ireland, 1599**: sent with a large army to crush Tyrone, he failed, made an unauthorised **truce**, and returned to England without permission, bursting into the queen's bedchamber at Nonsuch.
4. **Disgrace**: he was put under house arrest and stripped of offices; in 1600 his valuable **monopoly on sweet wines** was **not renewed**, leaving him in debt.
5. **Rebellion**: in **February 1601** he and a few hundred followers marched into the City of London, hoping Londoners would rise against Cecil. They did not. His supporters had paid for a performance of Shakespeare's **Richard II** (a play about deposing a king) the day before.
6. **Outcome**: Essex was arrested, tried and **executed** in February 1601.

**How strong was her authority?**
- **Weakening**: faction, unrest, monopolies and Essex's challenge showed tension; courtiers looked ahead to James.
- **Still strong**: Essex's rising **failed** within a day because Londoners stayed **loyal**; the Privy Council and government held firm; Parliament was won over by the Golden Speech; the succession passed **peacefully** in 1603.

::: terms
- **Court**: the monarch's household, councillors and courtiers, the centre of politics and patronage.
- **Privy Council**: the queen's main group of advisers who ran day-to-day government.
- **Patronage**: the giving of offices, lands, titles and monopolies to win and reward loyalty.
- **Faction**: a group of courtiers who supported each other against rivals at court.
- **Progress**: a summer journey of the court to stay at the houses of nobles and gentry.
- **Monopoly**: the sole right to make or sell a product, granted by the Crown as patronage.
- **Royal prerogative**: matters the monarch decided alone, such as religion, foreign policy, marriage and succession.
- **Freedom of speech**: MPs' claim to speak freely in Parliament, which Elizabeth limited.
- **Succession**: who would inherit the throne after the monarch's death.
- **Golden Speech**: Elizabeth's speech to the Commons in 1601, after her concession on monopolies.
- **Virgin Queen**: the image of Elizabeth as unmarried and devoted to her people.
:::

::: example Q1 Interpretation (8)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a historian writing a biography of Elizabeth in 2003. It says: "Essex's rebellion was the act of a desperate man, not a serious threat. It lasted barely a day, London did not stir, and it ended with Essex on the scaffold. If anything, it showed how firmly the old queen still held her kingdom." How convincing is Interpretation A about Essex's rebellion? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretation A. (8)

Interpretation A is convincing that Essex was **desperate**. After his failure in **Ireland** in 1599, his unauthorised truce with Tyrone and his return without permission, he was put under house arrest and lost his offices. When his **monopoly on sweet wines** was not renewed in 1600, he faced ruin, which helps explain why he gambled on rebellion.

It is also convincing that it was **not a serious threat**. In February 1601 he marched into London with only a few hundred men; Londoners did **not** join him, and he was quickly arrested and executed.

However, it may underestimate the warning signs. Essex had been the queen's leading favourite and a popular hero after **Cadiz**; his supporters staged **Richard II** to suggest a monarch could be removed. His rising grew out of real problems of the 1590s: faction with **Robert Cecil**, an ageing queen, war, bad harvests and monopolies. So the interpretation is convincing about the rebellion itself, but the discontent behind it shows her authority was under more strain than it suggests. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Explain what was important (8)
Explain what was important about patronage at Elizabeth's court. (8)

Patronage was important first because it **bound courtiers to Elizabeth**. She controlled titles, offices, lands, pensions and monopolies; men like **Robert Dudley**, given Kenilworth in 1563 and made Earl of Leicester, owed everything to her, so they had every reason to stay loyal.

Second, it let her **balance factions**. By sharing rewards between rivals such as Cecil and Leicester, she stopped any one group dominating, so all had to compete for her favour. This made her the centre of power without needing a husband to rule through.

Third, patronage could **cause problems**. Monopolies cost her nothing but raised prices, causing protests in Parliament in 1597 and 1601. And when patronage was withdrawn, as when **Essex** lost his sweet wines monopoly in 1600, a disappointed favourite could turn to rebellion. So patronage was her main tool of control, but it needed careful management. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of the ways in which Elizabeth dealt with Parliament. (8)

Elizabeth summoned Parliament mainly for **taxes** and laws, and insisted that religion, foreign policy, marriage and the succession were her **prerogative**. Because MPs were worried after she nearly died of smallpox in 1562, they petitioned her to marry or name an heir in **1563** and **1566**; she rebuked them and refused to be pressured.

Puritan MPs then pushed for Church reform and **free speech**. When **Peter Wentworth** argued in 1576 that MPs should speak freely, he was sent to the Tower, which showed the limits she set. To avoid such clashes she relied on **Privy Councillors** in both Houses and a Crown-chosen Speaker to steer business, and she used her **veto** when needed.

In the late 1590s MPs attacked **monopolies**. Rather than confront them, in **1601** she promised to cancel the worst and gave her **Golden Speech**. This concession kept MPs' loyalty, showing that she managed Parliament by firmness on key matters and flexibility on others. (Level 4.)
:::
