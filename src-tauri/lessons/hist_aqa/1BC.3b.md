## What it is

This lesson covers the last two bullets of Part three. **Czechoslovakia**: **Dubček and the Prague Spring** movement; the **USSR's response** to the reforms; the **effects of the Prague Spring on East-West relations**, including the **Warsaw Pact**; and the **Brezhnev Doctrine**. **The easing of tension**: **sources of tension**, including the **Soviets' record on human rights**; the **reasons for détente and for SALT 1**; and the part played by the key individuals **Brezhnev and Nixon**.

The big idea: in 1968 the USSR showed again, as in Hungary in 1956, that it would use force to keep its satellites communist and loyal. The West protested but did nothing, which showed that both sides now accepted each other's spheres of influence. Combined with the crushing cost of the arms race, the fright of Cuba, the American failure in Vietnam and the split between the USSR and China, that acceptance made possible a period of **détente**: a relaxation of tension that produced the first agreement to limit nuclear weapons, **SALT 1**, in 1972.

### 1. Czechoslovakia before 1968

- Czechoslovakia had been a communist state since the coup of 1948, run on Stalinist lines under **Antonín Novotný**.
- By the mid-1960s its economy was **stagnating**; living standards lagged behind the West, and students and writers were openly critical of censorship and the lack of freedom.
- In **January 1968** Novotný was replaced as party leader by **Alexander Dubček**, a reforming communist who had Brezhnev's initial approval.

### 2. Dubček and the Prague Spring

Dubček wanted **"socialism with a human face"**: a communism that people supported willingly, not one imposed by fear. In **April 1968** the party published an **Action Programme** of reforms.

| Area | Reforms of the Prague Spring |
|---|---|
| **Free speech** | **Censorship relaxed**: newspapers, radio and television could criticise the government; open debate flourished |
| **Politics** | More open discussion inside the party; talk of allowing other political groups to take part; less power for the secret police |
| **Travel** | Greater freedom to **travel abroad**, including to the West |
| **Economy** | Less central planning; more say for workers and managers; some market elements and small private businesses |
| **Regions** | More self-government for Slovakia |

**Crucially**, Dubček insisted that Czechoslovakia would **remain communist** and stay **loyal to the Warsaw Pact**. He believed this would reassure Moscow. The reforms were hugely popular, and the period became known as the **Prague Spring**.

### 3. The USSR's response

**Soviet fears**
- **Spreading reform:** if Czechoslovakia could relax control, people in Poland, East Germany and even the USSR might demand the same. The hardline leaders of **East Germany (Ulbricht)** and **Poland (Gomułka)** pressed Brezhnev to act.
- **Losing control:** free debate could lead to demands to end the Communist Party's monopoly, as in Hungary in 1956.
- **Strategic danger:** Czechoslovakia bordered **West Germany**. Any drift towards the West would open a gap in the Warsaw Pact's front line.
- **Independent communists:** in August 1968 Dubček welcomed visits from **Tito** of Yugoslavia and **Ceaușescu** of Romania, both of whom followed independent lines. Moscow feared a bloc of disobedient states.

**From warnings to invasion**
- Through the summer Brezhnev and other Warsaw Pact leaders **warned** Dubček, held military exercises in Czechoslovakia, and met him in late July and early August. An apparent compromise was reached at **Bratislava (August 1968)**.
- On the night of **20–21 August 1968**, about half a million troops from the **USSR, Poland, Hungary and Bulgaria**, with East German support, **invaded** Czechoslovakia. **Romania refused** to take part.
- The Czechoslovak army was ordered **not to resist**. Citizens resisted **peacefully**: they argued with the soldiers, removed street signs to confuse them, and held protests and strikes. Some people were killed.
- **Dubček** and other leaders were taken to **Moscow** and forced to sign an agreement reversing most reforms. He was removed in **April 1969** and replaced by **Gustáv Husák**, who restored strict control ("normalisation").
- In **January 1969** a student, **Jan Palach**, set himself on fire in Prague in protest and became a symbol of resistance.

**Hungary 1956 and Czechoslovakia 1968 compared**

| | Hungary 1956 | Czechoslovakia 1968 |
|---|---|---|
| Leader | Nagy | Dubček |
| Key demand | Leave the Warsaw Pact; multi-party elections | Stay in the Warsaw Pact; reform communism from within |
| Invaders | Soviet forces | Warsaw Pact forces (USSR, Poland, Hungary, Bulgaria, East German support) |
| Resistance | Armed fighting; thousands killed | Mostly non-violent; far fewer deaths |
| Leader's fate | Executed (1958) | Demoted, later sent to a minor job |
| West's response | Protests, no action | Protests, no action |

### 4. The Brezhnev Doctrine

- **Leonid Brezhnev** had been Soviet leader since 1964.
- After the invasion he set out what became known as the **Brezhnev Doctrine** (autumn 1968): if socialism was threatened in any communist country, the other communist states had the **right and duty to intervene**, by force if necessary, to protect it.
- In effect, **no member of the Soviet bloc would be allowed to abandon communism or leave the Warsaw Pact**. The sovereignty of the satellites was limited.

### 5. Effects of the Prague Spring on East-West relations and the Warsaw Pact

| Effect | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Western condemnation, but no action** | The USA and its allies condemned the invasion, but the USA was bogged down in **Vietnam** and would not risk war in the Soviet sphere. A UN resolution condemning it was vetoed by the USSR |
| **Spheres of influence accepted** | The West's inaction confirmed that it accepted Soviet control of Eastern Europe in practice. Paradoxically, this made it easier to negotiate: each side knew where the lines were |
| **Warsaw Pact held together by force** | The invasion was a joint Warsaw Pact action, showing the alliance was a tool to police its own members. Unity was restored, but by fear |
| **Divisions in the communist world** | **Romania** refused to take part and condemned it; **Yugoslavia** and **China** criticised it; **Albania** formally left the Warsaw Pact; many Western communist parties distanced themselves from Moscow |
| **The Brezhnev Doctrine** | It became the basis of Soviet policy in Eastern Europe until the late 1980s |
| **Delay to talks** | Planned arms-control talks were postponed after the invasion, but they began in 1969 |

### 6. Sources of tension that remained

Even as relations eased, serious tensions continued:
- **Human rights in the USSR:** the Soviet state imprisoned critics (dissidents), sent some to labour camps or psychiatric hospitals, censored writers, and restricted emigration, especially of Soviet Jews who wished to leave. Western governments and campaigners criticised this record, and it became a growing issue in East-West relations in the 1970s.
- **The Berlin Wall**, where people were still being shot trying to escape.
- **Vietnam:** the USA was fighting communist North Vietnam and the Vietcong, which received weapons and supplies from the USSR and China.
- **The arms race continued:** both sides developed anti-ballistic missile (ABM) defences and missiles carrying several warheads.
- **Rivalry in the developing world**, for example in the Middle East.

### 7. Détente: reasons

**Détente** (a French word for the relaxation of tension) describes the improvement in superpower relations in the late 1960s and 1970s.

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **The cost of the arms race** | Both economies were strained. The USSR spent a huge share of its wealth on weapons while its people faced shortages of food and consumer goods; the USA faced rising inflation and debt |
| **Nuclear parity** | By the late 1960s the USSR had roughly caught up with the USA in nuclear strength. Neither could gain an advantage, so agreement made sense |
| **The lessons of Cuba** | The missile crisis had shown how close the world could come to destruction; the hotline and Test Ban Treaty (1963), the Outer Space Treaty (1967) and the Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968) had already shown agreements were possible |
| **Vietnam** | The USA wanted to **withdraw with honour** and hoped the USSR and China would press North Vietnam to negotiate. The war had made Americans weary of confrontation |
| **The Sino-Soviet split** | Relations between the USSR and China had broken down; in **1969** their troops fought on the border. The USSR feared a US-China alliance, especially after **Nixon visited China in February 1972**, so it had good reason to improve relations with the USA |
| **European pressure** | West Germany's chancellor **Willy Brandt** sought better relations with the East (Ostpolitik); agreements over Berlin and the borders of Germany followed in the early 1970s |
| **Economic needs** | The USSR wanted Western **technology**, **trade** and **grain**; the USA wanted new markets |

### 8. SALT 1, 1972

- The **Strategic Arms Limitation Talks** began in **1969** and ended with agreements signed by **Nixon and Brezhnev in Moscow in May 1972**.

| SALT 1 agreements | Detail |
|---|---|
| **ABM Treaty** | Each side was allowed only a **limited number of anti-ballistic missile sites**, so neither could protect itself fully against a nuclear attack, keeping deterrence stable |
| **Interim Agreement** | A **freeze** for five years on the number of **ICBMs** and submarine-launched missile launchers each side had |
| **Basic Principles** | Both promised to avoid confrontation and work for peaceful coexistence |

**Why SALT 1 mattered**
- It was the **first agreement to limit** the superpowers' strategic nuclear weapons, rather than just testing.
- It showed each side **accepted the other as an equal** and that negotiation could work.
- It led to further summits, trade deals and the **Helsinki Agreement (1975)**.

**Its limits**
- It did not cover **bombers** or missiles carrying **multiple warheads**, so the number of warheads continued to grow.
- It **froze** numbers rather than reducing them.
- It did not end rivalry in other areas, such as human rights or the developing world.

### 9. The roles of Brezhnev and Nixon

| | **Brezhnev** | **Nixon** |
|---|---|---|
| Background | Soviet leader from 1964; author of the Brezhnev Doctrine; crushed the Prague Spring | US President from 1969; had a strong anti-communist record, so he could not easily be accused of being soft |
| Motives for détente | Reduce the crippling **cost** of the arms race; gain Western **technology, trade and grain**; win recognition of the USSR as an equal and of post-war borders; avoid a US-China alliance | End the **Vietnam War** with honour; reduce the **cost** of defence; use the China-USSR split ("triangular diplomacy"), working with his adviser **Henry Kissinger** |
| Key actions | Agreed to SALT talks; hosted Nixon in Moscow (1972) and signed SALT 1 | Visited **China** (February 1972) and **Moscow** (May 1972), the first sitting US president to visit Moscow; signed SALT 1 |
| Limits | Kept tight control of Eastern Europe and continued to suppress dissidents | Continued the war in Vietnam for years and bombed Cambodia and North Vietnam |

Both men were pragmatic: they pursued détente because it served their countries' interests, not because they had stopped being rivals.

::: terms
- **Prague Spring**: the period of reform in Czechoslovakia under Dubček from January to August 1968.
- **Socialism with a human face**: Dubček's aim of a communism that allowed free speech and won people's support.
- **Action Programme**: the April 1968 plan of reforms in Czechoslovakia, including less censorship and freedom to travel.
- **Normalisation**: the restoration of strict communist control in Czechoslovakia after the 1968 invasion, under Husák.
- **Brezhnev Doctrine**: the 1968 principle that communist states had the right to intervene in any country where socialism was threatened.
- **Dissident**: a person who openly criticises the government, such as critics imprisoned in the USSR.
- **Human rights**: basic freedoms such as free speech and freedom to travel, which the USSR was criticised for denying.
- **Détente**: the relaxation of tension between the USA and the USSR in the late 1960s and 1970s.
- **Sino-Soviet split**: the breakdown of relations between communist China and the USSR, leading to border fighting in 1969.
- **SALT 1**: the Strategic Arms Limitation agreements signed by Nixon and Brezhnev in May 1972.
- **ABM Treaty**: the part of SALT 1 limiting each side's anti-ballistic missile defences.
- **Nuclear parity**: rough equality in nuclear strength, which the USSR reached by the late 1960s.
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of how tensions between East and West began to ease between 1962 and 1972. (8)

The **Cuban Missile Crisis** of 1962 showed both superpowers how close they had come to nuclear war. **As a result**, in 1963 they set up the **hotline** and signed the **Limited Test Ban Treaty**, the first steps in managing the nuclear danger, followed by the Outer Space Treaty (1967) and the Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968).

**At the same time**, the arms race was becoming ruinously **expensive**. By the late 1960s the USSR had reached rough **parity** with the USA, so neither side could gain an advantage, which made limiting weapons sensible. The Warsaw Pact invasion of **Czechoslovakia in 1968** briefly delayed talks, but the West's inaction **showed** that it accepted the Soviet sphere, which made it easier to negotiate on other issues.

**Other pressures** pushed both sides further. The USA was trapped in **Vietnam** and wanted Soviet help to end the war, while the **Sino-Soviet split**, with border fighting in 1969, made Brezhnev fear an American alliance with China. **This fear was sharpened** when **Nixon visited China in February 1972**.

**This led** Brezhnev to welcome Nixon to **Moscow in May 1972**, where they signed **SALT 1**: the ABM Treaty and a freeze on ICBMs and submarine-launched missiles. **The consequence** was the high point of détente, though human rights and Vietnam remained sources of tension. (Level 4.)

::: example Q2 Two sources (12)
Source B, written for this practice question, is in the style of a statement published by the Soviet news agency on 21 August 1968, announcing that Warsaw Pact forces have entered Czechoslovakia at the request of Czechoslovak party leaders to defend socialism against counter-revolution and Western interference. Source C, also written for practice, is a description of a photograph taken in Prague on 21 August 1968, showing young people climbing onto a Soviet tank, waving the Czechoslovak flag and arguing with the soldiers. How useful are Sources B and C to an historian studying the Soviet response to the Prague Spring? (12)

**Source B** shows how the USSR **justified** the invasion: as help requested by loyal communists to save socialism from counter-revolution. Context shows the real reasons: fear that Dubček's reforms, the end of censorship and freedom to travel would spread to other satellites, pressure from Ulbricht and Gomułka, and Czechoslovakia's border with West Germany. As **official Soviet propaganda**, released on the day of the invasion, it is not reliable on facts, but it is very useful for the official case that became the **Brezhnev Doctrine**.

**Source C** shows **Czech reactions**: non-violent defiance and national pride. Context supports it: the army was ordered not to resist, and citizens confronted soldiers, removed street signs and protested. A photograph taken on the day captures a genuine scene, though the photographer chose a dramatic moment and it cannot show what happened elsewhere.

**Judgement.** Together the sources are very useful because they show both the Soviet justification and the reality on the streets, which contradicts the claim that Czechoslovaks asked for the invasion. B is more useful for Soviet motives as they were presented, C for the response the invasion provoked. (Level 4.)

::: example Q4 Essay (16)
"Détente happened mainly because of the cost of the arms race." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. (16)

**The cost of the arms race.** By the late 1960s both superpowers were spending vast sums on ICBMs, submarine missiles and anti-ballistic missile defences. The Soviet economy could not provide enough food and consumer goods while matching American spending; the USA faced inflation and debt. Since the USSR had reached rough parity, more spending would not bring advantage. SALT 1 (1972), which froze missile numbers and limited ABM sites, directly addressed this cost.

**Vietnam.** But the USA had another motive: Nixon wanted to leave Vietnam with honour and hoped that better relations with Moscow and Beijing would push North Vietnam to negotiate.

**The Sino-Soviet split.** Border fighting between the USSR and China in 1969, and Nixon's visit to China in February 1972, made Brezhnev fear a US-China alliance, giving him a strong reason to agree to SALT within three months.

**The lessons of Cuba.** The fright of 1962 had already produced the hotline and Test Ban Treaty, showing that agreements were possible.

**Brezhnev and Nixon.** Both were pragmatists; Nixon's anti-communist record protected him from accusations of weakness.

**Judgement.** The cost of the arms race was the underlying reason, because it gave both sides a shared interest in limits. But the timing of détente in 1972 is best explained by the Sino-Soviet split and Vietnam, which gave each leader an immediate diplomatic reason to deal. Cost made détente desirable; triangular diplomacy made it happen. (Level 4.)
