## What it is

This lesson covers **Part three: The Norman Church and monasticism**. The first bullet is **the Church**: the Anglo-Saxon Church before 1066; **Archbishop Lanfranc** and the reform of the English Church, including building churches and cathedrals; Church organisation and courts; Church-state relations; **William II and the Church**; the Church's wealth; relations with the **Papacy**; the **Investiture Controversy**. The second is **monasticism**: Norman reforms and new abbeys, monastic life, learning, schools, and the use of **Latin** and the **vernacular** (English).

The big idea: the Normans presented the Conquest as a **crusade to reform** a backward English Church, and the Pope backed them. Reform was real, but it was also a tool of **control**: by 1087 almost every bishop and most abbots were Normans, and great stone cathedrals towered over English towns. Top answers link **religion to power**, and compare William I's cooperation with the Church to William II's exploitation of it.

### 1. The Anglo-Saxon Church before 1066

**Strengths**
- England was thoroughly Christian, with many local **saints**, shrines and pilgrimages.
- Parish churches were multiplying, many built by local thegns.
- There was a strong tradition of writing in **English** (sermons, the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, translations), and fine manuscripts and craftwork.
- The tenth-century reform movement had restored many **Benedictine** monasteries.

**Weaknesses (as reformers saw them)**
- **Stigand**, Archbishop of Canterbury, was a **pluralist**: he held the bishopric of Winchester at the same time. He had been appointed while the previous archbishop, Robert of Jumièges (a Norman driven out by the Godwins), was still alive, and several popes refused to recognise him. He was seen as close to the Godwins.
- **Simony** (buying Church posts), **nepotism** (giving posts to relatives) and **clerical marriage** (many priests had wives) were common, as across much of Europe.
- Some cathedrals were in **small villages** (Dorchester-on-Thames, Selsey, Sherborne).
- Links with **Rome** were weaker than reformers in Europe wanted.

These weaknesses gave William a **religious justification** for invading: the Pope supported him partly because he promised to reform the English Church.

### 2. Lanfranc and reform

**Lanfranc** was an Italian lawyer and scholar who became a monk at Bec in Normandy and then abbot of St Stephen's in Caen. He was William's close adviser. In **1070**, at councils held with papal legates, **Stigand was deposed** and Lanfranc became **Archbishop of Canterbury**. He held the post until his death in **1089**.

| Reform | What Lanfranc did | Purpose / effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Personnel** | English bishops and abbots were replaced by **Normans** when they died or were removed; by William I's death only **Wulfstan of Worcester** remained of the English bishops | A loyal, reform-minded Church; Norman control of a powerful institution |
| **Primacy of Canterbury** | Established that the Archbishop of Canterbury had authority over the Archbishop of **York** (agreed at Winchester, 1072) | One clear chain of command; York, close to Scandinavia and the rebellious north, was kept under control |
| **Councils** | Regular Church councils made rules, e.g. against simony; at Winchester (1076) priests who were already married could keep their wives, but no married men could become priests in future and canons must be celibate | Brought England into line with European reform, but cautiously, to avoid rebellion |
| **Moving sees** | Cathedrals moved from villages to **towns**: Dorchester to **Lincoln**, Selsey to **Chichester**, Sherborne to **Old Sarum** | More people, more wealth, easier control; bishops next to castles |
| **Organisation** | Dioceses divided into **archdeaconries** and **deaneries**, with **archdeacons** supervising priests | Better discipline and supervision of parish clergy |
| **Church courts** | William ordered that Church cases (and clergy) should be heard in separate **Church courts**, not the shire and hundred courts | Church law (canon law) applied to clergy; bishops gained authority; later caused conflict over "criminous clerks" |
| **Monastic rules** | Lanfranc wrote **Constitutions** for monks at Canterbury, used by other monasteries | Stricter, more uniform Benedictine life |

**Building.** After a fire in 1067 Lanfranc rebuilt **Canterbury Cathedral** in the Norman **Romanesque** style. Across England, cathedrals and abbeys were rebuilt on a vast scale in stone (for example **Winchester**, **Durham**, **Lincoln**, **St Albans**), and many parish churches were rebuilt. Features: **round arches**, thick walls and massive round **pillars**, small windows, long naves.

**Why build?** Piety and reform; to **impress** and **intimidate**, showing that Norman power was permanent and backed by God; to replace English saints' shrines with Norman buildings (though many English saints' cults were kept, and some new Norman abbots who despised English saints learned to respect them).

**Resistance and limits.** Most English clergy could not resist, but there were tensions: at **Glastonbury** (1083) the Norman abbot Thurstan tried to force new chants on the monks, and when they resisted his soldiers attacked them in the church, killing some. Reform was **gradual**: married parish priests remained common for a long time.

### 3. Church and state, and the Papacy

**William I and the Church** had a close but firm relationship:
- William **chose** bishops and abbots, gave them their lands and received their **homage** (they were tenants-in-chief owing knights).
- He set rules: no pope was to be recognised in England, and no papal letters received, without his **permission**; no bishop could **excommunicate** a royal official or tenant-in-chief without his consent; Church councils' decisions needed his approval.
- When **Pope Gregory VII** demanded that William swear **fealty** (loyalty) to the Pope for England (around 1080), William **refused**, though he agreed to keep paying Peter's Pence (a traditional payment to Rome).
- **Lanfranc** worked closely with the king and even acted as regent, crushing the Revolt of the Earls in 1075.

**The Investiture Controversy.** Across Europe, reforming popes (especially **Gregory VII**) argued that kings and lords should not **invest** bishops with the symbols of office (the ring and staff), because spiritual power came from the Church. This led to a bitter conflict between Gregory and the German emperor **Henry IV** (who famously did penance at Canossa in 1077). In England, William I and Lanfranc **avoided** an open clash: William still invested his bishops, but he supported reform, so the Pope chose not to fight him. The quarrel reached England later, under William II and Anselm, and was settled under Henry I.

### 4. William II and the Church

**William Rufus** treated the Church mainly as a source of **money**.
- **Vacant sees**: when a bishop or abbot died, Rufus kept the post **empty** and took its revenues. After Lanfranc died in **1089**, Canterbury stayed vacant for about four years.
- **Ranulf Flambard**, his chief minister and tax-gatherer, was rewarded with the bishopric of **Durham** (1099). Monks hated Flambard's methods.
- **Anselm**, a respected scholar and abbot of Bec, was made Archbishop of Canterbury in **1093** when Rufus fell seriously ill and feared death. After he recovered they quarrelled: over which of two rival popes to recognise, over Anselm's **pallium** (the symbol of an archbishop's authority) and his wish to go to Rome, and over money and knights. Anselm went into **exile** in 1097.
- Rufus's reputation suffered because the chroniclers were **monks**: they described him as greedy, irreligious and immoral, so their accounts are hostile.

### 5. The wealth of the Church

The Church was very **rich**: it held a large share of the land of England (perhaps around a quarter by 1086), received **tithes** (a tenth of produce) from parishes, and earned income from pilgrims, gifts and its own estates. Bishops and abbots were great lords with knights and courts. This wealth explains both why kings wanted to **control** the Church and why William II's seizure of revenues caused such anger.

### 6. Monasticism

**The Rule of St Benedict** guided monks' lives: **poverty**, **chastity** and **obedience**, a fixed daily pattern of prayer, work and study, under an **abbot**.

**A monk's day**
- Up to eight church **services** a day and night (the "hours"), from Matins in the early hours to Compline at bedtime.
- **Work**: copying manuscripts, teaching, gardening, running the estates, caring for the sick and poor.
- **Reading and study**, and the **chapter** meeting each day to hear a chapter of the Rule and discuss business.
- Meals in silence in the refectory while one monk read aloud; sleep in a shared dormitory.
- Some children (**oblates**) were given to monasteries by their parents and grew up as monks.

**Norman reforms**
- English abbots were replaced by **Normans**, who brought monks from Normandy and enforced stricter discipline (Lanfranc's Constitutions).
- **New foundations**: **Battle Abbey** (founded by William on the battlefield at Hastings), **Selby** (Yorkshire), and abbeys and priories founded by barons. The **Cluniac** order (from Cluny in Burgundy) arrived, for example at **Lewes**. The number of monks and religious houses grew substantially by 1100.
- Great **abbey churches** were rebuilt in stone (St Albans, Bury St Edmunds, Winchester).
- Monasteries in the north were refounded, which helped the Church recover the region after the Harrying.
- Barons founded houses to **save their souls** (monks prayed for founders) and to show wealth and piety, often giving them land in England to "parent" abbeys in Normandy.

**Learning, schools and education**
- Monasteries and cathedrals ran **schools**, mainly to train boys for the Church. Teaching was in **Latin**.
- **Scriptoria**: monks copied books, and the Normans brought new texts from the Continent and built up libraries. Canterbury and Durham became important centres.
- Monks wrote **histories**, which are major sources for the period (for example the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, and later monk historians such as Orderic Vitalis).

**Latin and the vernacular**
- Before 1066 England was unusual in using its own language, **English**, for writs, laws, sermons and chronicles.
- After 1066 **Latin** became the language of government and Church documents (writs moved into Latin by the 1070s; Domesday was in Latin). The Norman elite spoke **French**.
- **English** remained the language of ordinary people, and one version of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle continued to be written in English at **Peterborough** well into the twelfth century. So the vernacular survived, but lost its official status.

::: terms
- **Pluralism**: holding more than one Church post at once, as Stigand held Canterbury and Winchester.
- **Simony**: buying or selling Church posts, condemned by reformers.
- **Nepotism**: giving Church posts to relatives.
- **See**: the area governed by a bishop, centred on his cathedral.
- **Romanesque**: the Norman style of architecture with round arches, thick walls and massive pillars.
- **Archdeacon**: a Church official who supervised the clergy of part of a diocese for the bishop.
- **Church courts**: separate courts introduced by William for Church cases and the clergy, using canon law.
- **Investiture**: giving a bishop the ring and staff of office; reforming popes said kings must not do this.
- **Vacancy**: a period when a bishopric or abbey had no head, during which William II took its income.
- **Rule of St Benedict**: the guide to monastic life based on prayer, work, study, poverty, chastity and obedience.
- **Vernacular**: the everyday spoken language of a people, here English, as opposed to Latin.
:::

**Paper 2 Section B.** Q1 interpretation (8) · Q2 explain (8) · Q3 account (8) · Q4 site essay (16).

1. **Q1 (8).** Interpretations often claim Lanfranc was a reformer or that reform was really about control. Test the claim with precise reforms (councils, moving sees, Church courts, primacy) and personnel changes (only Wulfstan left). Judge how far the claim is convincing.
2. **Q2 (8).** "What was important about Lanfranc's reforms?" Explain consequences for the Church (discipline, organisation), for Norman control (personnel, cathedrals in towns) and for Church-state relations.
3. **Q3 (8).** "Write an account of the ways in which the English Church was affected by the Normans." Move in order from Stigand to Lanfranc to William II, linking each change to what caused it.
4. **Q4 (16).** Use the White Tower's chapel of St John and the involvement of Bishop Gundulf to link the Church to Norman power; weigh religion against control.
:::

::: example Q1 Interpretation (8)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a historian writing a study of the medieval Church in 2011. It says: "Lanfranc was a genuine reformer. He found an English Church that was corrupt and backward and, with the king's support, brought it into line with the best practice of Europe." How convincing is Interpretation A about Lanfranc's reforms? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretation A. (8)

The interpretation is convincing that Lanfranc was a **genuine reformer**. He held regular **councils** that condemned simony and limited clerical marriage; he set up **archdeacons** to supervise priests, moved **cathedrals** from villages such as Selsey and Sherborne to towns, and, with William, created separate **Church courts**. His **Constitutions** made monastic life stricter. These matched reforms elsewhere in Europe.

It is also right about the **king's support**. William and Lanfranc worked closely together, and Lanfranc even crushed the Revolt of the Earls in 1075.

But calling the English Church simply "corrupt and backward" repeats the **Norman case**. Stigand was a pluralist, but the English Church had many saints, strong monasteries and a rich tradition of writing in English. And reform served **control**: almost all bishops and abbots became Normans, only **Wulfstan** remaining, and Canterbury's primacy over York kept the north in check. So the interpretation is convincing about Lanfranc's aims and methods but one-sided about why reform was needed. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Explain what was important (8)
Explain what was important about the Norman building of cathedrals and churches. (8)

It was important first as **reform**: Lanfranc rebuilt **Canterbury** after the fire of 1067, and moving sees to **towns** such as Lincoln and Chichester put new cathedrals where people and wealth were. This supported a better organised Church.

Second, it was important for **control**. Huge Romanesque buildings with thick walls and massive pillars, often beside a **castle**, as at Lincoln and Durham, showed the English that Norman rule was permanent and backed by God. Building them required **English labour** and money.

Third, it changed **religious life and culture**. Parish churches were rebuilt in stone, abbeys grew, and some English saints' shrines were rehoused in Norman buildings, which helped Normans win acceptance by keeping popular cults. In the long run these buildings created the cathedral landscape of England. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of the ways in which monasteries changed after the Norman Conquest. (8)

After 1066 English abbots were gradually **replaced by Normans**. Because these abbots wanted stricter discipline, they enforced the **Rule of St Benedict** more closely, using Lanfranc's **Constitutions**, and brought monks from Normandy. This sometimes caused conflict, as at **Glastonbury** in 1083, when the abbot's soldiers attacked monks who resisted new chants.

At the same time, Norman kings and barons **founded new houses**, such as **Battle Abbey** on the site of Hastings and **Selby** in Yorkshire, and the **Cluniac** order arrived at Lewes. Because founders wanted monks to pray for their souls and to show their wealth, they gave land, so the number of monks grew. Great abbey churches were rebuilt in stone.

These changes affected **learning**: libraries grew as new texts came from the Continent, schools taught in **Latin**, and monk historians recorded the period, though English survived in the Peterborough Chronicle. (Level 4.)
:::

::: watch
- **Describing reform without purpose.** Always link reforms to Norman control or to European Church reform.
- **Saying all English churches were destroyed.** Many were rebuilt, and English saints' cults were often kept.
- **Muddling Lanfranc and Anselm.** Lanfranc: Archbishop 1070–89, worked with William I. Anselm: from 1093, quarrelled with William II.
- **Forgetting the Investiture Controversy.** Explain what investiture was and why William I avoided a clash.
- **Ignoring language.** Latin replaced English in official documents, but English survived in speech and in the Peterborough Chronicle.
:::
