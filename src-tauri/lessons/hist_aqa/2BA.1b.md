## What it is

This lesson covers the third bullet of **Part one: The Normans: conquest and control**: **establishing and maintaining control**. It covers the **revolts of 1067–1075**, the **Harrying of the North**, **King William's leadership and government**, and **William II and his inheritance**.

The big idea: winning at Hastings did not make William master of England. For about ten years he faced rebellions in the south-west, the Midlands, the north and East Anglia, some backed by the Danes and the Scots. He held on by combining **force** (castles, the Harrying, new Norman landholders) with **continuity** (keeping the Anglo-Saxon system of government). High-level answers explain how these worked **together**, and how each revolt changed his policy.

### 1. From Hastings to the coronation

- After Hastings William secured **Dover** and Canterbury, then circled **London**, burning land to cut it off rather than attacking across the Thames.
- The English leaders had chosen **Edgar Aethling** as king, but support melted away. At **Berkhamsted** Edgar, Archbishop **Ealdred** of York, and the earls **Edwin and Morcar** **submitted** to William.
- William was crowned in **Westminster Abbey** on **Christmas Day 1066**. When the English shouted their approval, Norman guards outside feared a riot and set fire to nearby houses, a sign of the tension to come.
- Early on William was fairly **conciliatory**: English earls kept their lands if they submitted, and he let some lords buy back their estates. Those who had fought at Hastings lost land.

### 2. The revolts, 1067–1075

In 1067 William returned to Normandy, leaving his half-brother **Bishop Odo** and **William FitzOsbern** in charge. Their harsh rule and the building of castles caused resentment.

Ralph fled; Roger was imprisoned; **Waltheof was executed** in 1076 |

**Why did the revolts fail?**
- They were **not coordinated**: different regions rose at different times for local reasons.
- **Leaders** were divided or weak; Edwin and Morcar gave up quickly; Edgar had no base.
- **Foreign help was unreliable**: the Danes were more interested in plunder and accepted payment to leave.
- **Castles** let small Norman garrisons hold out until William arrived.
- William's **speed and ruthlessness**, and his willingness to use **English** troops (the fyrd helped at Exeter).

**How the revolts changed things.** Before 1069 William tried to rule with English earls. After the northern risings he relied on **Normans**: English landholders were replaced, castles multiplied, and the last English earl, Waltheof, was executed after 1075. By 1075 the revolt of the earls showed that the main danger now came from **Normans**, not the English.

### 3. The Harrying of the North, 1069–70

**What happened.** Over the winter of 1069–70 William's army moved through Yorkshire and the north, **destroying** villages, crops, ploughs and livestock, so that the land could not support rebels or a Danish army. Rebels were killed, and people who survived faced **famine**.

**Why William did it**
- The north had rebelled **repeatedly** and had killed a Norman earl and his men.
- The **Danes** were still in the area; destroying food and shelter meant no future invasion could be supplied there.
- York's castles had been destroyed; William was **furious** and wanted to make an example.
- The north was far from London, had close links with Scandinavia and Scotland, and had never been easy for southern kings to rule.

**Consequences**
- **Death and famine**: the twelfth-century monk **Orderic Vitalis** claimed over 100,000 died. Historians treat that figure with caution, but the scale was clearly huge, and some refugees fled as far as the Midlands or sold themselves into slavery.
- **Long-term damage**: the **Domesday Book** (1086) records large numbers of Yorkshire villages as **"waste"** and land values far below 1066 levels.
- **The north never seriously rebelled against William again**; the Danes found no base.
- **Reputation**: even Norman writers were uneasy. Orderic said William regretted it on his deathbed. The Pope and Church questioned such violence.

**Interpretations.** Some historians see a deliberate policy of terror that worked; others note that the "waste" in Domesday may also reflect later raids or reorganised estates. A good answer judges the Harrying both as a **solution** (it ended northern revolt) and as a **brutal choice** with long-lasting consequences.

### 4. William's leadership and government

**Force and control**
- **Castles**: hundreds were built in the decades after 1066, many in towns (York, Lincoln, Exeter, London). Rebuilding in stone, as in the White Tower, showed permanence.
- **Land as reward**: rebels' lands were given to Norman **tenants-in-chief**, who held them in return for **knights** and loyalty. By 1086 very little land remained with English lords.
- **Marcher earldoms**: compact earldoms on the Welsh border (Chester, Shrewsbury, Hereford) gave trusted Normans power to defend frontiers.
- **Personal ruthlessness**: the Harrying, the execution of Waltheof, punishment of rebels.

**Continuity and the Anglo-Saxon system**
- William claimed to be Edward's **lawful heir**, so he kept much of Edward's government to look legitimate.
- **Shires** and **sheriffs** (now mostly Normans), shire and hundred courts, **writs** (written orders, increasingly in Latin), the silver **coinage**, and the land tax (the **geld**) all continued.
- He worked with English churchmen at first, though by the 1070s most bishops were Normans.

**Leadership qualities.** Energetic, decisive, ruthless; he moved between England and Normandy, often leaving **regents** (Odo, FitzOsbern, later Lanfranc). He survived challenges in Normandy too, including the rebellion of his eldest son **Robert Curthose** in the late 1070s. He ordered the **Domesday survey** (1085–86) and took an oath of loyalty from landholders at **Salisbury** (1086), both of which strengthened royal control (see 2BA.2).

### 5. William II and his inheritance

- William I died in **September 1087** at Rouen, after an injury on campaign. He left **Normandy** to his eldest son **Robert Curthose**, **England** to his second son **William Rufus** (William II), and money to his youngest, **Henry**.
- **The problem**: many great barons held land on **both sides of the Channel**, so they owed loyalty to two rulers who might quarrel. Some preferred the easy-going Robert as ruler of both.
- **The rebellion of 1088**: led by **Bishop Odo** (freed from prison on William I's death) with other powerful barons, supporting Robert. Rufus appealed to the **English**, promising good government, lower taxes and fairer forest laws, and English troops helped him besiege rebel castles such as **Rochester**. Robert never crossed to England. Odo was exiled.
- **Rufus's rule**: he campaigned in **Normandy** in the 1090s; in **1096** Robert, wanting to go on the **First Crusade**, pledged Normandy to Rufus for a large sum, so Rufus ruled both. He secured the **northern border** (taking **Carlisle** in 1092; the Scots king Malcolm III was killed invading in 1093). He completed the **White Tower** and built **Westminster Hall**.
- **His reputation**: monks writing about him complained of his greed, heavy taxes, his chief minister **Ranulf Flambard**, and his exploitation of the Church (see 2BA.3). He was killed by an arrow while hunting in the **New Forest** in **August 1100**; Henry quickly seized the crown.

::: terms
- **Submission**: formally accepting someone as your lord or king, as the English leaders did at Berkhamsted in 1066.
- **Regent**: a person who governs on the king's behalf while he is away, such as Odo in 1067.
- **Harrying of the North**: William's destruction of crops, livestock and villages in the north in the winter of 1069–70.
- **Waste**: the Domesday Book's term for land producing no value, common in Yorkshire after the Harrying.
- **Tenant-in-chief**: a lord who held land directly from the king in return for loyalty and knights.
- **Marcher earldom**: a compact earldom on the Welsh border given to a trusted Norman to defend the frontier.
- **Writ**: a short written order from the king, sealed and sent to a sheriff or shire court.
- **Sheriff**: the king's officer in a shire, collecting taxes and keeping order; kept by the Normans.
- **Geld**: the Anglo-Saxon land tax, which the Normans kept collecting.
- **Inheritance**: the passing of lands and titles on death; William I split his between Robert (Normandy) and William Rufus (England).
:::

::: example Q1 Interpretation (8)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a historian writing a book on the Normans in 2012. It says: "William held England through fear. His castles towered over every town, and the burnt fields of the north showed what awaited anyone who resisted. The English obeyed because they had no other choice." How convincing is Interpretation A about how William established control of England? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretation A. (8)

Interpretation A is convincing about the role of **fear**. William built **castles** in towns such as York, Lincoln and Exeter, often demolishing houses to make space, and rebuilt key ones in stone. The **Harrying of the North** in 1069–70 destroyed crops and villages so thoroughly that Domesday recorded many Yorkshire villages as "waste" in 1086, and the north did not rebel again. The execution of **Waltheof** in 1076 also showed the price of rebellion.

But it is less convincing in saying fear was the only reason. William kept the Anglo-Saxon **sheriffs**, **shire courts**, **writs** and **coinage**, so government felt familiar and he looked like Edward's lawful heir. Many English **accepted** Norman rule: English troops helped at Exeter in 1068, and in 1088 the English fought for William II against Norman rebels.

So the interpretation is convincing about the importance of force, but too one-sided: control rested on force **combined** with continuity and English cooperation. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Explain what was important (8)
Explain what was important about the Harrying of the North. (8)

The Harrying was important first because it **ended rebellion in the north**. The north had risen repeatedly in 1068–69, killing Earl Robert de Comines and destroying York's castles with Danish help. By burning crops, ploughs and villages, William made sure no rebel army or Danish fleet could be fed there again, and the north did not seriously rebel again in his reign.

Second, it had a **terrible human cost**. Survivors faced famine; Orderic Vitalis, writing later, claimed over 100,000 died. Even if this is exaggerated, the **Domesday Book** shows the long-term damage: in 1086 many Yorkshire villages were still "waste" and land values were far lower than in 1066.

Third, it was important for William's **methods and reputation**. It marked the end of his attempt to rule with English earls: after 1069–70 power passed decisively to Normans. Yet it also troubled contemporaries, including Norman writers, so it shows both the success and the brutality of the conquest. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of the ways in which William dealt with rebellion between 1068 and 1071. (8)

In **1068** Exeter refused to accept William's demands. He besieged it until it surrendered, then **built a castle** there; because he spared the city, it was a sign he still hoped to win English loyalty. The same year Edwin and Morcar rebelled, so William marched north building castles at **Warwick**, **Nottingham** and **York**; faced with these, the earls submitted.

In **1069** northern rebels killed Robert de Comines and, with a **Danish** fleet and Edgar Aethling, captured York. This led William to change his approach: he **paid the Danes** to leave and launched the **Harrying of the North**, destroying the food and shelter rebels needed.

Because the Danes returned in **1070** and joined **Hereward** at Ely, William again bought them off, then attacked Ely by building a **causeway** through the fens. Its surrender in 1071, with Morcar imprisoned, ended the major English revolts. Each rising made William rely more on force and on Norman landholders. (Level 4: a linked, coherent account.)
