## What it is

This lesson covers the first two bullets of **Part four: Modern medicine** (c1900 to the present): **modern treatment of disease** (the pharmaceutical industry, **penicillin**, new diseases and treatments, **antibiotic resistance**, alternative treatments) and **the impact of war and technology on surgery** (plastic surgery, blood transfusions, X-rays, transplants, and modern methods such as **keyhole surgery**, lasers and radiation therapy).

The big idea: in the 20th century, medicine could finally **cure** many diseases, not just prevent or describe them. Change was fast because **science, government, industry and war** combined. Penicillin is the classic example: found by **chance**, developed by **scientists**, mass-produced with **government money** because of **war**.

### 1. Penicillin: discovery and development

| Stage | Who | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| **Discovery** (1928) | **Alexander Fleming**, St Mary's Hospital, London | Having seen soldiers die of infected wounds in the First World War, he studied staphylococcus bacteria. Returning from holiday, he noticed **mould** on a culture plate had killed the bacteria around it. He identified it as **penicillium** and published in 1929 |
| **Stalled** (1929–38) | Fleming | He could not produce it in quantity or purify it, did not test it on an infected animal, and had no funding to go further. He moved on to other work |
| **Development** (from 1939) | **Howard Florey** and **Ernst Chain**, Oxford | Chain read Fleming's paper; the team purified penicillin and in **1940** showed it cured infected **mice**. In 1941 a policeman, **Albert Alexander**, improved dramatically, but the supply ran out and he died |
| **Mass production** (1941–44) | **US and British governments** and drug companies | British factories were busy with war work, so Florey went to the **USA**. After the USA entered the war (December 1941) its government funded companies to mass-produce it. By **D-Day (1944)** there was enough to treat Allied wounded |

Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the **Nobel Prize** in 1945. Penicillin was the first **antibiotic**: a drug from a living organism that kills bacteria. After the war it became available to civilians, and many more antibiotics followed (for example streptomycin, used against TB).

**Factors:** **chance** (the mould); **individuals** (Fleming's observation, Florey and Chain's persistence); **science and technology** (purification, freeze-drying, deep-tank fermentation); **war** (the urgent need for a drug for infected wounds); **government** (US and British funding); **communication** (Chain found Fleming's published paper).

### 2. The pharmaceutical industry

- From the early 20th century, large drug companies (in Britain, the USA, Germany and Switzerland) grew into a huge **pharmaceutical industry**, with teams of scientists and costly laboratories.
- They mass-produce drugs, **research** new ones, and run long **clinical trials**. The **thalidomide** disaster of the early 1960s, when a drug given to pregnant women caused serious birth defects, led to much **stricter testing** and regulation.
- Major drug developments include **insulin** for diabetes (1920s), **Prontosil** and sulphonamide drugs (1930s), antibiotics, drugs for heart disease and blood pressure, and chemotherapy for cancer.
- Criticisms: new drugs are **very expensive**; companies focus on profitable medicines; developing a drug takes many years.

### 3. New diseases, resistance and alternatives

- **HIV/AIDS** was identified in the early **1980s**. At first there was no treatment and it caused fear and prejudice. **Antiretroviral** drugs (from the mid-1990s) now let people with HIV live long lives, though there is **no cure**. Government campaigns spread information about prevention.
- **Antibiotic resistance**: bacteria evolve so antibiotics no longer kill them. Overuse (prescribing for viral infections, not finishing courses, use in farming) speeds this up. **MRSA**, a resistant staphylococcus, became a feared **hospital "superbug"**. Fleming himself warned that misuse could make bacteria resistant. Few new classes of antibiotic have been found in recent decades.
- **Alternative treatments** such as **homeopathy**, **acupuncture**, **herbal remedies** and chiropractic grew popular from the late 20th century. Some people want more natural or personal care, or have conditions modern medicine cannot cure. Most have little scientific evidence for their effects, and whether the NHS should pay for them is debated.

### 4. War and technology in surgery

At the start of the 20th century, aseptic surgery and anaesthetics had solved infection and pain; **bleeding** was still the big problem.

| Development | Key details |
|---|---|
| **X-rays** | Discovered by **Wilhelm Röntgen** in **1895**. In the First World War, **mobile X-ray units** near the front located bullets and shrapnel; **Marie Curie** organised mobile units in France. X-rays later became routine in diagnosis |
| **Blood transfusions** | **Karl Landsteiner** identified **blood groups** in **1901**, so donors could be matched. In the **First World War**, adding **sodium citrate** stopped blood clotting, so it could be **stored**; blood depots were set up near the front. In the Second World War a national donation system was organised, which became the **National Blood Transfusion Service** in **1946** |
| **Plastic surgery** | **Harold Gillies**, a New Zealand-born army surgeon, treated soldiers with terrible **facial injuries** from shrapnel. He set up a specialist hospital at **Sidcup** and developed skin grafts, including the **pedicle tube**, keeping grafted skin attached to a blood supply. He photographed and recorded his cases |
| **Burns surgery** (Second World War) | **Archibald McIndoe**, Gillies's cousin, treated burned **RAF pilots** at **East Grinstead**. He used saline baths and new grafting techniques, and stressed **psychological** recovery; his patients formed the **Guinea Pig Club** |

**Why war mattered:** huge numbers of new kinds of wounds; urgency and government money; surgeons gained years of experience in months; ideas were shared quickly. But much of the science (blood groups, X-rays) came **before** the war; war **accelerated** its use.

### 5. Modern surgical methods

| Method | Details |
|---|---|
| **Transplant surgery** | The first successful kidney transplant was in the USA in 1954; the **first UK kidney transplant** was in **1960**. **Christiaan Barnard** performed the first human **heart transplant** in South Africa in **1967**; the patient lived only a few weeks. Rejection was the main problem until better **anti-rejection drugs** in the 1980s |
| **Keyhole surgery** | Operations through tiny cuts using a camera (endoscope) and long instruments, widely used from the **1980s**: less pain, smaller scars, faster recovery |
| **Lasers** | Precise cutting and sealing, for example in **eye surgery** |
| **Radiation therapy** | Radiation used to destroy **cancer** cells, developed through the 20th century from the discovery of radium and X-rays |
| **Scanners and robots** | CT and MRI scanners show the inside of the body in detail; robotic and microsurgery allow very precise work |

These need advanced **technology**, very skilled teams and a lot of **money**, which in Britain largely comes through the **NHS**.

### 6. Factors in modern medicine and surgery

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **War** | Urgency for penicillin's mass production; plastic surgery (Gillies, McIndoe); blood storage; mobile X-rays |
| **Science and technology** | Purification and fermentation; blood groups; X-rays; anti-rejection drugs; cameras, lasers, scanners |
| **Government** | Funded penicillin production; NHS pays for modern surgery and drugs; regulates drug testing |
| **Individuals** | Fleming, Florey, Chain, Gillies, McIndoe, Barnard |
| **Chance** | Fleming's mould in 1928 |
| **Communication** | Published papers (Chain found Fleming's); campaigns about AIDS and antibiotic use |
| **Economy and industry** | Pharmaceutical companies mass-produce and research drugs, driven by profit |

::: terms
- **Antibiotic**: a drug made from a living organism, such as penicillin, that kills bacteria inside the body.
- **Penicillin**: the first antibiotic, discovered by Fleming in 1928 and developed by Florey and Chain from 1939.
- **Pharmaceutical industry**: the large companies that research, test and mass-produce medicines.
- **Clinical trial**: testing a new drug carefully on patients before it is approved, made stricter after thalidomide.
- **Antibiotic resistance**: when bacteria evolve so that antibiotics no longer kill them, as with MRSA.
- **Alternative treatment**: a therapy outside mainstream medicine, such as homeopathy or acupuncture.
- **Plastic surgery**: surgery to repair or rebuild damaged parts of the body, pioneered by Gillies in the First World War.
- **Pedicle tube**: Gillies's skin graft kept attached at one end to keep its blood supply while it healed.
- **Blood bank**: a store of donated blood, made possible by matching blood groups and stopping clotting.
- **Transplant surgery**: replacing a failed organ with one from a donor, such as a kidney or heart.
- **Keyhole surgery**: operating through small cuts with a camera and long instruments.
:::

::: example Q1 Source utility (8)
Source A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a British government poster from 1944. It shows a wounded soldier on a stretcher being given an injection, with the slogan that penicillin, the new wonder drug, is saving the lives of our fighting men. How useful is Source A to an historian studying the development of penicillin? Explain your answer using Source A and your contextual knowledge. (8)

Source A is useful because it shows that by **1944** penicillin was being used to treat **wounded soldiers**. This fits what I know: after Florey and Chain showed it worked in **1940–41**, the **US and British governments** funded mass production, and by **D-Day in 1944** there was enough for the Allied wounded.

Its **provenance** makes it useful in another way. As a **government poster** in wartime it is **propaganda**: it calls penicillin a "wonder drug" to raise morale, and it does not mention the problems of production or that civilians could not yet get it. But that tells the historian that the government saw penicillin as a **war asset** worth publicising, which is why it paid for mass production.

It says nothing about how penicillin was **developed**: Fleming's chance discovery in 1928, his failure to produce it, and Florey and Chain's work. So it is very useful for the role of **war and government** in its mass production, but limited on its discovery. (Level 4.)

::: example Q2 Significance of penicillin (8)
Explain the significance of penicillin. (8)

**At the time**, penicillin was significant because it was the first **antibiotic**: for the first time doctors could cure bacterial infections inside the body. Mass-produced from 1943–44 with government funding, it saved many Allied soldiers from infected wounds, which had killed huge numbers in the First World War.

**Over time** it started the **antibiotic age**. Its success pushed the **pharmaceutical industry** to search for more antibiotics, such as streptomycin for TB, and made formerly deadly infections, such as pneumonia and infected cuts, easily treatable. It also made complex **surgery** safer by controlling infection.

But its significance also has a **negative** side: overuse has caused **antibiotic resistance**, such as **MRSA**, and few new antibiotics have been found in recent decades. So penicillin transformed medicine, but its long-term benefits depend on careful use. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Similarity (8)
Explain two ways in which the work of Ambroise Paré and Harold Gillies was similar. (8)

**First, both developed new surgical methods because of war.** Paré, a French army surgeon in the 16th century, ran out of boiling oil for gunshot wounds and found a soothing **ointment** worked better; he also used **ligatures** to tie off blood vessels during amputations. Gillies, an army surgeon in the **First World War**, faced soldiers with shattered faces from shrapnel and developed **skin grafts** and the **pedicle tube**.

**Second, both aimed to restore function and appearance, and spread their methods.** Paré designed **artificial limbs** for amputees and published his work in French, so ordinary surgeons could read it. Gillies rebuilt faces so soldiers could eat, speak and return to society, recorded every case with photographs and drawings, and trained other surgeons, including McIndoe. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Factor essay (16)
Has war been the main factor in the development of medicine? Explain your answer with reference to war and other factors. Use a range of examples from across your study of Health and the people: c1000 to the present day. (16)

**War** has often sped up change. Medieval surgeons such as John of Arderne gained experience on campaigns, and Paré's ointment and ligatures came from the battlefield. In the 20th century war drove the most rapid progress: **mobile X-rays**, **stored blood** and **plastic surgery** in the First World War, and in the Second World War McIndoe's burns surgery and the **mass production of penicillin**, paid for because governments needed it for soldiers. War also revealed poor health, as with the Boer War recruits.

But war rarely **created** the key ideas. **Science and technology** did: Röntgen discovered X-rays and Landsteiner blood groups before 1914, and germ theory came from laboratories in peacetime. **Individuals** made the breakthroughs (Fleming, Florey and Chain, Gillies), and **chance** played a part in penicillin. **Government** was essential: it funded penicillin and later, through the NHS, paid for transplants and keyhole surgery.

**Judgement.** War was the main factor in the **speed** of 20th-century surgery and penicillin's production, because it supplied urgency, money and experience. But across the whole period science, individuals and government were more fundamental, because war could only accelerate discoveries that already existed. (Level 4.)

---
Any two (1 each): smaller cuts and scars; less pain; less blood loss; lower risk of infection; faster recovery and shorter hospital stays.

::: watch
- **Giving Fleming all the credit.** He discovered penicillin by chance; Florey and Chain developed it; governments and industry mass-produced it.
- **Saying war invented X-rays or blood groups.** Both were discovered before 1914; war spread and improved their use.
- **Treating modern medicine as all progress.** Mention resistance (MRSA), cost, side effects and the thalidomide disaster.
- **Mixing up Gillies and McIndoe.** Gillies: First World War, faces, Sidcup. McIndoe: Second World War, RAF burns, East Grinstead.
- **Forgetting the British angle.** The theme is health in Britain: show how developments abroad (Barnard, US production) affected British patients.
