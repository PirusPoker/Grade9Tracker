## What it is

This lesson covers **Part two: Challenging royal authority** (c1500 to c1800): the **Pilgrimage of Grace** (1536) against Henry VIII; the **English Revolution**, from Charles I's quarrels with Parliament through the **Civil War**, the **New Model Army** and **radicals**, to the **execution of Charles I** (1649) and **Cromwell's** rule; and the **American Revolution**, when Britain's colonies rejected "taxation without representation".

The big idea: the question moved from "is the king ruling badly?" to "**who should hold power**: king, Parliament or the people?" The pilgrims of 1536 still claimed loyalty to the king; about a century later Parliament put a king on **trial** and killed him, and the American colonists then created a **republic** based on representation.

### 1. The Pilgrimage of Grace (1536)

**Background:** Henry VIII broke with Rome to end his marriage to Catherine of Aragon. The **Act of Supremacy** (1534) made him **Supreme Head of the Church of England**. His chief minister, **Thomas Cromwell**, organised the **dissolution of the smaller monasteries** from 1536.

**Causes:**

| Type | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Religious** | Anger at the closure of monasteries, attacks on traditional worship and saints' days, and rumours that parish church treasures would be seized; many northerners were loyal to the old Catholic faith |
| **Social** | In the north, monasteries gave **charity**, care for the sick and travellers, education and jobs; their loss hurt local communities |
| **Economic** | Poor harvests, new taxes (a subsidy raised in peacetime), rising rents and entry fines, enclosure |
| **Political** | Hatred of **Thomas Cromwell**, a low-born "evil counsellor"; northern nobles and gentry resented losing influence at court; concern over the royal succession |

**Events:** a rising in **Lincolnshire** in **October 1536** was quickly dispersed. A far larger one followed in **Yorkshire**, led by the lawyer **Robert Aske**. Tens of thousands of rebels, including nobles, gentry, clergy and commoners, took **York** and marched under the banner of the **Five Wounds of Christ**. They called themselves **pilgrims**, claimed loyalty to the king, and blamed his advisers. Their demands (drawn up at **Pontefract**) included restoring the monasteries and the Pope's authority in spiritual matters, and removing Cromwell.

**Henry's response:** his commander, the **Duke of Norfolk**, had too few troops to fight, so he **negotiated** at **Doncaster** (December 1536), promising a **general pardon** and a **parliament** in the north. The rebels went home. When a new rising broke out in early **1537**, Henry used it as an excuse to break his promises: around **200** people were executed, including **Aske**, hanged in chains at York.

**Impact on royal authority:** it was the largest rebellion of Tudor times, yet it **failed**. Henry **strengthened** his control: the **Council of the North** was reorganised to govern the region, and the **larger monasteries** were dissolved too (by 1540). It showed that **religious change** could provoke mass resistance, but also that a determined monarch could crush it by deception and force.

### 2. The causes of the English Revolution

**Charles I** (reigned 1625–1649) believed in the **Divine Right of Kings**: that he was chosen by God and answerable only to God.

| Cause | Details |
|---|---|
| **Money** | Charles raised money without Parliament's consent, such as **forced loans**. Parliament protested with the **Petition of Right** (1628). During **Personal Rule** (**1629–40**), when he ruled without Parliament, he used old rights such as **ship money**, extended to inland counties; John Hampden's refusal to pay became a famous test case |
| **Religion** | Charles married a **Catholic**, Henrietta Maria. **Archbishop Laud** brought in ceremonies, altar rails and decoration that **Puritans** saw as Catholic. Puritans who opposed him were harshly punished |
| **Scotland** | Forcing a new **prayer book** on Presbyterian **Scotland** (1637) caused rebellion. Charles had to recall Parliament (1640) to pay for war |
| **Parliament's power** | The **Long Parliament** (from 1640) had Charles's adviser Strafford executed, abolished ship money and demanded more control. A Catholic rising in **Ireland** (1641) raised the question of who should command an army |
| **Trigger** | In **January 1642** Charles entered Parliament with soldiers to **arrest five MPs**, who had escaped. Both sides prepared for war, which began in **August 1642** |

### 3. The Civil War, the New Model Army and radicalism

- The **Royalists** (Cavaliers) fought the **Parliamentarians** (Roundheads), who held London, the navy and more money.
- In **1645** Parliament created the **New Model Army**: a national, full-time, well-paid and disciplined army, commanded by **Sir Thomas Fairfax**, with **Oliver Cromwell** leading the cavalry. Officers were promoted on **merit**, not birth, and many soldiers were committed **Puritans**. It won the decisive **Battle of Naseby** (1645), and Charles surrendered in 1646.
- The army became a **political force**. Soldiers debated how England should be governed, notably at the **Putney Debates** (1647).

**Radical groups:**

| Group | Ideas |
|---|---|
| **Levellers** (leader **John Lilburne**) | Power should come from the **people**; a vote for most adult men; equality before the law; religious toleration; regular elections |
| **Diggers** (**Gerrard Winstanley**) | Land should be owned **in common**; in **1649** they dug and planted common land at St George's Hill in Surrey, until driven off |

Religious radicals such as **Quakers** and **Fifth Monarchists** also challenged the Church. Cromwell and the army leaders **suppressed** Leveller mutinies in 1649: radical ideas went further than most leaders wanted.

### 4. The trial and execution of Charles I, Cromwell and the Commonwealth

- Charles negotiated with all sides and started a **Second Civil War** (1648) with Scottish help. The army now saw him as untrustworthy.
- In **December 1648**, **Pride's Purge** removed MPs who wanted to negotiate, leaving the **Rump Parliament**, which set up a **High Court of Justice** to try the king for **treason** against the people.
- Charles **refused to recognise** the court, saying no court had authority over a king. He was found guilty and **executed** outside the Banqueting House in **Whitehall** on **30 January 1649**.

**Significance of the execution:** for the first time in England a king was tried and executed by his own subjects, in the name of the people. It struck at the **Divine Right** of Kings. But many were shocked: only a minority of MPs supported it, and Charles's dignity on the scaffold won sympathy.

**The Commonwealth and the Protectorate:**
- The monarchy and the **House of Lords** were abolished; England became a **republic** (the **Commonwealth**), 1649–53.
- Cromwell dismissed the Rump in **1653** and became **Lord Protector** (1653–58) under a written constitution. He ruled with Parliaments, but often clashed with them; for a time **Major-Generals** governed the regions, enforcing Puritan morality, which was unpopular.
- After Cromwell's death (1658), government collapsed, and **Charles II** was restored in **1660**.

**Impact:**
- **Short term:** military rule and Puritan restrictions made the republic unpopular; the monarchy came back.
- **Long term:** the monarchy returned **limited**. When James II tried to rule without Parliament, he was overthrown in **1688**, and the **Bill of Rights** (1689) confirmed that the monarch needed Parliament's consent for taxes and laws. Divine Right was finished in practice. Leveller ideas about votes and rights inspired later democrats.

### 5. The American Revolution

**Causes:**
- After the **Seven Years' War** (ended 1763), Britain had huge debts and wanted the **13 American colonies** to help pay for their own defence.
- **Taxation:** the **Stamp Act** (**1765**) taxed newspapers and legal documents; it was repealed after protests and boycotts, but Parliament insisted it had the right to tax the colonies. Later duties on imports, including **tea**, followed.
- **"No taxation without representation":** the colonists had **no MPs** in the British Parliament, so they argued that Parliament had no right to tax them. The British government claimed they were "virtually" represented.
- **Relationship between government and people:** clashes such as the **Boston Massacre** (1770) and the **Boston Tea Party** (**1773**), when colonists dumped tea into Boston harbour, led Britain to punish Massachusetts with harsh laws, which united the colonies against Britain.
- **Ideas:** Enlightenment ideas of natural rights; **Thomas Paine's** pamphlet **Common Sense** (1776) argued for independence and a republic.

**Events:** fighting began in **1775**. On **4 July 1776** the colonists issued the **Declaration of Independence**, claiming that all men are created equal with rights to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness, and that government needs the **consent of the governed**. With French help, they won; Britain recognised independence in **1783**.

**Impact and significance:**
- Britain **lost** its most important colonies, a blow to its power and prestige.
- The USA became a **republic** with a written constitution, elected representatives and no king: a model of **representative government**.
- It influenced the **French Revolution** (1789) and encouraged **British radicals** to demand reform of Parliament; Paine's later work spread these ideas in Britain.
- Limits: the vote was restricted, and **slavery** continued.

### 6. Factors in Part two

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Religion** | Dissolution of the monasteries; Laud's reforms and Puritan opposition; Scottish prayer book |
| **The economy** | Peacetime taxes in 1536; forced loans and ship money; taxes on the colonies |
| **Government** | Henry VIII's ruthless response; Charles I's Personal Rule; Parliament's claims to power; British policy towards the colonies |
| **Ideas** | Divine Right versus consent; Leveller democracy; "no taxation without representation"; natural rights |
| **War** | The Bishops' Wars forced Charles to recall Parliament; the Seven Years' War debts led to colonial taxes |
| **Individuals** | Aske, Thomas Cromwell, Charles I, Oliver Cromwell, Lilburne, Paine |
| **Communication** | Pamphlets spread radical ideas in the 1640s and in America |

::: terms
- **Act of Supremacy**: the 1534 law making Henry VIII Supreme Head of the Church of England.
- **Dissolution of the monasteries**: the closure of England's monasteries and seizure of their wealth by the Crown, from 1536.
- **Pilgrimage of Grace**: the 1536 northern rising led by Robert Aske against religious change and Thomas Cromwell.
- **Divine Right of Kings**: the belief that a monarch is chosen by God and answerable only to God.
- **Personal Rule**: the period 1629–40 when Charles I governed without calling Parliament.
- **Ship money**: an old tax on coastal areas for the navy, which Charles I extended to the whole country without Parliament's consent.
- **New Model Army**: Parliament's full-time, disciplined army from 1645, with promotion on merit.
- **Levellers**: radicals led by John Lilburne who wanted a wide vote for men, equality before the law and religious toleration.
- **Commonwealth**: the republic that ruled England after the execution of Charles I, 1649–53.
- **Lord Protector**: Oliver Cromwell's title as head of state, 1653–58.
- **No taxation without representation**: the American colonists' argument that Parliament could not tax them because they had no MPs.
- **Declaration of Independence**: the 1776 statement by the American colonies breaking from Britain and setting out natural rights.
:::

::: example Q1 Source utility (8)
Source A, written for this practice question, is a description of a print sold in London shortly after 30 January 1649. It shows Charles I kneeling calmly on the scaffold at Whitehall, praying, with a crown of thorns in his hand and a heavenly crown above his head, while a large crowd weeps. How useful is Source A to an historian studying reactions to the execution of Charles I? Explain your answer using Source A and your contextual knowledge. (8)

Source A is useful because it shows that the execution produced **sympathy** for Charles. He was executed outside the **Banqueting House** in Whitehall, and accounts agree that he died with dignity. The weeping crowd suggests that many ordinary people were **shocked** that subjects had killed their king.

Its **provenance** shows how Royalists wanted him remembered. The crown of thorns and heavenly crown present Charles as a **martyr** like Christ, persecuted for his faith, and supporting the idea of the **Divine Right of Kings**. A print sold in London soon after the event was **propaganda** designed to win support for the royal cause and for his son. It ignores the army's view that Charles was a **traitor** who had restarted the war.

So it is very useful for the **Royalist** reaction and for the sympathy the execution created, which helped the monarchy return in **1660**, but it is one-sided about the reasons for the trial. (Level 4.)

::: example Q2 Significance of the American Revolution (8)
Explain the significance of the American Revolution. (8)

**At the time**, the American Revolution was significant because Britain **lost** its 13 American colonies, recognised as independent in **1783**. It began over **taxation**: after the Stamp Act (1765) and the Boston Tea Party (1773), the colonists argued that there should be "**no taxation without representation**", since they had no MPs.

It was also significant for **ideas**. The **Declaration of Independence** (1776) said that people have natural rights and that government needs the **consent of the governed**. The new USA was a republic with elected representatives, showing that government could work without a king.

**Over time** these ideas spread. They influenced the **French Revolution** and encouraged **British radicals** such as Thomas Paine, whose writing helped inspire the demand for reform of Parliament that led to 1832 and Chartism. Its limits (a narrow vote, slavery) aside, it made representation a central question in British politics. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Difference (8)
Explain two ways in which the Pilgrimage of Grace and the Peasants' Revolt were different. (8)

**First, their causes were different.** The **Peasants' Revolt** (1381) was mainly **economic and social**: the poll tax, the Statute of Labourers after the Black Death, and resentment of serfdom. The **Pilgrimage of Grace** (1536) was mainly **religious**: rebels opposed the **dissolution of the monasteries** and Henry VIII's break with Rome, marching under the banner of the Five Wounds of Christ, though economic grievances played a part.

**Second, they involved different people and methods.** The Peasants' Revolt was led by commoners, **Wat Tyler** and **John Ball**, who marched on **London**, burned the Savoy Palace and killed the Archbishop. The Pilgrimage included **nobles and gentry** as well as commoners, was led by the lawyer **Robert Aske**, stayed in the **north**, and was largely peaceful: the rebels negotiated with the Duke of Norfolk at Doncaster and went home after being promised a pardon. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Factor essay (16)
Has religion been the main factor in challenges to authority? Explain your answer with reference to religion and other factors. Use a range of examples from across your study of Power and the people: c1170 to the present day. (16)

**Religion** was central to several challenges. John's quarrel with the **Pope** weakened him before Magna Carta, and **John Ball's** preaching gave the Peasants' Revolt its idea of equality. The **Pilgrimage of Grace** (1536) was driven by anger at the **dissolution of the monasteries**. In the 1640s, **Laud's** reforms and the Scottish prayer book turned Puritans against Charles I, and religious radicals such as the Quakers challenged the social order. Later, Christian belief inspired abolitionists.

But other factors were usually as important. **The economy** caused the most challenges: scutage in 1215, the poll tax in 1381, ship money in the 1630s and taxes on the American colonies. **Ideas** of consent and representation became more important over time: the Levellers, "no taxation without representation", Chartism and the suffragettes. **War** often triggered crises, by creating the need for taxes.

**Judgement.** Religion was the main factor in the 16th and 17th centuries, when Church and state were bound together. But across the whole period the **economy**, especially tax, was the most common cause, and after 1700 **ideas** of representation and rights replaced religion as the driving force of challenges. (Level 4.)
