## What it is

This lesson covers the rest of Part one, **Germany and the growth of democracy**: the **impact of the First World War** (war weariness, economic problems, defeat, the end of the monarchy, and the post-war problems of reparations, the Ruhr and hyperinflation), and **Weimar democracy** (political unrest 1919–23, the **extent of recovery** under Stresemann 1924–29, and **Weimar culture**).

The big idea: the Weimar Republic was born out of defeat and blamed for it. It survived a near-fatal crisis in 1923 and recovered under Stresemann, but the recovery rested on American loans, and many Germans never accepted the Republic.

### 1. The impact of the First World War, 1914–18

- **War weariness.** About two million German soldiers died in the war and millions more were wounded. The **British naval blockade** cut food imports. In the **"turnip winter" of 1916–17** many people lived on turnips; hundreds of thousands of civilians died from hunger-related illness during the war. Strikes grew in 1917–18.
- **Economic problems.** The government paid for the war mainly by **borrowing** and **printing money**, expecting to make the defeated enemies pay. Prices rose sharply, national debt soared and real wages fell.
- **Defeat.** The spring offensive of 1918 failed; by late September the generals **Hindenburg and Ludendorff** told the Kaiser the war was lost and advised a more democratic government to seek peace.
- **The end of the monarchy.** In **late October–early November 1918** sailors at **Kiel** mutinied rather than sail on a last attack. Workers' and soldiers' councils spread across Germany. On **9 November 1918** the Kaiser **abdicated** and fled to the Netherlands; a republic was declared, led by the SPD's **Friedrich Ebert**. Civilian politicians signed the **armistice** on **11 November 1918**.
- **Consequence: the "stab in the back" myth.** Because the army had not been beaten on German soil, nationalists claimed it had been betrayed by socialists, Jews and the politicians who signed the armistice, the **"November criminals"**. The Republic carried the blame for defeat from day one.

### 2. The Weimar constitution (1919)

| Feature | Effect |
|---|---|
| **President** elected every 7 years | Appointed the Chancellor; commanded the army |
| **Article 48** | Let the President rule by decree in an emergency: a back door to dictatorship |
| **Reichstag** elected by men and women over 20 | Chancellor and government needed its support |
| **Proportional representation** | Fair, but many small parties won seats, so governments were **coalitions** that often collapsed |

### 3. Post-war problems

- **The Treaty of Versailles (June 1919)** was seen as a **Diktat** (dictated peace): war guilt (Article 231), loss of land and all colonies, an army of 100,000 men, and **reparations**, fixed in 1921 at **£6,600 million**. The Republic signed under protest and was hated for it.
- **The occupation of the Ruhr (January 1923).** When Germany fell behind with payments, **French and Belgian troops** occupied the Ruhr, Germany's industrial heartland, to take goods instead. The government ordered **passive resistance**: workers went on strike and were paid by the government.
- **Hyperinflation (1923).** With no goods produced and strikers to pay, the government **printed money**. Prices spiralled: by November 1923 a loaf of bread cost billions of marks and the mark was worthless.

| Who suffered | Who gained or coped |
|---|---|
| **Savers and the middle classes**: lifetime savings wiped out | **Debtors**, including those with mortgages and loans, paid off debts in worthless money |
| People on **fixed incomes and pensions** | Some **businessmen** bought up firms cheaply |
| Workers whose wages lagged behind prices; shortages of food | **Farmers** could barter food |

The middle classes in particular lost faith in the Republic, and the memory of 1923 shaped politics for years.

### 4. Political unrest, 1919–23

| Event | Who and what | Why it failed |
|---|---|---|
| **Spartacist rising (January 1919)** | Communists led by **Rosa Luxemburg** and **Karl Liebknecht** tried to seize Berlin to create a Soviet-style state | Ebert used the army and the **Freikorps** (ex-soldier volunteers); Luxemburg and Liebknecht were killed |
| **Kapp Putsch (March 1920)** | **Wolfgang Kapp** and Freikorps units seized Berlin to set up a right-wing government; the government fled | The army would not fire on the Freikorps, but a **general strike** by Berlin workers paralysed the city and the putsch collapsed |
| **Political murders (1919–22)** | Right-wing groups murdered hundreds of opponents, including the ministers **Erzberger (1921)** and **Rathenau (1922)** | Right-wing judges often gave light sentences |
| **Munich Putsch (8–9 November 1923)** | **Hitler** and Ludendorff tried to seize power in Bavaria, then march on Berlin | Bavarian leaders turned against him; police fired on the march; Hitler was arrested. At his trial he gained national publicity and served only about nine months, writing **Mein Kampf** |

Threats came from **left and right**, but the right was treated more leniently by the army and the courts, which tells you where the Republic's real weakness lay.

### 5. Recovery under Stresemann, 1924–29

**Gustav Stresemann** was Chancellor for a few months in 1923 and Foreign Minister until his death in October 1929.

| Policy | What it did |
|---|---|
| **Ending passive resistance (September 1923)** | Restarted production in the Ruhr |
| **The Rentenmark (November 1923)** | A new, strictly limited currency, ending hyperinflation; replaced by the Reichsmark in 1924 |
| **Dawes Plan (1924)** | Reparations payments reduced for the time being and spread out; **American loans** to rebuild industry; the French left the Ruhr by 1925 |
| **Locarno Treaties (1925)** | Germany accepted its western borders; it was treated as an equal |
| **League of Nations (1926)** | Germany joined, with a permanent seat on the Council |
| **Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928)** | Germany was among dozens of states renouncing war |
| **Young Plan (1929)** | Total reparations cut to about **£2,000 million**, paid over 59 years |

**The extent of recovery** is the key issue:
- **Signs of recovery:** stable currency; industrial output back to pre-war levels by about 1928; new roads, housing and factories; unemployment insurance (1927); Germany's international standing restored; extremist parties lost votes (the Nazis won only **12 seats in 1928**).
- **Limits:** the economy depended on **short-term American loans** that could be recalled; **farmers** suffered from falling prices; unemployment never fell far; the middle classes never recovered their savings; coalitions still collapsed; and in 1925 the old war hero **Hindenburg**, no friend of democracy, was elected President. Stresemann himself warned that Germany was "dancing on a volcano".

### 6. Weimar culture

Censorship was relaxed and Berlin became a centre of experiment:
- **Bauhaus** design school (founded by Walter Gropius in 1919): simple, functional buildings and furniture.
- **Art**: painters such as **Otto Dix** and George Grosz showed war cripples, poverty and corrupt elites.
- **Cinema**: films such as Fritz Lang's *Metropolis* (1927); stars such as Marlene Dietrich.
- **Cabaret and nightlife** in Berlin, often satirising politicians; jazz.
- **The "new woman"**: more women at work and in public life, short hair, independence.

**Why it divided Germans:** many older, rural and religious Germans and nationalists saw the new culture as immoral, foreign ("American") and Jewish-influenced. Weimar culture became evidence, for its enemies, that the Republic was decadent.

::: terms
- **Abdication**: giving up the throne, as Kaiser Wilhelm II did on 9 November 1918.
- **Armistice**: the ceasefire of 11 November 1918, signed by civilian politicians of the new republic.
- **November criminals**: the nationalist insult for the politicians who signed the armistice and later the Treaty of Versailles.
- **Article 48**: the clause of the Weimar constitution allowing the President to rule by decree in an emergency.
- **Proportional representation**: a voting system giving parties seats in proportion to votes, which led to many parties and unstable coalitions.
- **Diktat**: a dictated peace; the German view of the Treaty of Versailles, which they were not allowed to negotiate.
- **Reparations**: compensation Germany had to pay the Allies, set at £6,600 million in 1921.
- **Passive resistance**: the 1923 policy of refusing to work for the French and Belgian occupiers of the Ruhr.
- **Hyperinflation**: extremely rapid price rises, as in Germany in 1923 when the mark became worthless.
- **Freikorps**: armed bands of right-wing ex-soldiers, used against the Spartacists but involved in the Kapp Putsch.
- **Rentenmark**: the temporary currency introduced in November 1923 to end hyperinflation.
- **Dawes Plan**: the 1924 agreement that reorganised reparations payments and brought American loans to Germany.
:::

::: method
**Paper 1 Section A at a glance.** About an hour, 40 marks: Q1 differ (4), Q2 why differ (4), Q3 more convincing (8), Q4 describe two (4), Q5 in what ways (8), Q6 which bullet was more important (12). This topic is a favourite for all six: interpretations on recovery or culture, "describe two problems", "in what ways affected by hyperinflation", and "political or economic reasons" for Weimar's difficulties.

1. **Q1 (4): content only.** One overall difference, supported by a detail from each interpretation.
2. **Q2 (4): provenance + context.** Explain how each author's position, date, experience or purpose shaped the view, e.g. a Berlin cabaret performer versus a Bavarian priest on Weimar culture.
3. **Q3 (8): test both against knowledge.** Recovery questions turn on the Rentenmark, Dawes and Young Plans, Locarno and the League **versus** American loans, farmers and unemployment. End with a judgement such as "real but fragile".
4. **Q4 (4): two features, each with a precise detail** (dates, names, figures).
5. **Q5 (8): explain several effects; for Level 4, show differences between groups** (savers versus debtors in 1923) or over time.
6. **Q6 (12): a paragraph per bullet with evidence, then compare them**, e.g. economic crisis in 1923 fed political violence, so the bullets are linked.
:::

::: example Q1 How interpretations differ (4)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a Berlin journalist writing in 1928: "Germany is herself again. Prices are steady, factories are busy, new flats rise in every suburb, and our foreign minister sits with the great powers as an equal. The madness of 1923 is a bad dream." Interpretation B, also written for practice, is in the style of a historian writing in 2012: "The prosperity of the late 1920s was borrowed. American money paid for the new factories, farmers sank further into debt, and the middle classes had never got back what inflation took from them." How does Interpretation B differ from Interpretation A about Germany's recovery in the years 1924–29? Explain your answer based on what it says in Interpretations A and B. (4)

Interpretation A says Germany had **fully recovered**: prices were steady, factories busy and Germany was respected abroad (1–2). Interpretation B says the recovery was **shallow and fragile** because it rested on American money, and farmers and the middle classes were still suffering (+1). So A sees a genuine return to normal, while B sees a recovery built on loans that left many Germans behind (+1). Level 2, 4/4.
:::

::: example Q3 Which is more convincing (8)
Using Interpretations A and B above: which interpretation gives the more convincing opinion about Germany's recovery, 1924–29? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretations A and B. (8)

**A is convincing about stability and status.** The Rentenmark (November 1923) ended hyperinflation, and the Dawes Plan (1924) reduced payments for the time being and brought in loans, so factories reopened and industrial output was back to pre-war levels by about 1928. Abroad, Locarno (1925) and League membership (1926) made Germany an equal again.

**B is convincing about the foundations.** The Dawes loans were short-term American money that could be recalled at any time, which is exactly what happened after the Wall Street Crash in 1929. Farmers faced falling prices and debt throughout, and savers who lost everything in 1923 were never compensated.

**Judgement.** B is more convincing, because the test of a recovery is whether it lasts: within two years of 1928, recalled loans helped to push unemployment towards six million. A describes the surface of 1928 accurately, but B explains why it collapsed. (Level 4: both evaluated with precise knowledge; sustained judgement with a criterion.)
:::

::: example Q4 Describe two (4)
Describe two problems the Weimar Republic faced in 1923. (4)

**The occupation of the Ruhr.** In January 1923 French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr because Germany had fallen behind with reparations (1). The government ordered passive resistance and paid the strikers, so industrial production collapsed (1).

**The Munich Putsch.** In November 1923 Hitler and Ludendorff tried to seize power in Munich (1). It failed when police fired on the march, but it showed the Republic faced violent enemies on the right (1).
:::

::: example Q5 In what ways (8)
In what ways were the lives of the German people affected by hyperinflation in 1923? Explain your answer. (8)

**Savings and the middle classes.** People who had saved for years found their savings worthless, and those on fixed pensions could not buy food. Many middle-class Germans felt ruined and betrayed by the Republic.

**Daily life.** Prices rose so fast that workers were paid more than once a day and rushed to spend their wages; people bartered goods; food was scarce in the towns.

**Winners.** Debtors, including people with mortgages and businesses with loans, paid off debts in worthless marks, and some businessmen bought up firms cheaply.

**Complexity (Level 4).** The crisis did not affect everyone equally: it transferred wealth from savers to borrowers. That unevenness mattered politically, because the middle classes who lost most had been the Republic's natural supporters, and many later turned to the Nazis.
:::

::: example Q6 Which was more important (12)
Which of the following was the more important reason why Weimar Germany was difficult to govern, 1919–1923: political reasons; economic reasons? Explain your answer with reference to both bullet points. (12)

**Political reasons.** The Republic was blamed for the armistice and Versailles (the "November criminals"). Proportional representation created weak coalitions. It faced risings from the left (the Spartacists, January 1919) and the right (the Kapp Putsch, March 1920; the Munich Putsch, November 1923), and political murders such as Rathenau's in 1922. The army would not fire on the Kapp putschists, so the Republic could not rely on its own forces.

**Economic reasons.** War debts and reparations of £6,600 million strained the budget. When Germany fell behind in 1923, the French and Belgians occupied the Ruhr; paying passive resisters by printing money caused hyperinflation, wiping out savings.

**Judgement.** Political reasons were more important, because the Republic's illegitimacy in many Germans' eyes was there from 1918, before the economic crisis, and its enemies on the right were protected by the army and judges. But the two were linked: the economic crisis of 1923 gave Hitler his opportunity in Munich, and once Stresemann fixed the currency the violence faded. (Level 4.)

::: q
Why did Weimar culture divide Germans? (4)
---
It was experimental and freer after censorship was relaxed (1), e.g. Bauhaus, Otto Dix's paintings or Berlin cabaret (1); conservatives, rural and religious Germans saw it as immoral and un-German (1); nationalists linked it to the Republic, so it became a reason to oppose democracy (1).

::: watch
- **Calling 1924–29 a "golden age" with no limits.** The spec says "the extent of recovery": always weigh American loans, farmers, unemployment and the extremists.
- **Mixing up the putsches.** Spartacists 1919 (left, Freikorps crushed them); Kapp 1920 (right, general strike defeated it); Munich 1923 (Hitler, police fire).
- **Dating hyperinflation to 1919.** Inflation built up from the war, but hyperinflation was 1923, after the Ruhr occupation.
- **Saying everyone lost in 1923.** Debtors and some businessmen gained: showing that difference is Level 4.
- **Treating Weimar culture as just a list.** Explain why it divided Germans.
- **Confusing Dawes (1924: loans, reorganised payments) with Young (1929: total cut to about £2,000 million).**
:::
