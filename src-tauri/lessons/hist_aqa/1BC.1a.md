## What it is

This lesson covers the first bullet of Part one, **the end of the Second World War**: the **Yalta and Potsdam Conferences**; the **division of Germany**; the **contrasting attitudes and ideologies of the USA and the USSR**, including the **aims of Stalin, Churchill, Roosevelt, Attlee and Truman**; and the **effect of the dropping of the atom bomb** on post-war superpower relations.

The big idea: the USA, Britain and the USSR had been allies only because they shared an enemy. As Germany's defeat approached, their very different ideologies and aims came to the surface. At **Yalta** (February 1945) they still cooperated; by **Potsdam** (July–August 1945) new leaders, Soviet actions in Eastern Europe and the American **atomic bomb** had turned cooperation into suspicion. The wartime alliance was breaking down, and the **Cold War** was beginning.

### 1. Contrasting ideologies

| | **USA: capitalism and democracy** | **USSR: communism** |
|---|---|---|
| Politics | Free elections between several parties; free speech and a free press | **One-party state**: only the Communist Party; elections offered no real choice; censorship |
| Economy | **Private ownership** of businesses and property; free market; profit as the motive | **State ownership** of land and industry; a **planned economy** run by the government |
| Values | Individual freedom matters most, even if it brings inequality | Equality and the good of society matter more than individual rights |
| Rights | Protected by a constitution and the courts | Secret police and labour camps used against opponents |
| View of the other | Communism threatened freedom and aimed at world revolution | Capitalism exploited workers and was hostile to the USSR |

**Earlier sources of distrust:** Western countries had sent troops against the Bolsheviks in the Russian Civil War; Stalin had been excluded from Munich (1938); he had made the Nazi-Soviet Pact (1939); and he believed Britain and the USA had deliberately delayed opening a second front in France until **D-Day (June 1944)**, leaving the USSR to bear the worst of the fighting.

### 2. The aims of the leaders

| Leader | Aims in 1945 |
|---|---|
| **Stalin** (USSR) | **Security**: the USSR had suffered more than any other Ally, with over 20 million dead, and had been invaded through Poland twice in 30 years, so he wanted a **buffer zone** of friendly (communist) states in Eastern Europe. Keep Germany **weak** and take **reparations** to rebuild. Spread communism where possible |
| **Churchill** (Britain, until July 1945) | Deeply **suspicious** of Stalin and communism. Wanted a free and independent **Poland** (Britain had gone to war in 1939 over Poland), a balance of power in Europe, and to protect the **British Empire** |
| **Roosevelt** (USA, until April 1945) | Believed he could **work with Stalin**. Wanted a **United Nations** organisation to keep peace, Soviet help against **Japan**, free elections and self-determination, and to bring US troops home |
| **Truman** (USA, from April 1945) | More **hostile to communism** and less experienced in foreign affairs; he believed Stalin was breaking his Yalta promises and should be **stood up to**. He wanted to stop Soviet expansion and open trade |
| **Attlee** (Britain, from July 1945) | Labour leader who replaced Churchill midway through Potsdam. Broadly **continued Churchill's policy**: wary of the USSR, but Britain was now economically exhausted and increasingly dependent on the USA |

### 3. The Yalta Conference, February 1945

Held at **Yalta** in the Crimea (USSR). **Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin** met while Germany was close to defeat and Soviet troops were advancing through Eastern Europe. The atmosphere was generally **friendly**.

| Agreed | Disagreed or left vague |
|---|---|
| **Germany** (and **Berlin**) to be divided into **four zones** run by the USA, Britain, France and the USSR | **Poland's government**: Stalin had set up a communist government in Lublin; the West backed the Polish government in exile in London. They agreed the communist government would include some non-communists and hold **free elections**, but did not say how |
| **Free elections** in the liberated countries of Eastern Europe (the Declaration on Liberated Europe) | **Reparations**: Stalin wanted huge sums; Roosevelt and Churchill would not fix a figure, though they agreed that Germany would pay, with a large share to the USSR |
| Poland's eastern border to move **west**, so the USSR kept land it had taken in 1939; Poland to be compensated with German land | Poland's **western** border was left undecided |
| **Nazi war criminals** to be put on trial | |
| The USSR to join the **war against Japan** once Germany was defeated | |
| A **United Nations** organisation to be set up | |

**Verdict:** Yalta looked like a success, but its agreements on Eastern Europe were vague. "Free elections" meant different things to each side, and Stalin's armies already held the territory.

### 4. Between the conferences: why relations worsened

| Change | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Roosevelt died (April 1945)**; **Truman** became president | Truman was far more suspicious of Stalin and spoke bluntly to the Soviet foreign minister, Molotov |
| **Germany surrendered (May 1945)** | The common enemy was gone, so the main reason for the alliance disappeared |
| **Soviet control of Eastern Europe** | The Red Army occupied Poland and much of Eastern Europe. The communist government in Poland was confirmed and non-communist Polish leaders were arrested. The West saw Yalta's promises being broken |
| **The atomic bomb tested (July 1945)** | On the eve of Potsdam the USA successfully tested its first atomic bomb, making Truman more confident |
| **Churchill lost the British election** | Attlee replaced him during the conference, so two of the three leaders at Potsdam were new |

### 5. The Potsdam Conference, July–August 1945

Held at **Potsdam**, near Berlin, from **17 July to 2 August 1945**. **Stalin, Truman** and **Churchill**, replaced by **Attlee** after Labour's election victory. The atmosphere was much **cooler** than at Yalta.

| Agreed | Disagreed |
|---|---|
| The four **zones** of Germany and Berlin confirmed, run by an **Allied Control Council** | **Reparations**: Truman would not let the USSR take what it wanted from all of Germany; it was agreed each power would take reparations mainly from its **own zone**, with the USSR receiving some industrial equipment from the western zones. Stalin felt cheated |
| The **Nazi Party** banned; **denazification**; war criminals tried (the trials took place at **Nuremberg**) | **Poland and Eastern Europe**: Truman and Attlee objected to Soviet control and the lack of free elections; Stalin insisted on a friendly Poland |
| Germany to be **demilitarised** | **The future of Germany**: the USSR wanted it kept weak; the West increasingly wanted it to recover |
| Poland's western border moved to the rivers **Oder and Neisse**, giving Poland a large area of German land; Germans there were expelled | Stalin's demand for a Soviet military base in the **Turkish Straits** was not agreed |

During the conference **Truman told Stalin** that the USA had a powerful new weapon. Stalin showed little reaction, but his spies had already told him about it.

**Yalta and Potsdam compared**

| | Yalta (February 1945) | Potsdam (July–August 1945) |
|---|---|---|
| Leaders | Roosevelt, Churchill, Stalin | Truman, Churchill then Attlee, Stalin |
| Situation | Germany still fighting; alliance needed | Germany defeated; common enemy gone |
| Mood | Cooperative, friendly | Suspicious, tense |
| Eastern Europe | Promises of free elections | Soviet control obvious; West protested |
| Atomic bomb | Not yet tested | Tested; Truman confident |

### 6. The division of Germany and Berlin

- **Four zones**: the **USSR** in the east, **Britain** in the north-west, the **USA** in the south, and **France** in the west (formed from parts of the British and American zones).
- **Berlin**, deep inside the Soviet zone, was also divided into **four sectors**.
- The Soviet zone was mainly agricultural; the industrial heartland (the Ruhr) was in the British zone. This mattered for reparations and food supplies.
- The plan was for Germany to be run jointly and eventually reunited, but the two sides soon ran their zones in different ways, leading to the Berlin crisis of 1948–49 and two separate German states.

### 7. The atomic bomb and its effect

- The USA dropped atomic bombs on **Hiroshima (6 August 1945)** and **Nagasaki (9 August 1945)**. The USSR declared war on Japan on **8 August**. Japan **surrendered** in mid-August.

**Why it damaged relations**
- Truman **had not shared** the secret with Stalin as an ally, which increased Soviet distrust.
- Some historians argue the bombs were partly meant to **impress the USSR** and to end the war before the Soviets could gain much in the Far East; the USA kept the USSR out of the occupation of Japan.
- The USA now had an **atomic monopoly**, which made Truman feel able to take a **tougher line** with Stalin.
- Stalin saw the bomb as a threat and ordered Soviet scientists to build one as fast as possible; the USSR tested its first bomb in **1949**. This began the **arms race**.

**But** the bomb was not the only cause of the breakdown: the clash of ideologies, the change of leaders, the disputes over Poland and reparations, and the Red Army's control of Eastern Europe all played their part.

::: terms
- **Capitalism**: an economic system based on private ownership and free trade, linked in the USA with democracy and individual freedom.
- **Communism**: a system based on state ownership and a planned economy, run in the USSR by a single party.
- **Ideology**: a set of political beliefs about how a country and society should be run.
- **Grand Alliance**: the wartime alliance of the USA, Britain and the USSR against Nazi Germany.
- **Yalta Conference**: the February 1945 meeting of Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin, which agreed to divide Germany and to hold free elections in Eastern Europe.
- **Potsdam Conference**: the July–August 1945 meeting of Truman, Stalin and Churchill then Attlee, marked by disagreements over reparations and Eastern Europe.
- **Buffer zone**: an area of friendly states protecting a country from invasion; Stalin's aim in Eastern Europe.
- **Reparations**: payments or goods taken from a defeated country to compensate for war damage.
- **Denazification**: removing Nazi influence from German government, law and society after 1945.
- **Zones of occupation**: the four areas into which Germany and Berlin were divided, each run by one of the Allies.
- **Atomic monopoly**: the USA's position as the only power with the atomic bomb, from 1945 until the Soviet test of 1949.
- **Cold War**: the state of hostility between the USA and the USSR, fought by every means short of direct war.
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of how relations between the Allies changed between the Yalta and Potsdam conferences in 1945. (8)

At **Yalta** in February 1945 the Big Three were still fighting Germany together, **so** they cooperated. They agreed to divide Germany into four zones, to set up the United Nations, and that the USSR would fight Japan. Stalin also promised free elections in Eastern Europe, though the wording on Poland was vague.

In the following months the situation changed. **Roosevelt died in April** and **Truman**, who was much more suspicious of communism, became president. **When Germany surrendered in May**, the common enemy that held the alliance together was gone. **At the same time** the Red Army controlled Poland, where the communist government was confirmed and non-communist leaders were arrested. **This convinced** Truman that Stalin was breaking his Yalta promises.

**Then**, just before Potsdam, the USA tested its first **atomic bomb**, making Truman confident he could take a firm line. During the conference Churchill was replaced by **Attlee**. **As a result**, Potsdam was tense: the leaders confirmed the zones and the Oder-Neisse border, but quarrelled over reparations, which were limited mainly to each power's own zone, and over Soviet control of Eastern Europe.

**The consequence** was that by August 1945 trust had largely gone. The dropping of the atomic bombs on Japan, without consulting Stalin, deepened his suspicion and set the scene for the Cold War. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Two sources (12)
Source B, written for this practice question, is in the style of a note written by Truman in his diary during the Potsdam Conference in July 1945, saying that the new weapon is the most terrible in history and that the Russians will be easier to deal with now. Source C, also written for practice, is in the style of a message from Stalin to Soviet scientists in August 1945, ordering them to build an atomic bomb as quickly as possible, whatever the cost, because the balance has been destroyed. How useful are Sources B and C to an historian studying the effect of the atomic bomb on relations between the USA and the USSR? (12)

**Source B** shows that the bomb changed **Truman's attitude**: he expected it to give him leverage over Stalin. Context supports this: the test came on the eve of Potsdam, Truman was tougher than Roosevelt, and he only hinted at the weapon to Stalin. Its **provenance** gives it great value: a private diary written at the conference is likely to show Truman's honest thinking rather than public statements.

**Source C** shows the **Soviet reaction**: alarm, and determination to end the American monopoly. This is supported by context: Stalin had not been told about the bomb as an ally, the USSR was kept out of Japan, and the Soviet Union did test its own bomb in 1949, starting the arms race. As an order from Stalin himself, it is strong evidence of Soviet priorities, though written for scientists rather than to explain his feelings.

**Judgement.** Together the sources are highly useful because they show both sides' reactions and how one fed the other: American confidence produced Soviet fear, which produced an arms race. Neither shows the other causes of tension, such as Poland, but for the effect of the bomb itself they are very valuable. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Essay (16)
"The atomic bomb was the main reason for the breakdown of the wartime alliance in 1945." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. (16)

**The atomic bomb.** The successful test in July 1945 made Truman more confident at Potsdam, and the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August, without consulting Stalin, deepened Soviet distrust. The USA kept the USSR out of the occupation of Japan and enjoyed an atomic monopoly, so Stalin ordered a crash programme to build his own bomb. It changed the balance of power and made the USA less willing to compromise.

**Ideology.** But the alliance had always rested on a shared enemy, not shared values. Capitalism and communism were fundamentally opposed, and distrust went back to Western intervention in the Russian Civil War and Stalin's Nazi-Soviet Pact. Once Germany surrendered in May 1945, those differences came to the surface.

**Soviet actions in Eastern Europe.** Before the bomb was dropped, the Red Army already controlled Poland and Stalin had installed a communist government there, breaking the spirit of the Yalta promise of free elections. This was the main issue at Potsdam.

**New leaders.** Roosevelt's death in April brought in Truman, who was far less willing to trust Stalin; Churchill's replacement by Attlee also weakened personal ties.

**Judgement.** The bomb made relations worse, but it was not the main reason for the breakdown. The disputes over Poland and reparations, and Truman's hostility, came first, and they grew from deep ideological differences that only the war had hidden. The bomb accelerated a breakdown that was already under way by turning mistrust into an arms race. (Level 4.)

::: watch
- **Mixing up the leaders.** Yalta: Roosevelt, Churchill, Stalin. Potsdam: Truman, Stalin, Churchill then Attlee.
- **Saying the bomb was dropped before Potsdam.** It was tested in July, during the run-up to Potsdam; Hiroshima and Nagasaki came in August, after the conference.
- **Treating Yalta as a failure.** It produced many agreements; the problem was that the promises on Eastern Europe were vague.
- **Forgetting Attlee.** The spec names him: he replaced Churchill during Potsdam and broadly continued his policy.
- **Describing ideologies without linking them.** Show how the differences produced specific disputes, such as over elections in Poland.
- **Q3 as two separate descriptions.** "How relations changed" needs the causes of the change between February and August.
