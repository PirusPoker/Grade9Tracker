## What it is

This lesson covers the third bullet of Part three, **the outbreak of war**: the **occupation of Czechoslovakia** (March 1939); the **role of the USSR and the Nazi-Soviet Pact**; the **invasion of Poland and the outbreak of war in September 1939**; and **responsibility for the outbreak of war**, including that of the three key individuals the spec names: **Hitler, Stalin and Chamberlain**.

The big idea: within six months of Munich, Hitler had shown that his aims went far beyond uniting German speakers. Britain abandoned appeasement and guaranteed Poland. Hitler believed Britain and France would back down again, and the **Nazi-Soviet Pact** removed his fear of a war on two fronts. When he invaded Poland, Britain and France kept their promise, and a European war began.

### 1. The occupation of Czechoslovakia, March 1939

- After Munich, what remained of Czechoslovakia was **defenceless**: it had lost its border fortifications, much of its industry and its allies' trust. Poland and Hungary had also taken land.
- Hitler encouraged **Slovak nationalists** to demand independence, creating a crisis inside the country.
- In March 1939 the Czech President, **Emil Hácha**, was summoned to Berlin and bullied, with threats that Prague would be bombed, into accepting German "protection".
- On **15 March 1939** German troops marched into **Prague**. The Czech lands became the **Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia**; Slovakia became a separate state under German control.
- Shortly afterwards Germany also took the port of **Memel** from Lithuania, and in April **Italy invaded Albania**.

**Why this ended appeasement**
- The Czechs were **not Germans**. Hitler could no longer claim he only wanted to unite German speakers or correct the unfairness of Versailles.
- He had **broken the Munich Agreement** within six months, showing that his promises were worthless.
- Chamberlain, publicly humiliated, now accepted that Hitler aimed to dominate Europe by force.

**Britain's change of policy**

| Action | Date | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Guarantee to Poland** with France | **31 March 1939** | Britain and France promised to help Poland if its independence was threatened. Similar guarantees soon went to Greece and Romania |
| **Conscription** introduced in Britain | Spring 1939 | The first peacetime conscription in modern Britain: a sign Britain was preparing for war |
| Faster **rearmament** | 1939 | Especially fighter aircraft and air defences |

Hitler's next target was clear: **Poland**. He demanded the return of **Danzig** and a road and railway link across the **Polish Corridor**. Poland, now backed by Britain and France, **refused**. In May 1939 Germany and Italy also signed a military alliance, the **Pact of Steel**.

### 2. The role of the USSR

**Why Stalin distrusted Britain and France**
- He had been **excluded from Munich**, even though the USSR had an alliance with Czechoslovakia. He concluded that the Western powers were willing to let Hitler expand **eastwards**, perhaps hoping Germany and the USSR would fight each other.
- Many British Conservatives, including Chamberlain, deeply **distrusted communism** and the USSR.
- Stalin's purges had weakened the Red Army, and he needed **time to rebuild** it.

**The failed Anglo-French talks (summer 1939)**
- Britain and France opened talks with the USSR about an alliance to protect Poland. They moved **slowly**.
- In August they sent a **low-ranking military delegation**, which travelled to Moscow by a **slow sea route** and had no power to sign an agreement. Stalin took this as a sign that they were not serious.
- **Poland refused** to let Soviet troops cross its territory to fight Germany, because it feared they would never leave. Britain and France could not persuade it otherwise.
- Stalin also feared **Japan** in the east, where Soviet and Japanese forces clashed in 1939, so he wanted to avoid a war in Europe at the same time.

### 3. The Nazi-Soviet Pact, 23 August 1939

Signed in Moscow by the two foreign ministers, **Joachim von Ribbentrop** (Germany) and **Vyacheslav Molotov** (USSR). The world was astonished: Nazism and communism were bitter enemies.

| Public terms | Secret terms |
|---|---|
| A **non-aggression pact** for **ten years**: neither would attack the other or help anyone who did | A secret protocol divided **Poland** between them and placed **Finland, Estonia, Latvia** and **Bessarabia** in the Soviet **sphere of influence** |

**Why each side signed**

| Hitler | Stalin |
|---|---|
| Could invade Poland **without fear of a Soviet attack**, avoiding a **war on two fronts** (the mistake Germany made in 1914) | Bought **time** to strengthen the Red Army before the war with Germany he expected |
| Believed that without Soviet help, Britain and France could not save Poland and **would back down** | Gained **territory** in eastern Poland and influence over the Baltic states, a buffer zone against future attack |
| Gained Soviet raw materials through a trade agreement | Distrusted Britain and France after Munich and the half-hearted talks; a deal with Hitler offered more |

**Significance:** the pact made war over Poland **almost certain**. It gave Hitler the confidence to attack, and it left Britain and France without the only ally able to defend Poland directly.

### 4. The invasion of Poland and the outbreak of war

| Date (1939) | Event |
|---|---|
| **23 August** | Nazi-Soviet Pact signed |
| **25 August** | Britain signed a formal **alliance with Poland**, confirming its guarantee; Hitler briefly postponed the attack |
| **1 September** | German forces **invaded Poland**, using the **Blitzkrieg** ("lightning war") of air attacks and fast tank columns. Germany claimed a staged Polish attack on a German border post as an excuse |
| 1–2 September | Britain and France demanded that Germany withdraw. Hitler did not reply |
| **3 September** | Britain and France **declared war** on Germany |
| 17 September | The **USSR invaded eastern Poland**, as agreed in the secret protocol. Poland was crushed and divided within weeks |

Hitler had expected Britain and France to give way, as they had at Munich. He was wrong: Britain could not abandon a public guarantee without losing all credibility. Yet in practice Britain and France could do little to help Poland directly; this was the start of the "Phoney War" in the west.

### 5. Responsibility for the outbreak of war

The spec names three key individuals. A strong answer weighs each one and links them together.

**Hitler**
- His **aims** (overturning Versailles, uniting Germans, Lebensraum in the east) made expansion and conflict likely; they were set out in *Mein Kampf*.
- He **rearmed**, broke treaty after treaty (Rhineland, Anschluss), and broke the **Munich Agreement** by taking Prague.
- He **invaded Poland**, knowing Britain and France had guaranteed it.
- Some argue he was a **gambler** who expected Britain and France to back down again, and did not plan a general war in 1939; others argue that war was always his goal.

**Chamberlain**
- **Appeasement** encouraged Hitler: each concession made him more confident, and Munich convinced him that Britain would not fight.
- He **excluded the USSR** from Munich and handled the 1939 talks with Stalin slowly and without enthusiasm, helping push Stalin into Hitler's arms.
- His **guarantee to Poland** was hard to carry out in practice and arguably encouraged Polish defiance.
- **In his defence:** appeasement had strong public support and gave Britain time to rearm; when Hitler broke his word, Chamberlain changed policy and kept Britain's promise.

**Stalin**
- The **Nazi-Soviet Pact** gave Hitler the security he needed to invade Poland; without it, Hitler would have faced a possible war on two fronts.
- The **secret protocol** shows Stalin also wanted territory, and he invaded Poland himself on 17 September.
- **In his defence:** he had been excluded from Munich and given little reason to trust the West; he was buying time for a war he expected.

**Other factors**
- The **Treaty of Versailles** created German grievances, such as the Polish Corridor and Danzig.
- The **failure of the League** in Manchuria and Abyssinia showed aggression could succeed.
- **Mussolini's** alliance (the Axis, the Pact of Steel) encouraged Hitler; **France** followed Britain's lead.

**Making the judgement:** most historians place the main responsibility on **Hitler**, because his aims and actions drove events; the others created the conditions or failed to stop him. A sophisticated answer shows how the factors interacted: Chamberlain's appeasement and exclusion of Stalin → Stalin's pact with Hitler → Hitler's confidence to invade Poland.

::: terms
- **Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia**: the Czech lands occupied by Germany in March 1939 and ruled as part of the Reich.
- **Guarantee to Poland**: the promise by Britain and France on 31 March 1939 to help Poland if its independence was threatened.
- **Danzig**: the German-speaking free city run by the League, whose return Hitler demanded from Poland in 1939.
- **Pact of Steel**: the military alliance signed by Germany and Italy in May 1939.
- **Nazi-Soviet Pact**: the non-aggression pact signed by Germany and the USSR on 23 August 1939.
- **Secret protocol**: the hidden part of the Nazi-Soviet Pact dividing Poland and Eastern Europe between Germany and the USSR.
- **Non-aggression pact**: an agreement between two countries not to attack each other.
- **War on two fronts**: fighting enemies in the east and west at the same time, which Hitler wanted to avoid.
- **Blitzkrieg**: "lightning war"; fast attacks combining aircraft, tanks and infantry, used against Poland in 1939.
- **Sphere of influence**: an area where one power has control or special interests, without necessarily ruling it directly.
- **Ultimatum**: a final demand backed by a threat, such as Britain's demand that Germany withdraw from Poland.
:::

Source A, written for this practice question, is a description of a British cartoon published in September 1939. It shows Hitler and Stalin smiling and shaking hands across a map of Poland that has been torn in two, while each hides a dagger behind his back; a caption calls them "New Friends". Source A is critical of the Nazi-Soviet Pact. How do you know? Explain your answer using Source A and your contextual knowledge. (4)

The two leaders shake hands while hiding daggers, showing that the pact was a **cynical deal** between bitter enemies, Nazism and communism, who did not trust each other at all (1). This fits the context: the pact of 23 August 1939 astonished the world because Hitler had always attacked communism and had signed the Anti-Comintern Pact (1). The torn map of Poland between them shows the pact's real purpose: the **secret protocol divided Poland**, and Germany invaded on 1 September and the USSR on 17 September (1). Published in Britain just after war began, it reflects British anger that Stalin had helped make the war possible, after the failure of Britain's own talks with the USSR (1). Level 2, 4/4.

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of how events in 1939 led to the outbreak of war. (8)

In **March 1939** Hitler occupied the rest of Czechoslovakia, breaking the Munich Agreement. **Because** the Czechs were not German, this showed his aims went beyond uniting German speakers, **so** Chamberlain abandoned appeasement and with France **guaranteed Poland** on 31 March.

Hitler now demanded **Danzig** and a route across the Polish Corridor. Poland, **encouraged by the guarantee**, refused. Britain and France knew they could only help Poland effectively with Soviet support, **so** they opened talks with Stalin. **However**, the talks were slow, their delegation was low-ranking, and Poland refused to let Soviet troops cross its land. Stalin, already distrustful after being excluded from Munich, turned to Hitler.

**This led to** the **Nazi-Soviet Pact** of 23 August 1939. Hitler no longer feared a war on two fronts, and the secret protocol promised Stalin eastern Poland. Believing Britain and France would back down without Soviet help, Hitler **invaded Poland on 1 September**.

**As a result**, Britain and France sent an ultimatum, which Hitler ignored, and on **3 September** they declared war. Their guarantee had turned a German attack on Poland into a European war. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Two sources (12)
Source B, written for this practice question, is in the style of a Soviet newspaper article from August 1939, claiming that the non-aggression pact with Germany is a victory for peace and that the Soviet Union will not be drawn into a war for the profit of British and French capitalists. Source C, also written for practice, is in the style of the diary of a British diplomat, written in August 1939, describing the Anglo-French military delegation's slow journey to Moscow and its lack of authority to sign anything. How useful are Sources B and C to an historian studying why the USSR signed the Nazi-Soviet Pact? (12)

**Source B** shows the **official Soviet justification**: the pact as a guarantee of peace and a refusal to fight the West's war. Context supports part of this: Stalin believed Britain and France had tried to turn Hitler east at Munich, and he wanted time to rebuild the purged Red Army. But its **provenance** as Soviet state propaganda means it hides the **secret protocol**, which gave Stalin eastern Poland and the Baltic states. It is useful for the public reasons, not the full ones.

**Source C** helps explain **why the Western alternative failed**. Context confirms it: the delegation travelled by sea, had no power to sign, and could not overcome Poland's refusal to let Soviet troops in. As a private diary written at the time by an insider, it is likely to be honest and shows that British officials recognised the problem.

**Judgement.** Together they are very useful: C explains why Stalin lost faith in Britain and France, while B shows how he presented the pact. Neither reveals the territorial motive in the secret protocol, so the historian would need other evidence for that, but for the reasons Stalin chose Germany over the West they complement each other well. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Essay (16)
"Chamberlain was the person most responsible for the outbreak of war in 1939." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. (16)

**Chamberlain.** Appeasement made Hitler bolder: Munich (September 1938) handed him the Sudetenland and convinced him that Britain would never fight. Chamberlain's distrust of communism meant he excluded Stalin from Munich and handled the 1939 talks half-heartedly, sending a delegation with no power to sign. This pushed Stalin towards Hitler. His guarantee to Poland was impossible to enforce directly. In these ways he helped create the conditions for war.

**Stalin.** The Nazi-Soviet Pact (23 August 1939) was the immediate trigger: it freed Hitler from the threat of a two-front war, and its secret protocol showed Stalin sharing in the destruction of Poland, which he invaded on 17 September.

**Hitler.** But both reacted to Hitler. His aims of overturning Versailles and winning Lebensraum drove every crisis, from the Rhineland to Prague. He broke the Munich Agreement in March 1939, demanded Danzig, and chose to invade Poland on 1 September knowing Britain had guaranteed it.

**Judgement.** Chamberlain was not the most responsible. His mistakes made war more likely by encouraging Hitler and alienating Stalin, and Stalin's pact made it almost certain in 1939. But Hitler's aims and decisions were the driving force: without his determination to expand, appeasement and the pact would not have led to war. Chamberlain misjudged Hitler; Hitler caused the war. (Level 4.)
:::

- **Treating Chamberlain as only weak.** He did change policy in 1939, and kept his promise to Poland.
