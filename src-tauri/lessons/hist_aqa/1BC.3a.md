## What it is

This lesson covers the first two bullets of Part three. **The Berlin Wall**: the reasons for its construction and **Kennedy's response**. **Tensions over Cuba**: **Castro's revolution**, the **Bay of Pigs** and the **missile crisis**; the roles of **Castro, Khrushchev and Kennedy**; the **fears of the USA** and its reaction to missiles on Cuba; and the **dangers and results** of the crisis.

The big idea: in the early 1960s the Cold War reached its most dangerous point. In **Berlin** Khrushchev solved the problem of East Germans fleeing to the West by building a **wall** in August 1961; Kennedy protested but accepted it. In **Cuba**, a revolution on America's doorstep led to a failed American invasion and then to Khrushchev's gamble of placing **nuclear missiles** there. For thirteen days in October 1962 the world came closer to nuclear war than ever before. The way the crisis ended, and the fright it gave both sides, led to the first real steps to manage the nuclear danger.

### 1. Why the Berlin Wall was built

**The refugee problem**
- West Berlin, a Western-run island deep inside East Germany, was a **gateway to the West**. Once in West Berlin, East Germans could travel on to West Germany.
- Between 1949 and 1961 between two and three million people fled from East to West Germany, most of them through Berlin.
- Many were **young, skilled and educated**: doctors, engineers, teachers and skilled workers. This **"brain drain"** damaged East Germany's economy and was a huge **propaganda embarrassment**: people were voting with their feet against communism.
- West Berlin also showed off Western prosperity, and it was a base for **Western spying** in the Eastern bloc.

**Khrushchev's pressure on Berlin**
- In **November 1958** Khrushchev issued an **ultimatum**: the Western powers should leave West Berlin within six months and make it a "free city". The West refused, and the deadline passed during a series of talks.
- The **Paris summit** that was meant to settle Berlin collapsed over the **U2 incident** (May 1960).
- At the **Vienna summit (June 1961)** Khrushchev met the new, young President **Kennedy**, who had just been humiliated at the Bay of Pigs. Khrushchev renewed his demands and judged Kennedy weak.
- **Kennedy's reply:** he refused to give up West Berlin, increased defence spending and called up military reserves, saying the West would fight to defend West Berlin's freedom.
- Meanwhile the flood of refugees grew even larger in summer 1961 as East Germans feared the border might close.

**The Wall (August 1961)**
- During the night of **12–13 August 1961** East German troops and police sealed the border between East and West Berlin with **barbed wire**. Over the following days and months it was replaced by a **concrete wall**, with watchtowers, guards and a "death strip".
- Crossing points such as **Checkpoint Charlie** were tightly controlled. People who tried to escape were shot.
- In **October 1961**, after a dispute over the rights of Western officials to cross, **US and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie** before both withdrew.

**Why Khrushchev chose a wall**

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Stop the refugee flow | The brain drain threatened East Germany's survival |
| Avoid war | A wall sealed the border without attacking West Berlin, so the West had no reason to fight |
| Save face | Khrushchev had failed to force the West out of Berlin; a wall solved his problem another way |
| Stop spying | It made Western intelligence work in the East much harder |

### 2. Kennedy's response to the Wall

- Kennedy **protested** but did **not** try to remove the Wall: it was on East German territory and did not block Western access to West Berlin.
- Privately, Kennedy saw the Wall as better than a war: it showed that Khrushchev was not going to seize West Berlin.
- He sent **Vice-President Johnson** and **extra US troops** to West Berlin to reassure its people.
- In **June 1963** he visited West Berlin and, before a huge crowd, declared **"Ich bin ein Berliner"** ("I am a Berliner"), showing American solidarity with the city and condemning the Wall.

**Results of the Wall**

| For the East | For the West | For the Cold War |
|---|---|---|
| Stopped the refugee flow and stabilised East Germany | A propaganda gift: a communist state had to wall in its own people | Berlin became less of a flashpoint: the division was now fixed and both sides accepted it |
| Families were divided; people were killed trying to escape | West Berlin's freedom was preserved | The Wall became the **symbol** of the Iron Curtain and the Cold War |

### 3. Castro's revolution and the Bay of Pigs

**Cuba before 1959.** Cuba, only about **90 miles** from Florida, was ruled by the dictator **Batista**, who was backed by the USA. American companies owned much of the island's sugar, land, mines and utilities, and many Cubans lived in poverty.

**The revolution (1959).** **Fidel Castro** led a guerrilla war and took power in **January 1959**.
- He **nationalised** businesses and land, including American-owned property, and began land reform.
- The USA responded with **trade restrictions**, stopping imports of Cuban sugar, and later a full **embargo**.
- Castro turned to the **USSR**, which agreed to buy Cuban sugar and supply oil, machinery and weapons. In 1961 Castro declared himself a Marxist-Leninist.
- For the USA, a communist ally of the USSR in its own backyard was unacceptable.

**The Bay of Pigs (April 1961)**
- The **CIA**, under a plan prepared under Eisenhower, trained and armed about 1,400 **Cuban exiles** to invade Cuba and spark a rising against Castro.
- In **April 1961** they landed at the **Bay of Pigs**. Kennedy, who had approved the plan but wanted to hide American involvement, **refused direct US air support**.
- Castro's forces were well prepared; there was no popular rising, and the invaders were **killed or captured within days**.

| Consequences of the Bay of Pigs |
|---|
| A **humiliation** for Kennedy at the start of his presidency; Khrushchev concluded he was weak and inexperienced |
| Castro was strengthened at home and pushed **closer to the USSR**, asking for protection against another invasion |
| It helped persuade Khrushchev that Cuba needed defending, which led to the decision to send missiles |

### 4. The Cuban Missile Crisis, October 1962

**Why Khrushchev sent missiles**
- To **protect Cuba** from another American invasion.
- To **close the missile gap**, which in reality favoured the USA: the USA had far more long-range missiles and had placed **Jupiter missiles in Turkey**, on the Soviet border. Missiles in Cuba could reach most of the USA.
- To **test Kennedy**, whom he thought weak after the Bay of Pigs and Vienna.
- Possibly as a **bargaining chip** over Berlin or the Turkish missiles.

**US fears**
- Missiles in Cuba could hit **most major American cities** with only minutes of warning.
- It would change the **balance of power** and make a Soviet first strike more possible.
- **Political pressure:** after the Bay of Pigs and Vienna, Kennedy could not look weak again, especially with **congressional elections** due in November 1962.

**The thirteen days**

| Date (1962) | Event |
|---|---|
| **14 October** | A **U2 spy plane** photographed missile sites under construction in Cuba |
| **16 October** | Kennedy saw the photographs. He set up a group of advisers, **ExComm**, to consider the options |
| 16–21 October | ExComm debated: do nothing; diplomacy; an **air strike** on the sites; a full **invasion**; or a **naval blockade**. Military leaders favoured air strikes, but these might not destroy every missile and would kill Soviet personnel |
| **22 October** | Kennedy announced on **television** that there were missiles in Cuba and that the US Navy would impose a **"quarantine"** (blockade) to stop more weapons reaching the island. He demanded their removal |
| **24 October** | Soviet ships carrying weapons approached the quarantine line, then **stopped and turned back**. But work on the sites already in Cuba continued |
| **26 October** | Khrushchev sent a letter offering to **remove the missiles if the USA promised not to invade Cuba** |
| **27 October** | A second, tougher letter demanded the removal of the **US missiles in Turkey** as well. The same day a **U2 was shot down over Cuba** and its pilot killed. Military pressure for an attack grew. This was the most dangerous day |
| 27 October (evening) | Kennedy publicly **accepted the first letter's terms**. His brother **Robert Kennedy** secretly told the Soviet ambassador that the **Jupiter missiles in Turkey would be removed** later, provided this was kept secret |
| **28 October** | Khrushchev announced that the missiles would be **dismantled** and returned to the USSR |

**The roles of the three leaders**

| Leader | Role |
|---|---|
| **Kennedy** | Rejected the generals' call for air strikes and chose the more cautious **blockade**, giving Khrushchev time to back down; ignored the second letter and made a **secret concession** on Turkey. He emerged looking strong and calm |
| **Khrushchev** | Took the reckless gamble of placing missiles, but **backed down** rather than risk nuclear war. He gained a no-invasion promise and the removal of the Jupiters, but the Turkish deal was secret, so he appeared to have **lost** |
| **Castro** | His revolution and fear of invasion started the chain of events. He accepted the missiles and urged a hard line, but he was **not consulted** about the final deal and was furious. Cuba stayed communist and protected from invasion |

### 5. Dangers and results of the crisis

**The dangers**
- Both sides had nuclear weapons on high alert; a single mistake, such as the U2 shot down on 27 October or an incident at sea, could have triggered war.
- Military commanders on both sides pressed for force; leaders could not fully control events.
- Communication between Washington and Moscow was slow: letters took hours to arrive and be translated.

**The results**

| Result | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **The hotline (1963)** | A direct telephone and teleprinter link between the White House and the Kremlin, so leaders could communicate quickly in a crisis |
| **The Limited Test Ban Treaty (1963)** | The USA, the USSR and Britain agreed to **ban nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in outer space and under water** (underground tests continued). The first agreement to control nuclear weapons |
| Later agreements | The Outer Space Treaty (1967) and the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968) continued the process |
| **Kennedy** | His reputation rose: he had stood firm and won the public argument |
| **Khrushchev** | Seen as having backed down; this contributed to his removal from power in **1964** |
| **Castro** | Kept his revolution and a guarantee against invasion, but was angry at being sidelined |
| **The USSR** | Determined never to be humiliated again, it built up its nuclear forces, continuing the arms race |
| **Relations** | Both sides recognised how close they had come to disaster. This "**fright**" helped lead towards **détente** later in the decade |

**Who won?** Kennedy won the **public** victory: the missiles were withdrawn. Khrushchev won real **concessions**: no invasion of Cuba and the Jupiters out of Turkey. Arguably the **world** won, because both men chose to step back.

::: terms
- **Brain drain**: the loss of skilled and educated people, such as East Germans fleeing to the West before 1961.
- **Berlin Wall**: the barrier built from 13 August 1961 to seal off West Berlin from East Germany.
- **Checkpoint Charlie**: the best-known crossing point in the Berlin Wall, where US and Soviet tanks faced each other in October 1961.
- **Vienna summit**: the June 1961 meeting of Kennedy and Khrushchev, at which Khrushchev renewed his demands over Berlin.
- **Ich bin ein Berliner**: Kennedy's declaration of solidarity with West Berlin, made in the city in June 1963.
- **Nationalisation**: taking businesses and land into state ownership, as Castro did with American property in Cuba.
- **Embargo**: a ban on trade with a country, such as the US ban on trade with Cuba.
- **Bay of Pigs**: the failed invasion of Cuba by CIA-trained exiles in April 1961.
- **ExComm**: the group of advisers Kennedy set up to deal with the Cuban Missile Crisis.
- **Quarantine**: Kennedy's term for the naval blockade of Cuba announced on 22 October 1962.
- **Jupiter missiles**: US nuclear missiles based in Turkey, secretly removed as part of the deal ending the crisis.
- **Hotline**: the direct communication link between the White House and the Kremlin, set up in 1963.
- **Limited Test Ban Treaty**: the 1963 agreement banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water.
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962. (8)

The crisis grew out of **Castro's revolution** in 1959 and the failed **Bay of Pigs** invasion of April 1961. **Because** the USA had tried to overthrow him, Castro asked the USSR for protection, and Khrushchev, who also wanted to balance the US Jupiter missiles in Turkey, secretly sent **nuclear missiles** to Cuba.

On **14 October** a U2 plane photographed the missile sites. **This created** a crisis, because missiles only 90 miles from Florida could hit most American cities. Kennedy and **ExComm** rejected an air strike, which might not destroy every missile and could start a war, and **instead** on 22 October announced a **naval quarantine** of Cuba on television.

On 24 October Soviet ships approaching the blockade **turned back**, but work on the sites continued. Khrushchev then sent **two letters**: the first offered to remove the missiles if the USA promised not to invade Cuba; the second also demanded the removal of the missiles in Turkey. On **27 October** a U2 was shot down over Cuba, and war seemed close. Kennedy publicly accepted the first letter, **while** Robert Kennedy secretly promised the Turkish missiles would go. **As a result**, on **28 October** Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles.

**The consequences** were a hotline between Washington and Moscow and the Limited Test Ban Treaty of 1963, as both sides recognised how close they had come to nuclear war. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Two sources (12)
Source B, written for this practice question, is in the style of Kennedy's television broadcast to the American people on 22 October 1962, announcing that offensive missiles had been found in Cuba, that a strict quarantine would be imposed, and that any missile fired from Cuba would be treated as an attack by the Soviet Union. Source C, also written for practice, is in the style of a letter from Khrushchev to Kennedy on 27 October 1962, pointing out that the USA has missiles in Turkey right next to the Soviet Union and asking why the USSR may not do the same in Cuba. How useful are Sources B and C to an historian studying the Cuban Missile Crisis? (12)

**Source B** shows the **American position**: the discovery of the missiles, the choice of a blockade rather than an air strike, and the warning that the USSR would be held responsible. Context confirms the U2 photographs of 14 October and the ExComm debates. Its **provenance** is valuable: a public speech by the President himself, meant to rally Americans and to warn Khrushchev, so it shows the official US case. It does not reveal Kennedy's caution or his later willingness to compromise.

**Source C** shows the **Soviet argument** and the turning point of the crisis. Context: this was Khrushchev's second, tougher letter, sent on the most dangerous day, when a U2 was shot down. Its point about Turkey was valid: the Jupiters were there, and they were removed in the secret deal. As a private letter between leaders, it shows Khrushchev's bargaining position.

**Judgement.** Together they are very useful because they show both sides' cases at the height of the crisis. C is perhaps more revealing, because it explains why the deal included Turkey, which the public in 1962 never knew. B shows the public face of the crisis, C its hidden diplomacy. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Essay (16)
"Khrushchev was the main loser of the Cuban Missile Crisis." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. (16)

**Khrushchev as loser.** He had to withdraw the missiles in full view of the world, and because the Turkish deal was kept secret he appeared to have backed down under American pressure. His gamble had failed to close the missile gap. Soviet hardliners saw it as a humiliation, and it contributed to his removal in 1964.

**Khrushchev's gains.** But he achieved real aims. Kennedy promised not to invade Cuba, so the communist revolution was protected, which had been one of the main reasons for sending the missiles. The Jupiter missiles were removed from Turkey in 1963. The hotline and Test Ban Treaty reduced the danger of war.

**Castro.** Castro arguably lost more in status: he was not consulted about the deal, his island had been used as a bargaining chip, and he was furious. Yet his regime survived.

**Kennedy.** Kennedy won a public triumph, but he had to give a no-invasion pledge and secretly remove US missiles, so his victory was not complete.

**Judgement.** Khrushchev was the main loser in terms of **image and power**: the world believed he had backed down, and it cost him his position. But in terms of **real gains** he did better than he appeared, and Castro was the one most ignored. The crisis is best seen as a compromise that both superpowers presented as a victory, except that only Kennedy was able to. (Level 4.)
