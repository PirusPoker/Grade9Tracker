## What it is

This lesson covers the first two bullets of Part two: **the League of Nations**, its **formation and covenant**, **organisation**, **membership and how it changed**, **powers**, the work of its **agencies**, and its **contribution to peace in the 1920s**, including successes and failures such as the **Aaland Islands, Upper Silesia, Vilna, Corfu and Bulgaria**; and **diplomacy outside the League**: the **Locarno treaties** and the **Kellogg-Briand Pact**.

The big idea: the League was a new experiment in keeping peace by **collective security**: nations agreeing to act together against an aggressor. In the 1920s it solved disputes between small states and did valuable humanitarian work, but it backed down when a great power (Italy at Corfu) or a powerful ally (Poland at Vilna) defied it. The biggest steps towards peace in the late 1920s were taken by agreements made outside it.

### 1. Formation and covenant

- The League was **Wilson's idea** (the last of his Fourteen Points). Its **Covenant** (rules) was written into all the peace treaties of 1919–20, and it began work in **January 1920**, with its headquarters in **Geneva**, Switzerland.
- **Aims:** to prevent war through **collective security**; to encourage **disarmament**; to settle disputes by discussion and arbitration; to improve people's living and working conditions.
- Key articles: members promised to respect and preserve each other's territory and independence (**Article 10**); members would take action, starting with economic sanctions, against any member that went to war in breach of the Covenant (**Article 16**).

### 2. Organisation

| Body | Who and how | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Assembly** | Every member had one vote; met **once a year**; discussed general policy, the budget and new members | Decisions had to be **unanimous**, which was slow and difficult |
| **Council** | Smaller; met several times a year and in emergencies. **Permanent members** at first: **Britain, France, Italy and Japan** (Germany added in 1926), plus temporary members elected by the Assembly | Each permanent member had a **veto**; the great powers put their own interests first |
| **Secretariat** | The League's civil service, keeping records and organising its work | Often understaffed for the huge range of tasks |
| **Permanent Court of International Justice** | Judges at **The Hague** in the Netherlands, settling legal disputes between states | Could only give rulings; it had **no way to enforce** them |
| **Commissions and committees** (agencies) | Specialist bodies such as the ILO and the Health Organisation (see below) | Depended on members' money and cooperation |

### 3. Membership and how it changed

- **42 states** at the start; around 60 at its peak.
- The **USA never joined**: the Senate refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles. Without the world's richest nation, economic sanctions were weakened because members could still trade with America.
- **Germany** was not allowed in at first; it joined in **1926** (after Locarno) and left in **1933** under Hitler.
- **The USSR** was not invited (it was communist and feared); it joined only in **1934** and was **expelled in 1939** after attacking Finland.
- **Japan** left in **1933** and **Italy** in **1937**, both after being criticised for invasions.
- So the League was dominated by **Britain and France**, who had their own priorities: Britain its empire and trade, France its security against Germany.

### 4. Powers

The League had no army of its own. It could act in three stages:
1. **Moral persuasion** (verbal sanctions): condemn an aggressor and tell it to stop.
2. **Economic sanctions**: members stop trading with the aggressor.
3. **Military force**: members could, in theory, combine their forces, but no member was obliged to supply troops.

The weakness: each stage depended on members, especially Britain and France, being **willing** to act, and on the aggressor caring about world opinion.

### 5. The work of the agencies

| Agency | What it did in the 1920s and 1930s |
|---|---|
| **International Labour Organisation (ILO)** | Brought together governments, employers and workers to improve working conditions; campaigned for limits on working hours and on child labour and for safer workplaces. Many members did not adopt its recommendations because of the cost |
| **Health Organisation** | Worked to fight diseases such as leprosy and malaria; spread information on public health. Its work later continued in the World Health Organisation |
| **Refugees Committee** | Under **Fridtjof Nansen**, helped return hundreds of thousands of former prisoners of war home after the war, and helped refugees, for example from Turkey in the early 1920s; "Nansen passports" gave stateless people identity documents |
| **Slavery Commission** | Worked to end slavery and forced labour, for example helping to free slaves in Sierra Leone |
| **Mandates Commission** | Supervised how Britain, France and others ran the former colonies of Germany and Turkey |

The agencies are often seen as the League's **most lasting success**, because their work did not depend on great powers risking war.

### 6. The League and disputes in the 1920s

| Dispute | What happened | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Vilna (1920)** | Vilna was the capital of the new state of **Lithuania**, but many Poles lived there. A Polish army seized it. Lithuania appealed; the League asked Poland to withdraw. Poland refused | **Failure.** Britain and France would not act against Poland, which they saw as an ally against Germany and Russia. Poland kept Vilna |
| **Aaland Islands (1921)** | Both **Sweden** and **Finland** claimed these islands; most islanders were Swedish speakers. Both sides asked the League to decide | **Success.** The League gave them to **Finland**, with guarantees that they would not be fortified. Sweden accepted |
| **Upper Silesia (1921)** | An industrial region claimed by **Germany** and **Poland**. A **plebiscite** was held; there was violence between the communities | **Success.** The League divided the region: Germany got more of the land and people, Poland most of the industry. Both accepted, though Germans resented it |
| **Corfu (1923)** | An Italian general, **Tellini**, was murdered while mapping the Greek-Albanian border. **Mussolini** blamed Greece, demanded compensation, then bombarded and **occupied Corfu**. Greece appealed to the League | **Failure.** The League proposed that Greece pay compensation through the League, but Mussolini went to the **Conference of Ambassadors** (an Allied body outside the League), which made Greece apologise and **pay Italy directly**. Italy then left Corfu. A great power had bullied a small one and got its way |
| **Bulgaria (1925)** | After soldiers were killed in a border incident, **Greece invaded Bulgaria**. Bulgaria appealed | **Success.** The League condemned the invasion and ordered Greece to withdraw and pay compensation; Greece obeyed. But Greeks pointed out that Italy had been treated differently over Corfu |

**The pattern:** the League succeeded when the states involved were **small**, wanted a peaceful answer, or could be pressured by Britain and France. It failed when a **great power** or a state that Britain and France needed was the aggressor.

### 7. Diplomacy outside the League

**The Locarno treaties (1925)**
- Signed by **Germany, France, Belgium, Britain and Italy**.
- Germany **accepted its western borders** as fixed by Versailles, including the demilitarised Rhineland; Britain and Italy **guaranteed** them.
- Germany agreed to settle disputes over its **eastern borders** with Poland and Czechoslovakia by arbitration, but did **not** accept them as permanent.
- Created the **"spirit of Locarno"**, a mood of optimism and cooperation, and led to Germany **joining the League in 1926** with a permanent seat on the Council.
- **Debate:** it improved relations, but it was agreed **outside** the League, suggesting the great powers did not trust it; and leaving the eastern borders open hinted that they might one day change.

**The Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928)**
- Drawn up by the French foreign minister **Aristide Briand** and the US Secretary of State **Frank Kellogg**.
- Signatories promised to **renounce war** as a way of settling disputes ("as an instrument of national policy"). It was eventually signed by over 60 countries, **including the USA**, Germany, Japan and Italy.
- **Weakness:** it had **no means of enforcement**, and it allowed wars of self-defence, so aggressors could claim they were defending themselves. Japan and Italy, both signatories, invaded other countries in the 1930s.

### 8. Verdict on the 1920s

| Success | Limits |
|---|---|
| Settled the Aaland Islands, Upper Silesia and Bulgaria disputes | Failed over Vilna and Corfu, where its members' interests or a great power were involved |
| Agencies helped refugees, prisoners of war, workers and the sick | Never used military force; sanctions untested against a major power |
| By 1926 Germany was a member and relations were better than at any time since 1914 | The most important agreements (Locarno, Kellogg-Briand) were made outside the League |
| No major war between great powers | Disarmament made little progress; the USA and USSR were outside |

Many historians argue the 1920s were a success mainly because **no great power was yet determined on aggression**; the League's weaknesses were hidden rather than solved.

::: terms
- **League of Nations**: the international organisation set up in 1920 to keep peace and encourage cooperation, based in Geneva.
- **Covenant**: the set of rules of the League, written into the peace treaties of 1919–20.
- **Collective security**: the idea that member states act together to protect any member attacked by an aggressor.
- **Assembly**: the League's parliament, in which every member had a vote; it met once a year and needed unanimous decisions.
- **Council**: the smaller League body with permanent members such as Britain and France, which dealt with crises and had a veto.
- **Permanent Court of International Justice**: the League's court at The Hague, which judged disputes between states but could not enforce its rulings.
- **Economic sanctions**: measures such as stopping trade, used to pressure an aggressor without fighting.
- **Moral persuasion**: using world opinion and condemnation to pressure a state to change its behaviour.
- **International Labour Organisation (ILO)**: the League agency that worked to improve working conditions around the world.
- **Conference of Ambassadors**: a body of Allied diplomats, outside the League, that overruled it in the Corfu crisis of 1923.
- **Locarno treaties**: the 1925 agreements in which Germany accepted its western borders, leading to its entry into the League in 1926.
- **Kellogg-Briand Pact**: the 1928 agreement in which over 60 states promised to renounce war, with no means of enforcement.
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of how the League of Nations dealt with the Corfu crisis of 1923. (8)

The crisis began when the Italian general **Tellini**, working for the Conference of Ambassadors to map the border between Greece and Albania, was murdered on Greek soil. **Because** Mussolini wanted to show Italian strength, he blamed Greece and demanded a huge compensation payment and an apology.

When Greece refused to accept all his demands, Mussolini **bombarded and occupied the Greek island of Corfu**, killing civilians. **As a result**, Greece appealed to the League, and the Council considered the case.

The League condemned Italy's action and proposed that Greece pay compensation to the League, to be handed over once Tellini's killers were found. **However**, Mussolini refused to accept the League's authority and pressed the **Conference of Ambassadors**, a body outside the League, to decide instead. **This led to** Greece being made to apologise and pay compensation directly to Italy, after which Italy left Corfu.

**The consequence** was serious damage to the League's reputation: a great power had used force against a small state and been rewarded, showing that Britain and France would not stand up to Italy. This contrasted with the Bulgaria crisis of 1925, when Greece was made to withdraw, suggesting the League applied one rule to the weak and another to the strong. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Two sources (12)
Source B, written for this practice question, is a description of a poster published by the League of Nations in 1928. It shows workers, doctors and children from many countries gathered around a globe, with the caption "The League works for all peoples." Source C, also written for practice, is a description of a British cartoon from 1923, showing a fierce Mussolini standing on a map of Corfu while a small figure labelled "League" timidly holds up a sign saying "Please". How useful are Sources B and C to an historian studying the League of Nations? (12)

**Source B** shows the League's **humanitarian work**: the ILO's work on conditions, the Health Organisation's campaigns against disease, and Nansen's help for refugees and prisoners of war. Context supports this: the agencies are widely seen as the League's most lasting achievement. Its **provenance** limits it: it is the League's own **propaganda**, made to build support in 1928, at the height of the optimism after Locarno and Germany's entry, so it presents the League only positively. That still makes it useful as evidence of what the League **wanted** people to believe about itself.

**Source C** shows the League's **weakness against a great power**. In 1923 Mussolini occupied Corfu after the murder of Tellini, and the Conference of Ambassadors, not the League, settled the crisis in Italy's favour. As a British cartoon from the time, it shows that even in a leading member state people saw the League as feeble, which is significant because Britain was meant to be its strongest supporter.

**Judgement.** Together they are very useful because they show the League's two sides in the 1920s: real success in welfare and weakness in security against major powers. C is more useful about peacekeeping, the League's central purpose, while B is useful for its aims and image. (Level 4.)

::: example Q4 Essay (16)
"The League of Nations was a success in the 1920s." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. (16)

**For.** The League settled several disputes peacefully: the Aaland Islands (1921) went to Finland and Sweden accepted; Upper Silesia (1921) was divided after a plebiscite; in Bulgaria (1925) Greece withdrew when ordered. Its agencies achieved a great deal: Nansen's work returned hundreds of thousands of prisoners of war, the Health Organisation fought disease, and the ILO pressed for better conditions. By 1926 Germany had joined, suggesting the League was becoming the centre of world diplomacy.

**Against.** When its members' interests were involved it failed: Poland kept Vilna (1920) because Britain and France would not act against an ally, and Mussolini got his way over Corfu (1923) through the Conference of Ambassadors. The most important agreements, Locarno (1925) and Kellogg-Briand (1928), were made outside the League, suggesting the great powers preferred their own diplomacy. Disarmament made little progress, and the USA never joined.

**Judgement.** The League was a limited success. It did well when disputes involved small states willing to accept its decisions, and its humanitarian work was outstanding. But it never faced a determined great power and won; at Corfu it backed down. Its success in the 1920s depended on the absence of serious aggression, which is why it collapsed in the 1930s when Japan and Italy challenged it. (Level 4: sustained judgement.)
