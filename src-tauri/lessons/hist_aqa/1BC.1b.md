## What it is

This lesson covers the second bullet of Part one, **the Iron Curtain and the evolution of East-West rivalry**: **Soviet expansion in Eastern Europe**; **US policies**; the **Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan**, their purpose and **Stalin's reaction**; **Cominform**; **Comecon**; **Yugoslavia**; and the **Berlin Blockade and Airlift**.

The big idea: between 1945 and 1949 Europe split into two hostile blocs. Stalin turned the countries the Red Army occupied into communist states loyal to Moscow. The USA answered with **containment**: the Truman Doctrine promised political and military support against communism, and the Marshall Plan supplied the money. Stalin saw this as American economic imperialism and tightened his grip through Cominform and Comecon. The struggle came to a head over **Berlin**, where the blockade and airlift of 1948–49 led directly to two separate German states and the NATO alliance.

### 1. Soviet expansion in Eastern Europe, 1945–48

After the war the Red Army occupied most of Eastern Europe. Stalin wanted a **buffer zone** of friendly states. Over three years, local communists, backed by Soviet troops and advisers, took power, often using **"salami tactics"**: slicing away opponents one piece at a time.

**The typical pattern**
1. A **coalition** government of several parties, including communists.
2. Communists took control of key ministries, especially the **interior ministry** and the **police**.
3. Opponents were accused of being fascists or traitors, arrested, exiled or frightened into silence.
4. Rigged **elections** gave communists control; other parties were merged with them or banned.
5. A **one-party state** loyal to Moscow.

| Country | How communists took control |
|---|---|
| **Poland** | The Soviet-backed communist government from the war took power. Non-communist leaders were arrested or harassed, and the 1947 elections were rigged; the main opposition leader fled abroad |
| **Hungary** | Communists lost the 1945 election but held the interior ministry and the police, which they used to break up the larger parties. By 1947–49 Hungary was a communist state |
| **Romania** and **Bulgaria** | Communist-led governments formed with Soviet backing from 1945; opponents were executed or imprisoned, and the monarchies were abolished |
| **Czechoslovakia** | The last to fall. Communists were the largest party after the 1946 elections but shared power. In **February 1948** they seized full control in a **coup**; the non-communist foreign minister, Jan Masaryk, was found dead soon after |
| **East Germany** | The Soviet zone of Germany, run on communist lines |

The **Western view:** this broke the Yalta promise of free elections and showed that Stalin aimed to spread communism. The **Soviet view:** these were defensive measures to protect the USSR and the result of popular support for communists who had resisted the Nazis.

### 2. The Iron Curtain speech, March 1946

- Churchill, no longer Prime Minister, spoke at **Fulton, Missouri**, in the USA, with Truman present, in **March 1946**.
- He said an **"iron curtain"** had come down across Europe, from the Baltic to the Adriatic, cutting off the Soviet-controlled East.
- He called for Britain and the USA to stand together against Soviet expansion.
- **Stalin** called Churchill a **warmonger** and compared him to Hitler. The phrase "Iron Curtain" became the name for the division of Europe.

### 3. US policies: containment

In the USA, officials increasingly argued that the USSR was expansionist and would only respond to firmness. This led to the policy of **containment**: stopping communism from spreading beyond the areas where it already ruled.

**The Truman Doctrine (March 1947)**
- **Trigger:** in **Greece** a civil war was being fought between the government and communist guerrillas. Britain had been supporting the Greek government but announced in early 1947 that it could **no longer afford** to. Turkey was also under Soviet pressure.
- Truman asked Congress for **400 million dollars** in aid for **Greece and Turkey**.
- He declared that the USA would **support free peoples** resisting attempts by armed minorities or outside powers to take them over.
- **Significance:** the USA abandoned **isolationism** and committed itself to opposing communism anywhere in the world. The world was presented as a choice between two ways of life.
- **Result in Greece:** with American aid, the government defeated the communists by 1949.

**The Marshall Plan (announced June 1947)**
- Announced by the US Secretary of State **George Marshall**; officially the **European Recovery Programme**.
- **Why:** Europe was devastated, short of food and fuel after a severe winter (1946–47), and in debt. Communist parties were strong in countries such as **France and Italy**. Americans believed that **poverty bred communism**, so a prosperous Europe would resist it. A recovered Europe would also buy **American goods**.
- **What:** about **13 billion dollars** in aid (money, food, machinery and goods) to **16** Western European countries from 1948 to 1952. Aid was offered to all of Europe, including the USSR and Eastern Europe.
- **Results:** Western Europe recovered rapidly; communist support fell in the West; Europe was divided economically as well as politically.

**Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan: keep them distinct**

| | Truman Doctrine | Marshall Plan |
|---|---|---|
| Type | **Political and military** promise | **Economic** aid |
| Date | March 1947 | Announced June 1947; aid 1948–52 |
| First targets | Greece and Turkey | All of Europe offered; 16 Western countries took it |
| Aim | Contain communism by supporting governments under threat | Contain communism by removing the poverty that fed it |
| A common way to put it | The promise | The money that carried the promise out ("two halves of the same walnut") |

### 4. Stalin's reaction

- Stalin called the Marshall Plan **"dollar imperialism"**: a way for the USA to gain economic control of Europe and turn it against the USSR.
- The Soviet foreign minister **Molotov** walked out of the conference in Paris that discussed the plan (July 1947).
- Stalin **forbade** the Eastern European states to accept aid, even though **Czechoslovakia and Poland** had shown interest. This helped convince Stalin to complete communist control of Czechoslovakia in 1948.

**Cominform (1947)**
- The **Communist Information Bureau**, set up in **September 1947**.
- It brought together the communist parties of Eastern Europe, plus those of **France and Italy**, under Soviet direction.
- **Purpose:** to coordinate their policies, ensure they followed Moscow's line, spread propaganda against the Marshall Plan, and root out disloyalty.

**Comecon (1949)**
- The **Council for Mutual Economic Assistance**, set up in **January 1949**.
- The Soviet answer to the Marshall Plan: it was meant to coordinate trade and economic development among the Eastern bloc states.
- **In practice** it tied their economies to the USSR, often on terms that benefited the Soviet Union, and kept them out of Western trade.

### 5. Yugoslavia: the exception

- **Tito**, leader of the communist partisans, had **liberated Yugoslavia largely by his own forces**, not by the Red Army. He therefore had his own power base and popular support.
- Tito was a convinced communist but wanted to run Yugoslavia **his own way**: he resisted Soviet control of the economy and the army and had plans for a Balkan federation.
- In **1948** Stalin had Yugoslavia **expelled from Cominform**. He tried economic pressure and plots to remove Tito, but failed.
- Tito accepted some **Western aid** and stayed independent of both blocs.
- **Significance:** it showed communism was not one united movement, and it made Stalin even more determined to control the rest of Eastern Europe tightly. Communist leaders elsewhere suspected of **"Titoism"** were purged.

### 6. The Berlin Blockade and Airlift, 1948–49

**Causes**

| Cause | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Different aims for Germany** | The USSR wanted Germany weak and paying reparations; the West wanted it to **recover**, partly to reduce the cost of feeding it and to build a barrier against communism |
| **Bizonia and Trizonia** | Britain and the USA merged their zones economically in **1947** (**Bizonia**); France joined in 1948. Stalin saw a Western German state being created |
| **Marshall Aid** | The western zones received aid and recovered; the Soviet zone was stripped for reparations, so the contrast grew |
| **The new currency (June 1948)** | The West introduced a new currency, the **Deutschmark**, in its zones and then in West Berlin, without agreement. The USSR saw this as a threat to its zone's economy |
| **West Berlin itself** | A capitalist island deep inside the Soviet zone: a shop window for Western prosperity and a base for spying |

**The Blockade (from June 1948)**
- Stalin **cut all road, rail and canal links** between the western zones and West Berlin. About two million West Berliners had only weeks of food and fuel.
- His aim was probably to force the West to **abandon West Berlin**, or to drop plans for a West German state.

**The Airlift**
- The West refused to withdraw but did not want to force a way through and risk war. Instead, British and American aircraft flew in **food, coal and other supplies** to West Berlin's airports.
- At its height a plane landed every few minutes, and thousands of tons of supplies arrived each day. Over the whole airlift more than two million tons were delivered.
- Stalin did **not shoot down** the planes, which would have meant war. In **May 1949** he **lifted the blockade**.

**Results**

| Result | Significance |
|---|---|
| **West Berlin stayed in Western hands** | A **propaganda victory** for the West; the USA showed it would defend its interests in Europe. Stalin had backed down |
| **NATO (April 1949)** | The Western powers formed a military alliance, committing the USA to the defence of Western Europe |
| **Two German states (1949)** | The **Federal Republic of Germany** (West Germany) was set up in 1949, followed by the **German Democratic Republic** (East Germany) in October 1949. Germany was now formally divided |
| **Berlin a flashpoint** | It remained the focus of East-West tension and would lead to the Berlin Wall in 1961 |

::: terms
- **Iron Curtain**: Churchill's 1946 phrase for the division of Europe between the communist East and the democratic West.
- **Salami tactics**: the step-by-step removal of opponents by which communists took power in Eastern Europe.
- **Satellite state**: a country officially independent but under the control of the USSR.
- **Containment**: the US policy of stopping communism from spreading to new countries.
- **Truman Doctrine**: the 1947 promise that the USA would support free peoples resisting communist takeover, starting with Greece and Turkey.
- **Marshall Plan**: the European Recovery Programme of about 13 billion dollars of US aid to rebuild Europe, 1948–52.
- **Dollar imperialism**: Stalin's term for the Marshall Plan, which he saw as a way for the USA to control Europe through money.
- **Cominform**: the Communist Information Bureau, set up in 1947 to coordinate communist parties under Soviet control.
- **Comecon**: the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, set up in 1949 to coordinate the economies of the Eastern bloc.
- **Titoism**: following an independent communist path, as Tito did in Yugoslavia after the split with Stalin in 1948.
- **Berlin Blockade**: the Soviet closure of all land and canal routes to West Berlin, from June 1948 to May 1949.
- **Berlin Airlift**: the supply of West Berlin by British and American aircraft during the blockade.
:::

::: example Q3 Account (8)
Write an account of how the crisis over Berlin developed in 1948–49. (8)

The crisis grew out of **disagreement over Germany's future**. The USSR wanted Germany kept weak, while Britain and the USA wanted it to recover, **so** they merged their zones into Bizonia in 1947 and poured in Marshall Aid. Stalin feared a strong Western German state being built on his border.

**This came to a head** in June 1948 when the West introduced a **new currency**, the Deutschmark, in its zones and in West Berlin. Stalin saw this as an attack on the economy of the Soviet zone. **In response** he **cut all road, rail and canal links** to West Berlin, hoping to starve the West into leaving the city.

The West refused to give up West Berlin but did not want to risk war by forcing a way through. **Instead**, British and American planes began the **Airlift**, flying in food and coal. **Because** shooting down the planes would have meant war, Stalin could not stop them, and over the following months thousands of tons arrived every day. In **May 1949** Stalin **lifted the blockade**.

**The consequences** were far-reaching. The West had won a major propaganda victory. The Western powers formed **NATO** in April 1949, and Germany was formally divided when the Federal Republic and then the German Democratic Republic were created in 1949. Berlin remained a flashpoint of the Cold War. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q2 Two sources (12)
Source B, written for this practice question, is a description of an American poster from 1948 promoting the Marshall Plan. It shows a ship loaded with grain and machinery sailing towards a smiling crowd in a rebuilt European town, with the caption "Prosperity makes us free". Source C, also written for practice, is in the style of a speech by a Soviet official at the United Nations in 1947, claiming that the USA is using its dollars to divide Europe and to interfere in the affairs of independent states. How useful are Sources B and C to an historian studying the Marshall Plan? (12)

**Source B** shows the **American purpose** behind Marshall Aid: food and machinery to rebuild Europe, and the belief that prosperity would protect freedom against communism. Context supports this: Europe was devastated after the harsh winter of 1946–47, and communist parties were strong in France and Italy. Its **provenance** as American publicity in 1948, when aid was starting, means it shows how the USA wanted the plan understood. It hides the economic advantage to the USA of creating markets for American goods.

**Source C** shows the **Soviet reaction**: the plan as dollar imperialism dividing Europe. Context supports part of this: aid did help divide Europe into two economic blocs, and Molotov walked out of the Paris talks. As a speech at the UN, it was propaganda aimed at world opinion and at Western Europeans, and it ignores the fact that aid was offered to the East too, but Stalin refused.

**Judgement.** Together they are very useful because they show the two rival interpretations of the plan at the time: generous recovery aid or a weapon of the Cold War. In fact it was both, which is exactly what the two sources reveal when read together. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Essay (16)
"The USSR was more to blame than the USA for the development of the Cold War between 1945 and 1949." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. (16)

**The USSR.** Stalin broke the spirit of the Yalta promise of free elections by imposing communist governments in Poland, Hungary, Romania and Bulgaria through salami tactics, and in February 1948 communists seized power in Czechoslovakia. Cominform (1947) bound communist parties to Moscow, and Stalin's Berlin Blockade (1948) was an aggressive attempt to force the West out of the city, which could have led to war.

**The USA.** But US policy also raised tension. The Truman Doctrine (1947) divided the world into two camps and committed the USA to opposing communism everywhere, and the Marshall Plan, though generous, created an economic bloc that Stalin saw as dollar imperialism. The Western decision to merge zones and introduce a new currency in 1948 was taken without the USSR and provoked the Blockade.

**Interaction.** Each side's actions confirmed the other's fears. Soviet expansion produced containment; the Marshall Plan produced Cominform and Comecon; Western plans for Germany produced the Blockade, which produced NATO and two German states.

**Judgement.** The USSR was more to blame, because its takeover of Eastern Europe came first and made the West fear further expansion. Yet the USA's response turned a regional dispute into a global confrontation. Stalin acted from a genuine fear for Soviet security, but his methods of control made Western fears seem justified. (Level 4.)

::: watch

- **Mixing up Cominform and Comecon.** Cominform (1947) was political, controlling communist parties; Comecon (1949) was economic, the answer to Marshall Aid.
- **Saying the Blockade began because of the Airlift.** The Blockade (June 1948) came first; the Airlift was the response.
- **Forgetting Yugoslavia.** The spec names it; use it to show the limits of Soviet control and Stalin's response.
- **Saying Churchill was Prime Minister in 1946.** He gave the Iron Curtain speech as leader of the opposition, in the USA.
