## What it is

This lesson covers the third bullet of **Part three: A revolution in medicine**: **public health in industrial Britain** (c1800 to c1900). It covers the problems of the new industrial towns, the **cholera epidemics**, the reformers **Edwin Chadwick** and **John Snow**, the **Great Stink** and **Bazalgette's sewers**, and the shift in government from doing almost nothing to the compulsory **1875 Public Health Act**.

The big idea: in 1800 the government believed in **laissez-faire**: it was not the state's job to clean towns or provide water. By 1875 Parliament **forced** local councils to do it. That is one of the biggest changes in the whole health theme. It happened because several factors **worked together**: deadly epidemics, reformers' evidence, new science, engineering, and working men getting the vote.

### 1. Problems of industrial towns

The **Industrial Revolution** drew huge numbers of people into towns such as Manchester, Leeds and Liverpool, and into London, far faster than anyone planned for.

| Problem | Details |
|---|---|
| **Housing** | Cheap **back-to-back** houses built by speculators, with no through-ventilation; whole families in one room; damp **cellar dwellings** |
| **Sanitation** | Shared **privies** (outdoor toilets) for many households; **cesspits** that overflowed and were emptied by hand; waste thrown into streets and rivers |
| **Water** | Drawn from **rivers**, shared **pumps** or wells, often polluted by sewage; private water companies piped it to wealthier streets only, sometimes for a few hours a day |
| **Environment** | Factory smoke; slaughterhouses and rubbish heaps; long hours of dangerous work and poor diet |

The results were high death rates, especially among **infants** and the **working class**. Diseases such as **typhus**, **typhoid**, **tuberculosis** and **smallpox** were common. People still mostly blamed **miasma** (bad air from filth), and nobody yet knew that germs carried disease.

**Why government did little:** belief in **laissez-faire**; ratepayers did not want higher **rates** (local taxes); water companies and landlords resisted; local government was weak; and most working men had **no vote**, so MPs could ignore them.

### 2. Cholera epidemics

**Cholera** arrived in Britain in **1831** (first in Sunderland) and struck in major epidemics in **1831–32**, **1848–49**, **1853–54** and **1866**.

- Victims suffered severe diarrhoea and vomiting and could die within a day or two. It killed **rich and poor**, though the crowded poor suffered most.
- People blamed **miasma**: responses included burning tar to clean the air, quarantines, temporary local boards of health, whitewashing and days of prayer.
- Because it threatened the **middle and upper classes** too, each epidemic added pressure for reform.

### 3. Edwin Chadwick and the 1842 Report

**Edwin Chadwick**, a civil servant who worked on the **Poor Law**, wanted to know why so many people needed poor relief.

- His **Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population** (**1842**) gathered evidence from towns across Britain.
- He showed that **life expectancy** was far lower for labourers in industrial towns than for the professional classes, and far lower in towns than in the countryside.
- He blamed **filth and bad air** (miasma), not the moral failings of the poor. His idea of the cause was **wrong**, but his solutions were **right**: drains, sewers, clean water and refuse removal.
- His key argument was **economic**: disease made workers unable to work, so families fell onto poor relief; cleaning towns would **save ratepayers money**.
- The report **sold widely** and shocked readers, but Parliament did not act at once.

### 4. The 1848 Public Health Act

Chadwick's report and the **returning cholera** led to the first **Public Health Act** (**1848**).

- It set up a **General Board of Health** in London, with Chadwick as one of its members.
- Towns **could** set up a **local board of health** to provide clean water, drains and sewers, and appoint a **medical officer of health**. Where death rates were especially high, the central Board could require one.
- It was **permissive**: most towns could choose whether to act, and many did not, because of the cost.
- The General Board was attacked for bullying local councils, and Chadwick's abrasive style made enemies; he was removed in **1854**, and the Board was wound up in **1858**.

Some towns did act alone. **Liverpool** appointed Britain's first **medical officer of health**, **William Henry Duncan**, in **1847**, under a local Act.

### 5. John Snow and the Broad Street pump

**John Snow**, a London doctor and anaesthetist, doubted miasma because cholera attacked the **gut**, not the lungs.

- In **1849** he published his argument that cholera spread through **contaminated water**.
- In the **1854** epidemic in **Soho**, London, he **mapped the deaths** and found they clustered around the public **water pump on Broad Street**.
- He persuaded the local authorities to **remove the pump handle**, and cases in the area fell (although the outbreak was already declining).
- Evidence that convinced him: workers at a nearby **brewery** drank beer and their own water and stayed healthy; a woman living away from Soho who had Broad Street water fetched for her died of cholera. The well was later found to be close to a leaking **cesspit**.

**Significance:** Snow used **statistics and mapping** to show that cholera was **waterborne**, pointing to the solution: clean water and sewers. But he had no germ to show, so many in authority stuck to miasma and his ideas were **not fully accepted** at the time. **Koch** identified the cholera bacterium in **1883**, confirming Snow's conclusion.

### 6. The Great Stink and Bazalgette's sewers

- In the hot summer of **1858** the sewage-filled **Thames** gave off a terrible smell, the **Great Stink**, and MPs beside the river could hardly work.
- The smell, and fear of miasma, pushed Parliament to give the **Metropolitan Board of Works** the money and power to build new sewers.
- Its chief engineer, **Joseph Bazalgette**, designed huge **intercepting sewers** that caught London's sewage before it reached the Thames and carried it east, away from the centre. The main system was opened in the **mid-1860s**, and work continued into the 1870s; the **Thames Embankment** was built to house part of it.
- When cholera returned in **1866**, the areas already connected to the new system escaped most of it; it struck hardest in the **East End**, where the work was not finished.

Note the **irony**: London's sewers were built because MPs believed in **miasma**, yet they worked because they stopped **sewage polluting drinking water**.

### 7. Why government changed: the 1875 Public Health Act

By the 1870s attitudes had changed, because several factors combined:

- **The vote:** the **1867 Reform Act** gave the vote to many skilled **working-class men** in towns, so politicians had to win their support.
- **Science and evidence:** **germ theory** (1861) explained disease scientifically; Chadwick, Snow and the statistician **William Farr** showed where and why people died.
- **Epidemics and the Great Stink** made doing nothing dangerous and embarrassing; **engineering** in London showed the solutions worked.
- **Local government** was better organised: in **Birmingham** in the mid-1870s, the mayor **Joseph Chamberlain** took over water and gas supplies and cleared slums.

**The 1875 Public Health Act** (passed under **Disraeli's** Conservative government) was **compulsory**. Local councils **had to**:
- provide **clean water**, and proper **drainage and sewers**;
- appoint a **medical officer of health** and **sanitary inspectors**;
- deal with nuisances such as waste and unhealthy conditions.

In the same year the **Artisans' Dwellings Act** let councils clear slums and rebuild (permissive), and the **Sale of Food and Drugs Act** targeted adulterated food.

| | **1848 Public Health Act** | **1875 Public Health Act** |
|---|---|---|
| **Type** | **Permissive**: towns chose whether to act | **Compulsory**: councils had to act |
| **Central body** | General Board of Health (ended 1858) | No new central board; duties placed on local councils |
| **Medical officers** | Optional | **Required** |
| **Impact** | Limited; many towns did nothing | Real improvement over the following decades: water, sewers, inspection |

Death rates did fall in the later 19th century, though slowly, and **poverty** remained a major cause of ill health, which the next century's reformers would tackle.

### 8. Factors in public health, c1800–c1900

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Government** | Moved from laissez-faire to **permissive** (1848) and then **compulsory** (1875) action; local councils became key |
| **Individuals** | Chadwick (evidence and campaigning), Snow (proof of waterborne cholera), Bazalgette (sewers), Chamberlain (local reform) |
| **Science and technology** | Statistics and mapping; germ theory; **engineering** (sewers, pumping stations, water pipes) |
| **Communication** | Chadwick's report sold widely; newspapers covered cholera and the Great Stink; Snow published his findings |
| **Chance** | A hot summer caused the Great Stink beside Parliament |
| **Economy** | Industrialisation caused the crisis, but also gave the wealth and engineering to solve it; cost was the main argument against reform |
| **Superstition and religion** | Miasma beliefs; days of prayer during cholera outbreaks |

::: terms
- **Laissez-faire**: the belief that government should not interfere in people's lives or the economy, so it left public health to individuals.
- **Cesspit**: a pit for collecting human waste, which often overflowed and leaked into wells.
- **Cholera**: a waterborne disease causing severe diarrhoea, which struck Britain in epidemics in 1831–32, 1848–49, 1853–54 and 1866.
- **Chadwick's Report**: the 1842 report on the sanitary condition of the labouring population, linking dirt and disease to poor relief costs.
- **Permissive act**: a law allowing local authorities to act but not forcing them, like the 1848 Public Health Act.
- **General Board of Health**: the central body set up by the 1848 Act to encourage local boards of health, wound up in 1858.
- **Medical officer of health**: a doctor employed by a town to monitor and improve public health, compulsory after 1875.
- **Broad Street pump**: the Soho water pump that John Snow linked to the 1854 cholera outbreak by mapping deaths.
- **Great Stink**: the smell of the sewage-filled Thames in the hot summer of 1858, which pushed Parliament to fund new sewers.
- **Intercepting sewers**: Bazalgette's large sewers that caught London's waste before it reached the Thames and carried it east.
:::

::: example Q2 Significance of John Snow (8)
Explain the significance of John Snow. (8)

**At the time**, Snow was significant because he challenged **miasma**. In 1849 he argued that cholera spread through **water**, and in the 1854 Soho epidemic he **mapped the deaths** and found they clustered around the **Broad Street pump**. He persuaded the authorities to remove the pump handle, and noted evidence such as the brewery workers who escaped. This was a new, **statistical method** of investigating disease.

But his significance at the time was **limited**. He could not identify the cause he was describing, so many officials kept believing in miasma, and his findings did not immediately change government policy.

**Over time** he became much more significant. His conclusion was confirmed when **Koch** identified the cholera bacterium in 1883, and it gave a clear reason for the **clean water and sewers** later required by the **1875 Public Health Act**. His mapping is seen as a foundation of **epidemiology**, so he mattered more in the long term than in his lifetime. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Difference (8)
Explain two ways in which the 1848 and 1875 Public Health Acts were different. (8)

**First, they differed in how much power they gave the government to force change.** The **1848 Act** was **permissive**: towns could set up a local board of health and improve drains and water if they chose, but most were not forced to, so many did nothing because of the cost. The **1875 Act** was **compulsory**: every local council **had to** provide clean water, drainage and sewers and appoint a medical officer of health.

**Second, they were passed in different contexts.** In 1848 government still believed in **laissez-faire**, Chadwick's case rested on the **miasma** theory, and working men had no vote; Parliament acted mainly because cholera was returning. By 1875, **germ theory** had been published (1861), Snow's work had shown that cholera was waterborne, the **Great Stink** had forced action in London, and the **1867 Reform Act** had given many working men in towns the vote, so politicians had an electoral reason to improve conditions. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Factor essay (16)
Has government been the main factor in improving public health in Britain? Explain your answer with reference to government and other factors. Use a range of examples from across your study of Health and the people: c1000 to the present day. (16)

**Government** made the largest improvements because only it could organise and pay for change on a big scale. Medieval town laws on waste had little effect, and in 1665 the authorities could only order quarantines. Real change came with the **1875 Public Health Act**, which **forced** councils to provide clean water, sewers and medical officers of health, and the **Metropolitan Board of Works** paid for Bazalgette's sewers. In the 20th century, the Liberal reforms, the **Beveridge Report** and the **NHS** (1948) were government actions.

But government often acted only when **other factors** pushed it. **Individuals** supplied the evidence: Chadwick's 1842 report and Snow's mapping of the Broad Street outbreak in 1854. **Science and technology** explained the problem (germ theory) and solved it (sewers and pumping engines). **Chance** played a part, as the hot summer of the Great Stink forced MPs to act. **Democracy** mattered: after the 1867 Reform Act working men could vote, which helped to explain the 1875 Act. **War** exposed poor health, as with the Boer War recruits.

**Judgement.** Government has been the main factor, because public health is about whole populations and needs laws and money that only government can provide; the permissive 1848 Act shows how little happened without compulsion. But government was usually **reactive**: it acted when epidemics, evidence, science and voters made inaction impossible. (Level 4.)
:::
