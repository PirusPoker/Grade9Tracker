## What it is

This lesson covers **Part one: Challenging authority and feudalism** (c1170 to c1400): **King John** and the barons, **Magna Carta** (1215) and its impact; **Henry III**, **Simon de Montfort**, the **Provisions of Oxford** (1258) and the **Parliament of 1265**; and the **Peasants' Revolt** (1381).

The big idea: medieval kings were powerful, but not all-powerful. Under **feudalism**, the king depended on his **barons** for soldiers and money, and on **peasants** for labour. When kings taxed too heavily, lost wars or ignored custom, these groups **challenged** them. The challenges usually **failed in the short term** but left ideas that mattered for centuries: the king is under the **law**, taxes need **consent**, and the realm should be governed with **advice**.

### 1. Feudalism: the background

The king granted land to **barons** and bishops in return for loyalty, advice, knights and payments; knights held land from barons; most **peasants** were **villeins** (serfs), bound to their lord's land and owing him labour. The king was expected to rule with **advice**, respect **custom** and protect the Church. Breaking these expectations risked rebellion.

### 2. King John and Magna Carta

**Why the barons were dissatisfied with John (reigned 1199–1216):**

| Cause | Details |
|---|---|
| **War and defeat** | John lost **Normandy** and most of his French lands to Philip II of France by **1204**, earning the nickname "Softsword". His attempt to win them back failed when his allies were defeated at **Bouvines** in **1214** |
| **Money** | To pay for war he raised **scutage** (a payment instead of military service) far more often than earlier kings, charged huge fees to inherit land, and sold justice and favours |
| **Religion** | He quarrelled with **Pope Innocent III** over who should be Archbishop of Canterbury (**Stephen Langton**). The Pope placed England under an **interdict** (1208), stopping most church services, and **excommunicated** John (1209). John submitted in 1213 |
| **Personal rule** | He was seen as untrustworthy and cruel; he took barons' sons as hostages and punished enemies without proper trial |

**The resolution:** after Bouvines, a group of barons **rebelled**, renounced their loyalty and captured **London** in May 1215. John was forced to negotiate and agreed to **Magna Carta** at **Runnymede** in **June 1215**.

**Key terms of Magna Carta (the "Great Charter"):**
- The **Church** would be free from royal interference, including in elections.
- No **scutage** or extra **aid** would be raised without the **common counsel** of the kingdom (the barons and bishops), except for customary payments.
- No **free man** could be imprisoned, outlawed or punished except by the **lawful judgement of his peers** or the **law of the land**.
- Justice would not be **sold, denied or delayed**.
- Fixed fees for inheritance; widows could not be forced to remarry; standard weights and measures; rights for London and other towns.
- A council of **25 barons** would make sure the king kept the charter, and could seize his lands if he did not.

**Short-term impact: failure.** Neither side trusted the other. John asked the Pope to **annul** the charter, which he did within a few months, and civil war (the **First Barons' War**) broke out. The rebels invited **Prince Louis** of France to take the throne. John died in **1216**, and the regents of his young son **Henry III** **reissued** Magna Carta (1216, 1217 and **1225**) to win back the barons' loyalty.

**Long-term impact:** Magna Carta became a **symbol** that the king is **under the law** and that taxation needs **consent**. It gave little to villeins (not "free men"), but its clauses on lawful judgement were later read as protecting everyone; Parliament cited it against Charles I, and it inspired the American colonists.

### 3. Henry III, Simon de Montfort and the origins of Parliament

**Issues between Henry III (reigned 1216–1272) and his barons:**
- **Foreign favourites:** Henry gave land, money and power to his wife's relatives from **Savoy** and to his **Lusignan** half-brothers from Poitou. English barons felt excluded.
- **Costs and failure:** expensive, unsuccessful campaigns in France, and his acceptance of a papal offer of the crown of **Sicily** for his son, which committed England to pay huge sums.
- **Consent:** barons increasingly demanded a say over taxation in great councils, now called **parliaments**, in return for granting money. A poor harvest and famine in the late 1250s added to the crisis.

**The Provisions of Oxford (1258):** the barons forced Henry to accept reforms. A council of **fifteen** would advise the king and supervise government; **parliament** would meet **three times a year**; royal officials would be accountable. This was a major limit on royal power. Henry later got the Pope to release him from his oath, and conflict resumed.

**Simon de Montfort**, Earl of Leicester, was a French-born noble who had married Henry's sister. He became leader of the reforming barons.
- At the **Battle of Lewes** (**1264**) he defeated the royal army and captured **Henry** and Prince **Edward**, and governed in the king's name.
- To win wider support, he summoned the **Parliament of 1265**: as well as barons and bishops, it included **two knights from each shire** and, for the first time, **two burgesses** (townsmen) from each of a number of **towns**.
- Prince Edward escaped, and at the **Battle of Evesham** (**1265**) de Montfort was **killed** and his body mutilated.

**Short-term impact:** the reformers were crushed and the king restored. But some reforms were kept in later law, and Henry and Edward had learned that they needed cooperation.
**Long-term impact:** as **Edward I**, the prince regularly summoned knights and burgesses, because their consent made taxes easier to collect: the origin of the **House of Commons**. De Montfort is seen as a founder of Parliament, though he acted largely to strengthen his own position.

### 4. The Peasants' Revolt (1381)

**Causes:**

| Type | Cause |
|---|---|
| **Economic** | The **Black Death** (1348–49) killed a huge share of the population, so labour was scarce and surviving peasants could demand **higher wages** or move. The **Statute of Labourers** (**1351**) tried to hold wages at pre-plague levels and stop peasants moving, which caused deep resentment |
| **Social** | Peasants increasingly resented **serfdom** (villeinage): labour services and dues owed to lords. Lords tried to enforce them more strictly |
| **Political** | **Poll taxes** in 1377, 1379 and **1381** (the third, a flat rate on everyone over 15, hit the poor hardest) paid for the costly and failing **war with France**. **Richard II** was only **14**, and his advisers, especially his uncle **John of Gaunt**, were unpopular |
| **Ideas** | **John Ball**, a radical priest, preached that all people were equal in God's eyes, summed up in the rhyme "When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman?" |

**Events:**
1. In **May–June 1381** villagers in **Essex** attacked tax commissioners; revolt spread to **Kent**, where **Wat Tyler** became leader and the rebels freed John Ball from prison.
2. Rebels marched on **London** and entered the city in mid-**June**. They burned **John of Gaunt's Savoy Palace** and attacked lawyers and records.
3. At **Mile End**, Richard II met rebels and **promised** to end serfdom and to pardon them. Some rebels entered the **Tower of London** and killed the **Archbishop of Canterbury** (Simon Sudbury, the Chancellor) and the Treasurer.
4. At **Smithfield**, Wat Tyler made further demands; the **Mayor of London, William Walworth**, struck him down, and he was killed. Richard rode forward and told the rebels he would be their leader, and they **dispersed**.
5. Richard then **revoked** his promises; rebel leaders, including John Ball, were hunted down and **executed**.

**Impact:** in the short term the revolt **failed**: serfdom was not abolished and the leaders were killed. But **no more poll taxes** of this kind were imposed for centuries, Parliament was more cautious about taxing the poor, and lords became wary of provoking peasants. Over the following decades **serfdom declined** anyway, because labour remained scarce and peasants could bargain for wages and freedom. It is remembered as an early **popular challenge** to authority and to the idea that some people are born to serve others.

::: terms
- **Feudalism**: the medieval system in which land was held in return for service and loyalty, from king to barons to knights to peasants.
- **Villein**: an unfree peasant (serf) bound to a lord's land and owing labour services.
- **Scutage**: a payment to the king instead of military service, raised often by King John.
- **Interdict**: a papal ban on most church services in a country, placed on England in 1208.
- **Magna Carta**: the charter agreed by King John in June 1215, limiting royal power and protecting barons' rights.
- **Common counsel**: Magna Carta's requirement that the barons and bishops agree to new taxes.
- **Provisions of Oxford**: the reforms of 1258 setting up a council of fifteen and regular parliaments to supervise Henry III.
- **Burgess**: a representative of a town, summoned to Parliament for the first time in 1265.
- **Statute of Labourers**: the 1351 law that tried to fix wages at pre-Black Death levels and stop peasants moving.
- **Poll tax**: a tax charged per head, levied in 1377, 1379 and 1381, which triggered the Peasants' Revolt.
:::

::: example Q1 Source utility (8)
Source A, written for this practice question, is in the style of an account by an English monk in a chronicle written a few years after 1381. He describes the rebels as a mob of wicked and ignorant men who murdered the Archbishop, and praises the young King Richard for his courage at Smithfield, where he calmed the rebels and sent them home. How useful is Source A to an historian studying the Peasants' Revolt? Explain your answer using Source A and your contextual knowledge. (8)

Source A is useful because it describes real events: the rebels did kill **Archbishop Sudbury** in the Tower, and at **Smithfield**, after **Wat Tyler** was struck down by the Mayor, **Richard II** rode forward and persuaded the rebels to disperse. It shows that the revolt was violent and that the king's actions ended it.

Its **provenance** shapes its view. A **monk** belonged to the Church, which was a major landowner with serfs of its own, and the rebels had killed the Archbishop, so he was likely to be **hostile**. Calling them "ignorant" ignores their real **grievances**: the **poll tax** of 1381, the **Statute of Labourers** and resentment of serfdom. Writing a few years later, he also knew that Richard had **revoked** his promises, yet praises him, suggesting loyalty to the Crown.

So the source is useful for **how the ruling class and Church saw the revolt**, and for the main events, but an historian would need other evidence to understand the rebels' aims. (Level 4.)

::: example Q2 Significance of Magna Carta (8)
Explain the significance of Magna Carta. (8)

**In the short term**, Magna Carta (June 1215) was significant as the first time the barons forced a king to accept **written limits** on his power: no scutage without **common counsel**, no free man punished without **lawful judgement**, and a council of **25 barons** to enforce it. But it **failed** almost at once: John got the Pope to annul it and civil war followed.

It became more significant after John's death in 1216, when the regents of **Henry III** **reissued** it (1216, 1217, 1225) to win back the barons. Reissue made it part of the law, and later kings confirmed it repeatedly.

**In the long term** it became a **symbol** that the king is **under the law** and that taxes need **consent**, ideas that underpinned the growth of **Parliament**. Seventeenth-century opponents of Charles I used it against him, and it influenced the American colonists. Its significance grew far beyond its original purpose, which was to protect the rights of a small group of barons. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q3 Similarity (8)
Explain two ways in which the barons' rebellion against King John and the Peasants' Revolt were similar. (8)

**First, both were caused by taxes to pay for war against France.** John raised **scutage** far more often than earlier kings to pay for wars to recover **Normandy**, lost in 1204, and the defeat at **Bouvines** in 1214 triggered the barons' rebellion. In 1381 the third **poll tax** was raised to pay for the failing war with France, and attacks on tax commissioners in Essex started the Peasants' Revolt.

**Second, in both the king made promises he did not keep, but the revolt still had long-term effects.** John agreed to **Magna Carta** in 1215, then got the Pope to annul it. Richard II promised at **Mile End** to end serfdom, then **revoked** his promises and executed the leaders. Yet Magna Carta was reissued and became a lasting symbol of limits on the Crown, and after 1381 no more poll taxes of that kind were raised and serfdom declined. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Q4 Factor essay (16)
Has the role of the individual been the main factor in challenging the authority of the monarch? Explain your answer with reference to the role of the individual and other factors. Use a range of examples from across your study of Power and the people: c1170 to the present day. (16)

**Individuals** were often crucial. **Simon de Montfort** led the barons against Henry III, captured him at Lewes (1264) and summoned the 1265 Parliament with knights and burgesses. **John Ball** and **Wat Tyler** inspired and led the Peasants' Revolt. **Robert Aske** led the Pilgrimage of Grace (1536), and **Oliver Cromwell** led the army that defeated Charles I, who was executed in 1649. Monarchs' own actions mattered too: John's failures provoked Magna Carta.

But individuals usually led challenges caused by **other factors**. **The economy** and **war** were behind most: John's scutage for war in France; the poll tax of 1381 to pay for the French war; Charles I's attempts to raise money without Parliament. **Religion** drove the Pilgrimage of Grace and the conflict with Charles I. **Ideas** of consent, representation and later democracy gave challenges their purpose, from Magna Carta to the American colonists' "no taxation without representation".

**Judgement.** The role of the individual was important in **how** challenges happened, but **economic** grievances, especially **tax to pay for war**, were the main factor in **why** they happened: in 1215, 1381 and the 1640s, people challenged monarchs when kings demanded money without consent. (Level 4.)
