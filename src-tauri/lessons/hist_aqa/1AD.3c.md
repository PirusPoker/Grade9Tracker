## What it is

This lesson covers the third bullet of Part three: **America and the "Great Society"**: the **social policies of Presidents Kennedy and Johnson** on **poverty, education and health**; and the **development and impact of feminist movements in the 1960s and early 1970s**, including the **fight for equal pay**, the **National Organisation for Women**, **Roe v Wade (1973)**, the **Supreme Court ruling on equal rights (1972)** and the **opposition to the Equal Rights Amendment**.

The big idea: the prosperity of the 1950s had hidden millions of poor Americans and had left women's lives defined by home and family. In the 1960s two Democratic presidents used the power of the federal government to attack poverty, and a revived women's movement used books, organisations, laws and the courts to attack discrimination. Both made real changes, and both ran into limits: money, Congress, the Vietnam War, and a conservative backlash.

### 1. Poverty in the "affluent society"

- In the early 1960s roughly **one American in five** lived in poverty, even though the country as a whole was richer than ever.
- The poor were concentrated among **African Americans**, the **elderly**, single mothers, families in run-down **inner cities**, and rural areas such as **Appalachia** where mining and farming jobs had disappeared.
- **Michael Harrington's book *The Other America* (1962)** argued that this poverty was "invisible" to suburban, middle-class America. It is said to have influenced both Kennedy and Johnson.
- Many old people had no health insurance, and private medical bills could wipe out their savings.

### 2. Kennedy and the "New Frontier" (1961–63)

**John F. Kennedy** won the 1960 election narrowly. In his campaign he spoke of a **"New Frontier"**: new challenges at home and abroad that Americans should meet with energy and idealism.

| Area | What Kennedy did |
|---|---|
| **Poverty and jobs** | Raised the **minimum wage** (1961); the **Area Redevelopment Act (1961)** gave aid to depressed regions such as Appalachia; the **Manpower Development and Training Act (1962)** paid for training the unemployed; extended unemployment benefit and Social Security payments |
| **Housing** | The **Housing Act (1961)** funded slum clearance and new low-cost housing |
| **Women** | Set up the **Presidential Commission on the Status of Women (1961)**, chaired at first by Eleanor Roosevelt; its 1963 report recorded discrimination at work and in law. Signed the **Equal Pay Act (1963)** |
| **Idealism** | The **Peace Corps (1961)** sent young volunteers to work in poorer countries |
| **Health and education** | Proposed health care for the elderly and federal aid to schools, but **Congress rejected both** |

**Why Kennedy achieved less than he hoped**
- His victory was very narrow, so he had **no strong mandate**.
- **Congress** was dominated by a coalition of **Republicans and conservative Southern Democrats** who blocked spending and feared federal interference in the states (especially over race).
- Much of his attention went on **foreign policy**: the Bay of Pigs, Berlin, the Cuban Missile Crisis.
- He was **assassinated in Dallas on 22 November 1963**, before many of his plans could pass.

Historians disagree about Kennedy: some see him as an inspiring leader whose ideas Johnson completed; others see more style than achievement at home.

### 3. Johnson and the "Great Society" (1963–69)

**Lyndon B. Johnson** had been the Democrats' leader in the Senate for years and was a master at persuading Congress. He used the national grief after Kennedy's death to pass Kennedy's stalled plans and then went much further. In January 1964 he declared an **"unconditional war on poverty"**. In 1964 he won a **landslide** election against the conservative Republican Barry Goldwater, with large Democratic majorities in Congress.

| Area | Great Society measures |
|---|---|
| **Poverty** | **Economic Opportunity Act (1964)**: created the **Office of Economic Opportunity**, the **Job Corps** (training for young people), **VISTA** (volunteers working in poor areas, a home version of the Peace Corps), **community action** programmes run partly by the poor themselves; **Head Start** (from 1965) gave pre-school education and meals to poor children |
| **Education** | **Elementary and Secondary Education Act (1965)**: the first large federal grants to schools, aimed at districts with many poor children; **Higher Education Act (1965)**: grants and loans for university students |
| **Health** | **Medicare (1965)**: health insurance for people aged **65 and over**; **Medicaid (1965)**: health care for people on **low incomes** |
| **Civil rights** | Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965) were also part of Johnson's programme |
| **Cities and housing** | A new Department of Housing and Urban Development (1965); money for low-cost housing |

**How successful was the Great Society?**

| Successes | Limits and criticisms |
|---|---|
| The poverty rate fell sharply: by the end of the 1960s about **one in eight** Americans was poor, down from about one in five | The **Vietnam War** swallowed money and Johnson's attention; spending on the war on poverty was far smaller than on the war in Asia |
| Medicare and Medicaid gave millions of elderly and poor people health care for the first time, and have lasted | **Riots** in cities such as Watts (1965) and Detroit (1967) showed deep problems remained in Black ghettos |
| Head Start and federal school funding gave poor children more opportunity | **Conservatives** argued that welfare created dependency, wasted taxpayers' money and expanded the federal government too far |
| Johnson passed more social laws than any president since Roosevelt's New Deal | Some programmes were badly organised; community action angered local politicians; rising taxes and inflation turned voters against spending |

In 1968 Johnson, unpopular because of Vietnam, chose not to stand again. The Republican **Richard Nixon** won, and the "war on poverty" lost momentum.

### 4. Women in the early 1960s

- After 1945 many women who had worked in the war returned home. 1950s films, magazines and adverts promoted the **ideal of the suburban housewife and mother**.
- Yet more women were working, mostly in **low-paid, low-status jobs** (secretaries, shop assistants, nurses, teachers). Women earned roughly **60 per cent** of what men earned.
- Few women became doctors, lawyers, managers or politicians; some universities and professions limited women's places; married women often found it hard to get credit in their own name.

### 5. The feminist movement develops

**Betty Friedan and *The Feminine Mystique* (1963).** Friedan surveyed women who had been to college with her and found many felt empty and frustrated as full-time housewives, which she called "the problem that has no name". The book sold in huge numbers and convinced many middle-class women that their dissatisfaction was shared and caused by society, not by personal failure.

**The fight for equal pay**
- The **Equal Pay Act (1963)** made it illegal to pay women less than men **for the same work**.
- Its limits: it did not cover all jobs at first; employers could give women's jobs different titles; and it did nothing about women being kept in lower-paid kinds of work. The gap between men's and women's average pay narrowed only slowly.
- The **Civil Rights Act (1964)** banned discrimination in employment on grounds of **sex** as well as race. But the **Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC)**, set up to enforce it, at first treated sex discrimination complaints as unimportant.

**The National Organisation for Women (NOW), 1966**
- Founded by Friedan and others, frustrated at the EEOC's attitude; Friedan was its first president.
- Aims: full equality for women in work, education and law; equal pay; enforcement of the 1964 Act; child-care provision; later, the right to abortion and the Equal Rights Amendment.
- Methods: **lobbying** Congress, **legal cases** against employers, publicity and demonstrations. It was largely a **liberal**, middle-class organisation working within the system.

**Women's liberation ("women's lib")**
- Younger, more **radical** feminists, many of whom had been involved in the civil rights and anti-Vietnam War movements, where they were often treated as second-class by male activists.
- They challenged the whole culture: beauty contests, advertising, marriage roles and sexuality. In **1968** they protested at the **Miss America** contest in Atlantic City, throwing items such as make-up and high heels into a "freedom trash can".
- **Consciousness-raising groups**: small meetings where women discussed their experiences and saw them as political ("the personal is political").
- **Gloria Steinem** helped found ***Ms.*** magazine in the early 1970s.

### 6. The early 1970s: the law and the courts

| Development | What it meant |
|---|---|
| **Equal Rights Amendment (ERA)** passed by **Congress in 1972** | A proposed change to the Constitution saying equal rights could not be denied on account of sex. To become law it needed ratification by **three-quarters (38) of the states**. Many states ratified quickly |
| **The Supreme Court ruling on equal rights (1972)** | In the early 1970s the Supreme Court began to rule that laws treating men and women differently could breach the Constitution's guarantee of **equal protection**, and it widened women's rights over their private lives. These rulings strengthened the idea that sex discrimination was unconstitutional |
| **Title IX (1972)** | Banned sex discrimination in education programmes receiving federal money, opening up places and sport to girls and women |
| **Roe v Wade (January 1973)** | The Supreme Court ruled, by **7 votes to 2**, that a woman's **right to privacy** under the Constitution included the right to choose an **abortion**, especially in the early months of pregnancy; states could restrict it later in pregnancy. Abortion laws in most states were struck down |

**Opposition: Phyllis Schlafly and the ERA**
- **Phyllis Schlafly**, a conservative lawyer and activist, launched the **STOP ERA** campaign in **1972**.
- She argued the ERA would **harm** women: they could be **conscripted** into the army; wives and widows could lose financial protections; the traditional family would be undermined. She presented herself as defending housewives and mothers.
- She drew support from many **religious** and conservative women, especially in the South and the Midwest.
- Result: ratifications **stalled** after the first rush, several states voted to withdraw their approval, and the ERA **never reached the 38 states** needed, even after its deadline was extended.
- **Roe v Wade** also created a powerful **"pro-life"** movement, much of it religious, which has campaigned against legal abortion ever since.

### 7. Impact: how far had women's lives changed by the mid-1970s?

| Changed | Did not change (or changed slowly) |
|---|---|
| Laws against pay and job discrimination (1963, 1964); Title IX in education (1972) | Average pay stayed well below men's; most women remained in "women's jobs" |
| Legal right to abortion (1973); more control over family size | Abortion became one of the most divisive issues in American politics |
| More women at university, in professions and in politics | The ERA failed; many women, like Schlafly's supporters, rejected feminism |
| Changed attitudes, language and media images of women | Black, working-class and poor women often felt the movement spoke mainly for white, middle-class women |

Second-order concepts to use: **change and continuity** (laws versus pay and attitudes), **consequence** (Friedan's book led to NOW; Roe led to the pro-life movement), **difference** (different groups of women experienced feminism differently), **significance** (which development mattered most, and for whom).

::: terms
- **New Frontier**: Kennedy's name for his programme of reforms at home and new challenges abroad, 1961–63.
- **Great Society**: Johnson's programme of social reform, including the war on poverty, Medicare and Medicaid and federal aid to education.
- **War on poverty**: Johnson's campaign, announced in January 1964, to reduce poverty through federal programmes such as the Job Corps and Head Start.
- **Medicare**: the 1965 federal health insurance scheme for Americans aged 65 and over.
- **Medicaid**: the 1965 scheme giving health care to Americans on low incomes.
- **Head Start**: the programme giving pre-school education, meals and health checks to poor children.
- **The Feminine Mystique**: Betty Friedan's 1963 book describing the frustration of educated women confined to the role of housewife.
- **NOW**: the National Organisation for Women, founded in 1966 to campaign for equal rights for women through law and lobbying.
- **Equal Pay Act**: the 1963 law making it illegal to pay women less than men for doing the same job.
- **Equal Rights Amendment (ERA)**: a proposed change to the Constitution, passed by Congress in 1972, banning denial of rights on account of sex; never ratified by enough states.
- **Roe v Wade**: the 1973 Supreme Court ruling that the right to privacy included a woman's right to choose an abortion.
- **STOP ERA**: Phyllis Schlafly's campaign, begun in 1972, to stop states ratifying the Equal Rights Amendment.
:::

::: example Q5 In what ways (8)
In what ways were women affected by the feminist movement in the 1960s and early 1970s? Explain your answer. (8)

**Work and pay.** Campaigning helped bring the Equal Pay Act (1963), which made it illegal to pay women less for the same job, and NOW pressed the EEOC to enforce the 1964 ban on sex discrimination. More women entered professions. But because women were kept in lower-paid jobs, the average pay gap closed only slowly.

**Education.** Title IX (1972) banned sex discrimination in federally funded education, opening up university places and school sport.

**Control over their lives.** Roe v Wade (1973) gave women the legal right to an abortion, especially in early pregnancy, giving them more control over whether and when to have children.

**Attitudes.** *The Feminine Mystique* (1963), consciousness-raising groups and protests such as the 1968 Miss America demonstration made many women question the housewife ideal.

**Complexity (Level 4).** The effects were uneven. Educated, middle-class white women, Friedan's original readers, gained most from new professional opportunities, while many Black and working-class women felt the movement ignored their needs. And some women actively opposed it: Phyllis Schlafly's STOP ERA campaign (1972) mobilised conservative housewives, and the ERA was never ratified. Feminism changed laws faster than it changed pay or attitudes.
:::

::: example Q6 Which was more successful (12)
Which of the following was the more successful in dealing with America's social problems: Kennedy's policies; Johnson's policies? Explain your answer with reference to both bullet points. (12)

**Kennedy's policies.** The New Frontier raised the minimum wage, aided depressed areas through the Area Redevelopment Act (1961), funded job training (1962) and housing (1961), and set up the Commission on the Status of Women, leading to the Equal Pay Act (1963). But Congress, dominated by Republicans and Southern Democrats, rejected his plans for health care for the elderly and federal aid to schools, and his narrow election gave him little power to force them through.

**Johnson's policies.** Johnson used his skill with Congress and the mood after Kennedy's assassination to pass the Economic Opportunity Act (1964), with the Job Corps, VISTA and Head Start; Medicare and Medicaid (1965); and the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (1965). Poverty fell from about one in five Americans to about one in eight by the end of the decade. But Vietnam drained money, the riots of 1965–67 showed ghetto poverty remained, and a backlash helped Nixon win in 1968.

**Judgement.** Johnson's policies were more successful because they actually became law and some, especially Medicare and Medicaid, still exist; Kennedy's most ambitious ideas stalled. Yet Johnson's success partly rested on Kennedy: many of his early measures were Kennedy's blocked proposals, passed in Kennedy's memory. Kennedy set the agenda; Johnson delivered it, though Vietnam stopped him finishing the job. (Level 4.)
