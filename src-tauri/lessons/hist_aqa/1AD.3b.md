## What it is

This lesson covers the second bullet of Part three: **racial tension and developments in the Civil Rights campaigns in the 1950s and 1960s**: **segregation laws**; **Martin Luther King and peaceful protests**; **Malcolm X and the Black Power movement**; and the **Civil Rights Acts of 1964 and 1968**.

The big idea: between 1954 and 1968 African Americans used the courts, mass peaceful protest and, later, the more radical ideas of Black Power to destroy legal segregation in the South. Federal laws in 1964, 1965 and 1968 banned discrimination, but poverty, segregated housing and racism in the North showed that legal equality was not the same as real equality.

### 1. Segregation laws

- **Jim Crow laws** in the Southern states separated Black and white Americans in schools, buses, restaurants, parks, toilets and water fountains. The Supreme Court had allowed this in 1896 (*Plessy v Ferguson*) as long as facilities were "separate but equal"; in practice Black facilities were far worse.
- **Voting:** literacy tests, poll taxes and intimidation kept most Black Southerners from voting.
- **Violence:** lynching and attacks by groups such as the Ku Klux Klan went largely unpunished by white juries.
- In the North there were no segregation laws, but discrimination in jobs and housing created segregated, poorer neighbourhoods (**ghettos**).

### 2. The courts: Brown v Topeka and Little Rock

- **Brown v Board of Education of Topeka (1954).** The NAACP, with its lawyer **Thurgood Marshall**, challenged the segregated schools of Topeka, Kansas, for Linda Brown. Under Chief Justice **Earl Warren**, the Supreme Court ruled **unanimously** that segregated schools were **unconstitutional** because separate schools could never be equal. A later ruling (1955) said desegregation should happen "with all deliberate speed", which Southern states treated as permission to delay.
- **Southern resistance:** White Citizens' Councils formed; many Southern politicians pledged to resist.
- **Emmett Till (1955).** A 14-year-old Black boy from Chicago was murdered in Mississippi for allegedly whistling at a white woman. His killers were acquitted by an all-white jury. His mother insisted on an open coffin, and photographs shocked the nation.
- **Little Rock (1957).** Nine Black students (the **Little Rock Nine**) were to attend Central High School in Arkansas. Governor **Orval Faubus** used the state National Guard to stop them; white mobs abused them. President **Eisenhower** sent federal troops to escort them into school. Television showed the hatred to the world, and the federal government had shown it would enforce the law.

### 3. Martin Luther King and peaceful protest

**Dr Martin Luther King** was a Baptist minister who believed in **non-violent direct action**, influenced by Christianity and Gandhi: break unjust laws peacefully, accept arrest, and win the moral argument and public sympathy.

| Event | What happened | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–56)** | **Rosa Parks**, an NAACP activist, was arrested on 1 December 1955 for refusing to give up her bus seat to a white man. King led the **Montgomery Improvement Association**; Black residents boycotted the buses for over a year, walking and organising car-pools. King's house was bombed | The **Supreme Court ruled bus segregation unconstitutional (1956)**; buses were desegregated. King became a national leader and founded the **SCLC (1957)** |
| **Sit-ins (1960)** | Four Black students sat at a whites-only lunch counter in **Greensboro**, North Carolina; sit-ins spread across the South. **SNCC** was formed by students | Many lunch counters desegregated |
| **Freedom Rides (1961)** | Black and white activists rode buses through the South to test a court ruling against segregated bus stations; buses were attacked and burned, riders beaten | Federal government enforced desegregation of interstate travel |
| **Birmingham, Alabama (1963)** | King targeted one of the most segregated cities. Police chief **"Bull" Connor** used **fire hoses and police dogs**, including on children who joined the marches. King wrote his *Letter from Birmingham Jail* | TV pictures shocked Americans; President **Kennedy** proposed a civil rights bill |
| **March on Washington (August 1963)** | About **250,000** people marched peacefully; King gave his **"I have a dream"** speech | Built pressure on Congress for the civil rights bill |
| **Selma, Alabama (1965)** | Marches for voting rights; on **"Bloody Sunday"** (March 1965) state troopers beat marchers on a bridge outside Selma; King then led a march to Montgomery | President **Johnson** pushed through the **Voting Rights Act (1965)** |

King won the **Nobel Peace Prize (1964)**. Later he campaigned against poverty and the Vietnam War and in the North, where his methods had less success (Chicago, 1966). He was **assassinated in Memphis on 4 April 1968**.

### 4. Malcolm X and Black Power

**Malcolm X** joined the **Nation of Islam** in prison and became its most famous preacher.
- He argued that white Americans would never accept Black people as equals, so African Americans should be **separate** and self-reliant, take pride in Black identity, and defend themselves "by any means necessary", including force if attacked.
- He criticised King's non-violence and the March on Washington.
- In 1964 he left the Nation of Islam; after a pilgrimage to Mecca he moderated his views on white people and cooperation. He was **assassinated in February 1965**.

**Black Power (from 1966)**
- **Stokely Carmichael**, leader of SNCC, popularised the slogan "Black Power" in 1966. It meant pride in Black identity and culture, African Americans controlling their own organisations and communities, and rejecting reliance on white liberals.
- **The Black Panther Party (1966)**, founded in Oakland, California, by **Huey Newton** and **Bobby Seale**: armed patrols to watch the police, plus community programmes such as free breakfasts for children. The authorities saw them as dangerous; there were violent clashes with police.
- **Riots** broke out in many cities, including **Watts, Los Angeles (1965)** and **Detroit (1967)**, driven by poverty, unemployment, poor housing and police brutality in Northern ghettos.
- At the **1968 Mexico City Olympics**, two Black American medal winners raised gloved fists on the podium.

**King and Malcolm X compared**

| | King | Malcolm X |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Integration and equal rights | Separation and Black self-reliance (until 1964) |
| Methods | Non-violent direct action, mass protest, appealing to the law and public opinion | Self-defence; rejected non-violence; preached Black pride |
| Main support | Southern churches, students, white liberals | Northern city ghettos, young African Americans |
| Achievements | Montgomery, Birmingham, Washington, Selma; built support for federal laws | Gave pride and confidence; influenced Black Power; highlighted Northern problems |

### 5. The Civil Rights Acts

| Law | What it did |
|---|---|
| **Civil Rights Act (July 1964)**, signed by **President Johnson** after Kennedy's assassination | Banned **segregation in public places** (hotels, restaurants, cinemas); banned **discrimination in employment** on grounds of race, religion, national origin or sex; set up the **Equal Employment Opportunity Commission** |
| **Voting Rights Act (1965)** | Banned literacy tests; federal officials could register voters in the South. Black voter registration in the South rose sharply |
| **Civil Rights Act (April 1968)**, passed days after King's death | Known as the **Fair Housing Act**: banned **discrimination in the sale or renting of housing** |

**How far had things changed by 1968?** Legal segregation in the South was finished, and Black voting and elected officials increased. But poverty, unemployment, segregated schools and housing in practice, and racist violence remained, especially in Northern cities.

::: terms
- **Segregation**: the separation of people by race in schools, transport and public places, enforced by Jim Crow laws in the South.
- **Civil rights**: the rights every citizen should have, such as equal treatment, voting and freedom from discrimination.
- **NAACP**: the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, which fought segregation in the courts.
- **Brown v Topeka**: the 1954 Supreme Court ruling that segregated schools were unconstitutional.
- **Boycott**: refusing to use or buy something as a protest, as in the Montgomery Bus Boycott of 1955–56.
- **Non-violent direct action**: peaceful protest that deliberately breaks unjust laws, used by King.
- **Sit-in**: a protest in which people occupy seats in a segregated place and refuse to leave.
- **Freedom Rides**: the 1961 bus journeys through the South to test the desegregation of interstate travel.
- **SCLC**: the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, King's organisation founded in 1957.
- **Nation of Islam**: the Black Muslim organisation that preached separation from white America, of which Malcolm X was a leader.
- **Black Power**: the 1960s movement for Black pride, self-reliance and control of Black communities.
- **Black Panthers**: the party founded in Oakland in 1966 that organised armed patrols and community programmes.
:::

::: example Q1 and Q2: interpretations on civil rights (4 + 4)
Interpretation A, written for this practice question, is in the style of a Black minister from Alabama who marched with King, writing in 1970: "We won because we refused to hate. We filled the jails, faced the dogs and the hoses, and the nation saw who was right. Without one shot fired we brought down Jim Crow." Interpretation B, also written for practice, is in the style of a former Black Panther from California, interviewed in 1988: "Marching and praying got Southern lunch counters opened. It did nothing for us in the ghetto. We were still poor, still beaten by the police. Only pride and power could change that." How does Interpretation B differ from Interpretation A about the success of the civil rights movement? (4)

A says the movement **succeeded** through non-violence, which won public sympathy and destroyed Jim Crow (1–2). B says peaceful protest achieved **only limited change**, ending some Southern segregation but leaving Black people in Northern ghettos poor and abused by police (+1). So A sees non-violence as the key to victory, B sees it as inadequate, arguing for Black pride and power (+1). Level 2, 4/4.

Why might the authors have a different interpretation? (4)

The minister lived in Alabama, where segregation was enforced by law, and took part in the campaigns that ended it, such as Birmingham and Selma; as a Christian minister he shared King's belief in non-violence (1–2). The Panther lived in a Californian city, where there were no Jim Crow laws but poverty and police brutality, as in the Watts riots of 1965, so Southern legal victories made little difference to him; the Panthers believed in self-defence (+1–2). Level 2, 4/4.

::: example Q4 Describe two (4)
Describe two features of the Montgomery Bus Boycott. (4)

**How it started.** On 1 December 1955 Rosa Parks was arrested for refusing to give up her seat on a Montgomery bus to a white man (1). Local leaders formed the Montgomery Improvement Association and chose Martin Luther King as its leader (1).

**How it was sustained.** For over a year Black residents refused to ride the buses, walking or using organised car-pools (1). Despite arrests and the bombing of King's house, it continued until the Supreme Court ruled bus segregation unconstitutional in 1956 (1).
:::

::: example Q5 In what ways (8)
In what ways did the civil rights movement change the lives of African Americans in the 1950s and 1960s? Explain your answer. (8)

**Education.** Brown v Topeka (1954) ruled segregated schools unconstitutional; at Little Rock (1957) federal troops enforced it, though desegregation was slow.

**Public places and transport.** Montgomery (1955–56) ended bus segregation there; sit-ins (1960) and the Freedom Rides (1961) desegregated lunch counters and interstate travel; the Civil Rights Act (1964) banned segregation in public places and discrimination in jobs.

**Voting and housing.** After Selma, the Voting Rights Act (1965) banned literacy tests and Black registration in the South rose sharply; the 1968 Act banned discrimination in housing.

**Complexity (Level 4).** The changes were mostly legal and mostly in the South. In Northern cities, where there were no Jim Crow laws, poverty, unemployment and police brutality continued, which is why riots broke out in Watts (1965) and Detroit (1967) and why Black Power appealed there. The movement transformed Southern law faster than it changed economic reality.
:::

::: example Q6 Which was more important (12)
Which of the following was more important in the progress of civil rights in the 1950s and 1960s: peaceful protest; the actions of the federal government? Explain your answer with reference to both bullet points. (12)

**Peaceful protest.** King's campaigns created crises the nation could not ignore: Montgomery (1955–56), the sit-ins (1960), the Freedom Rides (1961), Birmingham (1963), where TV showed Bull Connor's dogs and hoses used on children, the March on Washington (1963) and Selma (1965). Non-violence won national and world sympathy.

**Federal government.** The Supreme Court's Brown ruling (1954) and the 1956 bus ruling; Eisenhower's troops at Little Rock (1957); Kennedy's bill (1963); Johnson's Civil Rights Act (1964), Voting Rights Act (1965) and the 1968 Act. Only federal law could override Southern states.

**Judgement.** Federal action produced the lasting changes, but peaceful protest was more important, because it forced the federal government to act: Kennedy proposed his bill after Birmingham, and Johnson pushed the Voting Rights Act after Selma. Presidents had been reluctant to anger Southern Democrats until protest made inaction impossible. Each needed the other, but protest drove the timing. (Level 4.)
:::

::: watch
- **Lumping the Civil Rights Acts together.** 1964: public places and employment; 1965 (Voting Rights): literacy tests and registration; 1968: housing.
- **Saying segregation ended in 1954.** Brown ruled school segregation unconstitutional, but Southern states resisted for years.
- **Treating Malcolm X as simply violent.** He argued for self-defence, Black pride and separation, and moderated his views in 1964.
- **Ignoring the North.** The riots of 1965–67 and Black Power show the limits of legal change.
- **Telling the story without causation.** Link each campaign to its result: Birmingham → Kennedy's bill; Selma → the Voting Rights Act.
:::
