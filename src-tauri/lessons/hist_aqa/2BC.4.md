## What it is

This lesson covers **Part four: The historic environment of Elizabethan England**. AQA names one **site** each year, and Question 4 of the exam (16 marks) is always about it. For students sitting in **June 2028** the site is **Kenilworth Castle** in Warwickshire. The site changes each year (the Globe in 2026, the Spanish Armada in 2027), so check AQA's list for your exam year; the method here works for any site.

The big idea: Kenilworth shows how a **medieval fortress** was turned into an **Elizabethan palace** by the queen's favourite, **Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester**, largely to **impress Elizabeth** and win her favour, perhaps even her hand. It is evidence for **patronage**, **court culture**, **progresses**, **marriage politics**, the **wealth and fashion** of the "Golden Age", and change and continuity. Every feature you describe must be tied to these developments. You do not need to have visited.

### 2. Location and earlier history

- **Location**: in the heart of **Warwickshire**, in the Midlands, between Warwick and Coventry, on a low rise surrounded by water. In the Middle Ages a large artificial lake, the **great mere**, created by damming streams, protected it on the south and west. In Elizabethan times the mere became a **stage** for entertainments.
- **Norman origins**: the castle was founded in the **early twelfth century** by **Geoffrey de Clinton**, an official of Henry I. Its great stone **keep** (the Great Tower) dates from the Norman period.
- **Medieval fortress**: later kings, especially **King John**, spent heavily strengthening it with walls and water defences. In **1266** it withstood one of the longest sieges in medieval English history, held by rebels against Henry III.
- **Late medieval palace**: in the late fourteenth century **John of Gaunt**, Duke of Lancaster, built a magnificent **great hall** and grand private rooms, turning a fortress into a princely residence. It then passed to the Crown.

So when Elizabeth came to the throne, Kenilworth was already a royal castle with a long history of **power and display**.

### 3. People: Elizabeth and Robert Dudley

- **Robert Dudley** was Elizabeth's closest **favourite**: they had known each other since youth, and in the early 1560s many thought she might marry him. The death of his wife, **Amy Robsart**, in 1560 caused a scandal that made marriage politically impossible.
- In **1563** Elizabeth **granted** Kenilworth to Dudley, and in **1564** made him **Earl of Leicester**. This is **patronage** at the highest level: a great castle and estates given to bind a favourite to her, and to give him the wealth and status of a great noble.
- Dudley was a **Privy Councillor**, **Master of the Horse**, a leading **Protestant** and a patron of Puritan preachers and of a company of players (**Leicester's Men**). Kenilworth was his **power base** in the Midlands.
- **Other people** connected to the site: the builders and craftsmen of the 1570s; the courtiers and servants who came with the queen on **progress**; local people who watched and took part in the entertainments; writers who described the 1575 visit (a letter by **Robert Langham**, a member of Dudley's household, and published texts by the poet **George Gascoigne**).

### 4. Dudley's changes: from castle to palace

Dudley rebuilt much of the castle, mostly in the **late 1560s and early 1570s**, ready for the queen's visits.

| Feature | What Dudley did | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| **Leicester's Building** | A new tall block of private **apartments for the queen**, built in the early 1570s, with very large **windows** | Built to house Elizabeth in comfort and privacy; huge glass windows displayed **wealth** (glass was expensive) and **fashion**; it overlooked the mere and the park |
| **The Great Tower** | The Norman keep was remodelled, with larger windows and new rooms | Old **military** building reused for **comfort and display**; continuity of an ancient symbol of power |
| **Gaunt's great hall and apartments** | Kept and refurbished for feasting | Continuity: medieval splendour reused for Elizabethan **court culture** |
| **The gatehouse** | A grand new gatehouse built as the main entrance on the north side | Turned the approach into a **show** of status; decorated with Dudley's emblems (the bear and ragged staff) |
| **The privy garden** | A formal garden below the keep, with walks, arbours, obelisks, an aviary of birds and a marble fountain, described in Langham's letter (recreated by English Heritage in recent years) | Elizabethan love of **gardens**, symmetry and luxury; a private space to entertain the queen |
| **The park and the mere** | Hunting grounds and the great lake | Hunting and water pageants: places for **entertainment** rather than defence |
| **Stables and service buildings** | Large stables and service ranges, used for the huge household and the queen's visits | The scale of the court on **progress** |

**Comparisons.** Kenilworth fits the wider Elizabethan fashion for great houses (**prodigy houses**) such as Burghley, Longleat and Hardwick Hall, with large windows, symmetry, long views and gardens, built to impress and often to host the queen. But Kenilworth was unusual because it was a **medieval castle converted**, not a new house: Dudley joined an ancient symbol of power to the newest fashions.

### 5. Elizabeth's visit of July 1575

Elizabeth visited Kenilworth several times, but the **visit of July 1575**, lasting **19 days** as part of her summer **progress**, was the most famous and lavish entertainment of her reign.

**Entertainments**
- She was greeted with speeches, music and a show on the **mere**: the "**Lady of the Lake**" appeared on a floating island and offered the castle to her.
- Further water pageants, including a singer playing the role of Arion riding on a dolphin.
- **Fireworks**, **feasts** and music; **hunting** in the park; **bear-baiting**; dancing; a country wedding and a traditional play performed by men from **Coventry**.
- Poems and speeches praised Elizabeth as a goddess-like queen.

**Why did Dudley spend so much?**
1. **Gratitude and patronage**: to thank the queen for his rise, and to keep her **favour** in competition with rivals at court.
2. **Marriage hopes**: some entertainments seem to have hinted at **marriage**. A show urging the queen to marry was prepared but **not performed**, perhaps because she disliked its message. Dudley may still have hoped, but by 1575 marriage between them was unlikely.
3. **Status**: to show the court and the region his **wealth**, power and taste as a great nobleman.
4. **Political message**: displays praised Elizabeth as a **Protestant** queen; Dudley led the court's Protestant, anti-Spanish group.

**The outcome.** The queen enjoyed the visit, but it did not change her mind about marriage. In **1578** Dudley secretly married **Lettice Knollys**, which made Elizabeth furious. He remained in favour, commanded the English army in the **Netherlands** (1585–87) and at **Tilbury** in 1588, and died later that year.

### 6. Functions over time

| Period | Main functions |
|---|---|
| Norman and medieval | **Fortress**, royal stronghold, centre of lordship; defended by walls and the mere |
| Late medieval (John of Gaunt) | **Princely residence** and centre of a great household |
| Elizabethan (Dudley) | **Palace**, place of display and **entertainment**, a stage for royal **progresses**, Dudley's regional **power base** |

### 8. Change and continuity

**From earlier periods**
- **Continuity**: the Norman Great Tower, the curtain walls, the mere and **Gaunt's great hall** survived and were reused; the castle remained a symbol of **power** and a centre of **lordship** in the Midlands.
- **Change**: defence gave way to **comfort and display**; walls and keep had big windows cut into them; the mere became a stage; the new apartments, gatehouse and garden reflected **Renaissance** fashions.

**During Elizabeth's reign**
- Before 1563 it was a little-used Crown castle; after the grant it became a lavish **noble residence**.
- Building work in the late 1560s and early 1570s prepared it for the queen; the **1575 visit** marked its peak.
- After Dudley's death (1588) its importance declined. (Much later, after the Civil War, it was deliberately damaged, which is why it is a ruin today; this is beyond the period but explains what survives.)

### 9. Planning the 16-mark site essay

1. **Answer the statement directly** in the first sentence, with a judgement.
2. **For**: two site features (Leicester's Building, the 1575 visit, the garden, the grant of 1563), each linked to a development (patronage, marriage politics, fashion).
3. **Against or qualifying**: other purposes or factors (Dudley's own status, continuity of a medieval power base, Protestant politics).
4. **Change and continuity**: what changed from the medieval fortress and what stayed.
5. **Judgement**: which explanation is strongest and **why**, carried through.

::: terms
- **Historic environment**: a specified real place studied to understand how it connects to the events and developments of the period.
- **Great mere**: the large artificial lake around Kenilworth, once a defence and later a stage for pageants.
- **Great Tower**: the Norman keep at Kenilworth, remodelled with larger windows by Dudley.
- **Leicester's Building**: the tall block of apartments Dudley built for Elizabeth in the early 1570s, with huge windows.
- **Privy garden**: Dudley's formal garden below the keep, with arbours, an aviary and a marble fountain.
- **Progress**: the queen's summer journey with her court to stay at the houses of nobles and gentry.
- **Pageant**: a staged show with costumes, speeches and music, like the Lady of the Lake on the mere in 1575.
- **Patronage**: rewarding loyalty with land, titles and offices, as Elizabeth did by granting Kenilworth to Dudley.
- **Prodigy house**: a showy Elizabethan great house built to display wealth and host the queen.
- **Bear and ragged staff**: the heraldic badge of the Dudleys, used in decoration at Kenilworth.
:::

::: method
**Paper 2 Section B, Question 4 (16).** "'[Statement].' How far does a study of Kenilworth Castle support this statement? Explain your answer. You should refer to Kenilworth Castle and your contextual knowledge."

1. Find the statement's **key concept** (purpose, patronage, change, Golden Age, Elizabeth's power) and decide your judgement.
2. Start each paragraph with a **site feature** (the 1563 grant, Leicester's Building, the gatehouse, the garden, the 1575 visit, Gaunt's hall) and move to **contextual knowledge** (court, patronage, progresses, marriage, Golden Age).
3. Include at least one paragraph that **challenges or qualifies** the statement.
4. Use **change, continuity, causation and consequence** explicitly.
5. Keep the judgement running through, then end with a short conclusion saying **why** one view is stronger.
6. Do not just describe the castle, and do not write about Elizabeth without the site.

::: example Site essay: impressing Elizabeth (16)
"Kenilworth Castle was changed mainly to impress Elizabeth I." How far does a study of Kenilworth Castle support this statement? Explain your answer. You should refer to Kenilworth Castle and your contextual knowledge. (16)

**Kenilworth strongly supports the statement.** After Elizabeth granted the castle to **Robert Dudley** in 1563, he rebuilt it to receive her. **Leicester's Building**, built in the early 1570s, was a block of private apartments for the queen with huge, expensive **glass windows** overlooking the mere. The **privy garden**, with its aviary and marble fountain, was made for her pleasure. All this prepared for the **19-day visit of July 1575**, with the Lady of the Lake on the mere, fireworks, hunting and feasts. This fits the wider Elizabethan world, where nobles competed to host the queen on **progress** to keep her **favour**, the basis of **patronage** at court.

**But impressing Elizabeth was linked to other aims.** Dudley wanted to show his **status** as a great noble to the court and the Midlands: the new **gatehouse** bore his emblem, the bear and ragged staff, and Kenilworth was his regional power base. He may also have hoped to press his **marriage** suit: a show urging her to marry was prepared in 1575, though not performed. And the changes reflect **fashion** across England, seen in prodigy houses such as Burghley and Longleat.

**Continuity** also matters: Dudley reused the Norman Great Tower and **John of Gaunt's** great hall, so the castle's old meaning as a symbol of power continued.

**Judgement.** The statement is largely supported: the timing and design of the changes centred on the queen's visits. But impressing her was a means to other ends (favour, status, perhaps marriage), so "mainly to impress Elizabeth" is true of the method more than the motive. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Site essay: patronage (16)
"Elizabeth's power depended on the loyalty of favourites such as Robert Dudley." How far does a study of Kenilworth Castle support this statement? Explain your answer. You should refer to Kenilworth Castle and your contextual knowledge. (16)

**Kenilworth shows how Elizabeth used patronage to build loyalty.** By granting the castle to Dudley in **1563** and making him **Earl of Leicester** in 1564, she gave her favourite the wealth and standing of a great noble. In return, Dudley poured money into rebuilding Kenilworth and staged the lavish **1575** entertainments in her honour, praising her as a near-divine queen. He served her as a Privy Councillor, commanded the army in the **Netherlands** (1585–87) and at **Tilbury** in 1588. This shows that favourites could be powerful supporters.

**But the site also shows that power flowed from Elizabeth, not to her.** Dudley's spending at Kenilworth was a sign of **dependence**: he needed her favour. The visit did not change her mind about marriage, and when he secretly married Lettice Knollys in 1578 she was furious, yet he kept his place only because she allowed it. Elsewhere her power rested on able ministers like **Burghley** and **Walsingham**, on managing **Parliament** and factions, and on her **image**. The fall of **Essex** in 1601 shows that a favourite who lost patronage could turn against her, and failed.

**Judgement.** Kenilworth shows that favourites like Dudley strengthened her rule, but the relationship was one of **dependence on her**. Her power rested more on her control of patronage than on any favourite's loyalty. (Level 4.)
:::

::: example Explain what was important (8)
Explain what was important about Elizabeth's visit to Kenilworth in 1575. (8)

The visit was important first for **patronage and favour**. Dudley spent enormous sums on 19 days of entertainments (the Lady of the Lake, water pageants, fireworks, hunting and feasts) to thank Elizabeth for granting him Kenilworth and to keep her favour against rivals at court.

Second, it was important for the **marriage question**. Some entertainments hinted at marriage, and a show urging her to marry was prepared but not performed. The visit showed that, even in 1575, her marriage remained a live political issue, but also that she would not be pushed.

Third, it was important as a display of **Elizabethan court culture and image**. Poems and pageants presented her as a goddess-like queen; the new apartments with their huge windows and the privy garden displayed the wealth and fashion of the age. The surviving accounts (Langham's letter, Gascoigne's texts) made it famous as the greatest entertainment of the reign. (Level 4.)
:::
