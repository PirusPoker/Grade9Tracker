**Section B (19th Century Prose): A Christmas Carol**

**21** You should use the extract below and your knowledge of the whole novel to answer this question.

Write about how Dickens presents attitudes to poverty at different points in the novel. [40]

In your response you should:
- refer to the extract and the novel as a whole
- show your understanding of characters and events in the novel
- refer to the contexts of the novel.

*Stave One. Two gentlemen collecting for charity have come into Scrooge's counting-house.*

"At this festive season of the year, Mr. Scrooge," said the gentleman, taking up a pen, "it is more than usually desirable that we should make some slight provision for the Poor and Destitute, who suffer greatly at the present time. Many thousands are in want of common necessaries; hundreds of thousands are in want of common comforts, sir."

"Are there no prisons?" asked Scrooge.

"Plenty of prisons," said the gentleman, laying down the pen again.

"And the Union workhouses?" demanded Scrooge. "Are they still in operation?"

"They are. Still," returned the gentleman, "I wish I could say they were not."

"The Treadmill and the Poor Law are in full vigour, then?" said Scrooge.

"Both very busy, sir."

"Oh! I was afraid, from what you said at first, that something had occurred to stop them in their useful course," said Scrooge. "I'm very glad to hear it."

"Under the impression that they scarcely furnish Christian cheer of mind or body to the multitude," returned the gentleman, "a few of us are endeavouring to raise a fund to buy the Poor some meat and drink, and means of warmth. We choose this time, because it is a time, of all others, when Want is keenly felt, and Abundance rejoices. What shall I put you down for?"

"Nothing!" Scrooge replied.

"You wish to be anonymous?"

"I wish to be left alone," said Scrooge. "Since you ask me what I wish, gentlemen, that is my answer. I don't make merry myself at Christmas and I can't afford to make idle people merry. I help to support the establishments I have mentioned: they cost enough; and those who are badly off must go there."

"Many can't go there; and many would rather die."

"If they would rather die," said Scrooge, "they had better do it, and decrease the surplus population."

Write one continuous answer (about 45 minutes). It is marked here in two blocks. **This block marks your writing on the extract.**

- **13–15:** perceptive, evaluative analysis of the attitudes in the extract; precise comment on language and structure; context integrated to explain Dickens's purpose.
- **10–12:** thoughtful, developed analysis with apt references and relevant context.
- **7–9:** clear understanding of the attitudes, some analysis and some context.
- **4–6:** some understanding, mostly describing Scrooge's views.
- **1–3:** simple comments.

Indicative content:
- The gentleman's language is compassionate and specific ("Poor and Destitute", "want of common necessaries"), with the scale of suffering stressed by "thousands" and "hundreds of thousands".
- Scrooge answers with curt rhetorical questions about prisons, workhouses, the Treadmill and the Poor Law – institutions of punishment, not help; his sarcasm ("I'm very glad to hear it") shows contempt.
- The dialogue's structure: short, cold replies against longer, warmer speeches; his blunt "Nothing!" is the climax; his wish to be "left alone" shows isolation as a moral failing.
- "Decrease the surplus population" echoes Malthusian ideas that the poor were a burden; Dickens puts the cruellest phrase of his age into Scrooge's mouth so readers will recoil from it.
- Personified "Want" and "Abundance" anticipate the allegorical children Ignorance and Want in Stave Three.
- Context: the 1834 Poor Law Amendment Act and the deliberately harsh workhouses; Dickens's own childhood experience of poverty and his anger after visiting a ragged school; the novella published in 1843 as an argument to middle-class readers.

**Section C (Unseen Poetry)**

Read the two poems, *Platform Four* and *Packing*. Both poems are about a young person leaving home.

**3.1** Write about the poem *Platform Four* and its effect on you.

You may wish to:
- consider what the poem is about and how it is organised
- consider the ideas the poet may have wanted us to think about
- consider the poet's choice of words, phrases and images and the effects they create
- consider how you respond to the poem.

**Platform Four**

- She has her father's way of standing:
- one hand in a pocket, one on the strap
- of a bag too heavy for what it holds.
- I watch her check the board again.

- Twelve minutes. I had planned a speech,
- something about the boiler, the post,
- the number for the doctor, love.
- Instead I ask her if she's eaten.

- The train comes in the way trains always come,
- as if it has been coming all her life.
- She hugs me with the bag still on,
- so that I hold her and the weight together.

- Then glass. Her palm laid flat against it,
- a small white star that slides away.
- I stand until the rails stop singing,
- then walk back, lighter, to the car.

*(written for this mock paper)*

(15)
---
AO1 and AO2 equally weighted; five bands of 3.
- **13–15:** sensitive, evaluative response; perceptive analysis of language and structure; a personal response grounded in the text.
- **10–12:** thoughtful, developed response; clear analysis of several methods.
- **7–9:** clear understanding of the poem; some analysis of methods with relevant references.
- **4–6:** some understanding; general comments on methods.
- **1–3:** simple comments on content.

Indicative content:
- A parent (the speaker) sees a daughter off on a train; four regular four-line stanzas move through waiting, the train's arrival, the hug and the departure – a tight, controlled form for controlled feeling.
- The opening detail of her "father's way of standing" suggests inheritance and perhaps absence; the heavy bag "too heavy for what it holds" hints at emotional weight.
- The list of practical advice ending in "love" shows how hard it is to say the important thing; the anticlimax "if she's eaten" is touching and true to life.
- The train "coming all her life" suggests this departure was always inevitable; the hug "with the bag still on" makes the speaker hold "her and the weight together" – love and the burden of letting go in one image.
- "Then glass" – a blunt minor sentence marks the separation; the palm as "a small white star" is tender and distancing, sliding into the past.
- The ending is ambiguous: "lighter" may mean relief, freedom, or emptiness; strong answers explore more than one reading and give a personal response.

**Packing**

- I fold the room into a dozen boxes:
- the posters curling, letting go their tape,
- the cups for a sport I gave up years ago,
- the shoebox of old birthday cards I keep.

- My mother hovers in the doorway, asking
- which things are going, which of them can stay,
- as if a thing left here could be a promise
- that I'll come back to claim it one day.

- I tape the last box shut. The walls look wider,
- pale squares where all my pictures used to hang,
- the way a field looks once the crop is gathered:
- not empty, only resting, till the rain.
