::: q 4-5
**1.** Read this extract from Act 1 Scene 7 of *Macbeth*. Macbeth is alone, deciding whether to kill Duncan.

- He's here in double trust:
- First, as I am his kinsman and his subject,
- Strong both against the deed; then, as his host,
- Who should against his murderer shut the door,
- Not bear the knife myself. Besides, this Duncan
- Hath borne his faculties so meek, hath been
- So clear in his great office, that his virtues
- Will plead like angels, trumpet-tongued, against
- The deep damnation of his taking-off;
- And pity, like a naked new-born babe,
- Striding the blast, or heaven's cherubin, horsed
- Upon the sightless couriers of the air,
- Shall blow the horrid deed in every eye,
- That tears shall drown the wind. I have no spur
- To prick the sides of my intent, but only
- Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself
- And falls on th'other.

(a) State the three relationships Macbeth says he has with Duncan that should stop him committing the murder. (3)

(b) Write down the phrase in which Macbeth names the only reason he has for the murder. (1)

(c) Explain what Macbeth means by "falls on th'other". (1)

(5)
---
(a) Kinsman (1), subject (1), host (1).

(b) "Vaulting ambition" (1).

(c) A rider who leaps too eagerly lands on the far side of the horse — his ambition will overshoot and cause his own downfall (1).

**2.** (a) Explain what the Jacobean audience understood by the Divine Right of Kings. (2)

(b) Give two reasons why *Macbeth* would have pleased King James I. (2)

(4)
---
(a) The monarch was chosen by God (1), so killing a king was a sin against God and the natural order, not just a crime (1).

(b) Any two: James claimed descent from Banquo, who is shown as loyal and whose line inherits the throne; James wrote a book on witchcraft and the witches confirm his fears; the play shows regicide punished by chaos and divine justice; it echoes the Gunpowder Plot of 1605 (2).

::: q 4-5
**3.** Read this extract from Stave 1 of *A Christmas Carol*.

Oh! But he was a tight-fisted hand at the grindstone, Scrooge! a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous, old sinner! Hard and sharp as flint, from which no steel had ever struck out generous fire; secret, and self-contained, and solitary as an oyster. The cold within him froze his old features, nipped his pointed nose, shrivelled his cheek, stiffened his gait; made his eyes red, his thin lips blue; and spoke out shrewdly in his grating voice. A frosty rime was on his head, and on his eyebrows, and his wiry chin. He carried his own low temperature always about with him; he iced his office in the dog-days; and didn't thaw it one degree at Christmas.

(a) Write down two adjectives from the list Dickens uses to describe Scrooge's attitude to money. (2)

(b) Name the four spirits that visit Scrooge, in the order they appear. (2)

(c) State what Marley's chain is made of and what it represents. (1)

(5)
---
(a) Any two of: squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous (2).

(b) Marley's ghost; the Ghost of Christmas Past; the Ghost of Christmas Present; the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come (2; one mark for three in order).

(c) Cash-boxes, keys, padlocks, ledgers and steel purses — the burden of a life spent on money and the punishment he forged for himself "link by link" (1).

::: q 6-7
**4.** Look again at the lines from "And pity, like a naked new-born babe" to "tears shall drown the wind" in the Macbeth extract.

How does Shakespeare use language here to show the strength of Macbeth's conscience? Comment on at least two images and the effect of the verse. (6)
---
Award 5–6 for two or more images analysed with precise effect and a comment on form; 3–4 for clear explanation of one or two images; 1–2 for identification with simple comment.

Indicative content: pity is personified as a "naked new-born babe" — the most vulnerable, innocent image possible, yet it is "striding the blast", so weakness becomes overwhelming force; the heavenly imagery ("angels, trumpet-tongued", "heaven's cherubin") frames the murder as a sin judged by God, not merely a crime; the hyperbole of "tears shall drown the wind" shows Macbeth already imagining universal grief; the long, run-on sentence with the sense tumbling across line endings (enjambment) mirrors thoughts that will not stop, before the blunt caesura "I have no spur" pulls him up short; the virtues "plead" like advocates in a courtroom, so Macbeth is already on trial in his own mind.

::: q 6-7
**5.** Later in Act 1 Scene 7, Lady Macbeth responds to Macbeth's decision to "proceed no further in this business".

Explain how she persuades him and what this scene shows about their relationship at this point in the play. Use references to the play. (6)
---
Award 5–6 for a clear, well-supported explanation of both method and relationship; 3–4 for explanation of one with some support; 1–2 for general comments.

Indicative content: she attacks his manhood — "When you durst do it, then you were a man"; she uses the shocking image of dashing out the brains of her own baby to prove her commitment; she accuses him of cowardice, "the poor cat i' th' adage"; she provides the plan (drugging the grooms) that removes his practical fear; Macbeth's admiring "Bring forth men-children only" shows he is impressed and dominated; at this point she leads and he follows, a reversal of Jacobean expectations that the audience would find unnatural — foreshadowing how the balance shifts after the murder, when he acts alone and she is shut out ("Be innocent of the knowledge, dearest chuck").

::: q 6-7
**6.** How does Dickens use language in the Stave 1 extract to present Scrooge? Comment on the list, the similes and the extended metaphor of cold. (6)
---
Award 5–6 for analysis of all three methods with precise effects; 3–4 for clear comment on two; 1–2 for simple identification.

Indicative content: the asyndetic list of participles "squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching" piles up harsh consonants to make Scrooge's greed relentless and physical, ending in the moral judgement "old sinner"; the simile "hard and sharp as flint" suggests he could produce warmth (fire) but chooses not to, so his coldness is a choice; "solitary as an oyster" suggests something closed, hard and hidden — with the hint of a pearl inside, foreshadowing his redemption; the extended metaphor of cold that "froze", "nipped", "shrivelled" and "stiffened" him shows greed has physically deformed him, and "he iced his office in the dog-days" makes him a source of cold to others; the narrator's exclamatory, mocking tone ("Oh!") invites the reader to laugh at him rather than fear him.
:::

::: q 6-7
**7.** Explain how Dickens uses the Cratchit family to show his message about poverty and family. Refer to at least two moments in the novella. (6)
---
Award 5–6 for a clear explanation linked to Dickens's purpose with well-chosen references; 3–4 for explanation with some references; 1–2 for narrative retelling.

Indicative content: the Cratchits have almost nothing — Bob's "fifteen bob a week", the pudding "like a speckled cannon-ball", clothes "twice-turned" — yet the Christmas dinner in Stave 3 is full of warmth, showing that happiness does not depend on money; Tiny Tim's "God bless us every one" contrasts with Scrooge's "surplus population"; the empty stool and the crutch "carefully preserved" in Stave 4 show the human cost of Scrooge's meanness; Mrs Cratchit's anger at the toast to Scrooge shows the poor are not passive; Dickens uses the family to make poverty a matter of individual conscience — Scrooge's raise for Bob and becoming a second father to Tim in Stave 5 show that the rich can change lives directly.
:::

::: q 8-9
**8.** Starting with the extract from Act 1 Scene 7, explore how Shakespeare presents ambition as a destructive force in *Macbeth*.

Write about how Shakespeare presents ambition in this extract and in the play as a whole. (10)
---
Mark against these levels. 9–10: a convincing, critical and exploratory argument with judicious, integrated quotation from the extract and the whole play, precise analysis of methods and a considered link to context. 7–8: a thoughtful, developed response with well-chosen references and clear analysis of methods, some context. 5–6: a clear explanation of ideas with relevant references and some comment on method. 3–4: some understanding with references, mostly narrative. 1–2: a simple response with limited references.

Indicative content: in the extract ambition is the only "spur" and is condemned in the same breath — Macbeth knows it "o'erleaps itself" and predicts his own fall; earlier, "Stars, hide your fires" shows ambition as something shameful that needs darkness; Lady Macbeth's ambition is sharper ("the nearest way") and drives him past his conscience; once crowned, ambition becomes fear — "To be thus is nothing, / But to be safely thus" — leading to Banquo's murder and the pointless slaughter of the Macduffs; the outcome is emptiness, "Nought's had, all's spent", and the "Tomorrow, and tomorrow" speech where life is "a tale told by an idiot"; Banquo's contrasting restraint shows ambition itself is not the crime but what one will do for it; context — the Divine Right of Kings means ambition against a rightful king overturns the natural order, and the play shows Scotland itself sick until order is restored under Malcolm.

::: q 8-9
**9.** How does Dickens present the idea that people can change in *A Christmas Carol*?

Write about how Scrooge changes and the methods Dickens uses to present this change. (12)
---
Mark against these levels. 11–12: a convincing, exploratory argument that traces change across the whole novella, analyses methods (structure, symbolism, narrative voice, contrast) with judicious quotation and links to Dickens's purpose and context. 8–10: a thoughtful, developed response with well-chosen references and clear analysis of method. 5–7: a clear explanation with relevant references and some comment on method. 3–4: some understanding, mostly retelling. 1–2: simple comments.

Indicative content: structure — five staves like a carol, with the three spirits taking Scrooge through past, present and future so that change is shown as a process of memory, observation and fear; Stave 1 establishes what must change through the cold imagery and "Are there no prisons?"; the Ghost of Christmas Past reawakens feeling — Scrooge's tears at his younger self and Fezziwig, "I should like to be able to say a word or two to my clerk"; Present brings Ignorance and Want and turns his own words back on him; Yet to Come brings terror at the neglected grave; the weather and light imagery reverse in Stave 5 — "Golden sunlight; Heavenly sky" — and Scrooge is "as light as a feather"; the narrator's warm, comic voice and Scrooge's own promise "I will honour Christmas in my heart"; Dickens's purpose was to persuade the Victorian middle class that social responsibility was possible for anyone — the allegory works because even Scrooge can change, and the final line insists that the new Scrooge is permanent.
