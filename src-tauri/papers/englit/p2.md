::: q 4-5 3.2.1e
**3.** *An Inspector Calls* is set in 1912 and was first performed in 1945.

Explain one reason why Priestley chose to set the play before the two world wars. (3)
---
The audience of 1945 knows what the characters do not — two wars, the Depression, the sinking of the *Titanic* (1); so Birling's confident predictions are shown to be wrong, discrediting his individualist views (1); Priestley wanted the post-war audience to reject the old class-ridden society and build a fairer one, which the 1945 election was about (1).
:::

::: q 6-7 3.2.1c
**4.** Explain how Priestley uses the Inspector's final speech to deliver his message. Use at least two quotations. (6)
---
Award 5–6 for a clear explanation of the message with two or more quotations analysed for method; 3–4 for explanation with some quotation; 1–2 for general comment.

Indicative content: "there are millions and millions and millions of Eva Smiths and John Smiths" — repetition widens one girl into a whole class; "We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other" — the language of the church and of socialism, short declarative sentences with the weight of a sermon; "fire and blood and anguish" — a prophecy of the wars the audience has lived through, so the Inspector speaks with Priestley's hindsight; he leaves immediately, so the speech is addressed past the Birlings to the audience; the message is collective responsibility against Birling's "a man has to mind his own business".
:::

::: q 6-7 3.2.1d
**5.** Explain how Sheila changes during the play. Refer to at least two moments. (6)
---
Award 5–6 for a clear account of the change with well-chosen references and comment on Priestley's purpose; 3–4 for an account with some references; 1–2 for narrative.

Indicative content: at the start she is childish and pleased with her ring — "I think it's perfect. Now I really feel engaged"; her reaction to Eva's death is genuine — "these girls aren't cheap labour — they're people"; she accepts her guilt at Milwards and warns her mother, "you mustn't try to build up a kind of wall between us and that girl"; she sees through the Inspector's method — "he's giving us the rope — so that we'll hang ourselves"; she hands back the ring and refuses to pretend nothing happened; Priestley uses her as the model of the younger generation who can change, and the hope for 1945.
:::

::: q 6-7 3.2.2f 3.2.2g
**6.** How does Owen use language in *Exposure* to present the suffering of the soldiers? (6)
---
Award 5–6 for analysis of two or more methods with precise effects; 3–4 for clear comment on language; 1–2 for identification.

Indicative content: personification of the weather as the enemy — "merciless iced east winds that knive us", where "knive" turns the cold into a bayonet; the repeated refrain "But nothing happens" makes waiting itself the torture and mocks the idea of action; sibilance and onomatopoeia — "sudden successive flights of bullets streak the silence" — less deadly than the snow; ellipses and half-rhyme ("knive us / nervous") create unease and incompleteness; the soldiers' faith fades — "For love of God seems dying"; the final stanza's frozen burial party and "eyes are ice" complete the transformation of men into the landscape.
:::

::: q 6-7 3.2.3a
**7.** Read this poem.

**Allotment**

- My grandfather kept his summers in a shed:
- seed packets sorted by the month they'd need,
- a radio that only found the cricket,
- and string. There was always string.

- I used to think the work was in the digging.
- It was in the waiting: weeks of nothing showing
- while he walked the rows each evening anyway,
- reading the soil like a letter that hadn't come.

- The plot's gone back to grass now. Someone else
- has the key, the shed, the radio, the string.
- But when the first green nib of anything
- breaks ground in my own yard, I hear him say
- *there — patience — told you,* and I do not dig.
- I wait.

How does the poet present the speaker's feelings about their grandfather? (6)
---
Award 5–6 for a perceptive reading with two or more methods analysed and quotations well chosen; 3–4 for a clear reading with some analysis; 1–2 for simple comment.

Indicative content: the list of ordinary objects makes the memory concrete and affectionate, with the humour of "There was always string"; the metaphor "kept his summers in a shed" shows the grandfather as the keeper of seasons; the shift from "digging" to "waiting" is the lesson learned, and the simile "like a letter that hadn't come" gives his patience a note of hope or longing; loss is understated — "gone back to grass", the possessions listed again but now "someone else" has them; the italic voice shows the grandfather still speaks in the speaker's head; the final short line "I wait." enacts the inherited patience and turns grief into continuation.
:::

::: q 8-9 3.2.1d 3.2.1e
**8.** How does Priestley present the differences between the older and the younger generations in *An Inspector Calls*? (12)
---
Mark against these levels. 11–12: a convincing, exploratory argument with judicious quotation, precise analysis of dramatic methods and considered links to context. 8–10: a thoughtful, developed response with well-chosen references and clear analysis. 5–7: a clear explanation with relevant references and some comment on method. 3–4: some understanding, mostly retelling. 1–2: simple comments.

Indicative content: Mr Birling's individualism — "a man has to make his own way" — against Sheila's and Eric's growing sense of responsibility; Mrs Birling's refusal — "I accept no blame for it at all" — against Eric's "we did her in all right"; the older generation care about reputation ("there'll be a public scandal"), the younger about the girl; the ending splits them — the parents relieved when the Inspector may be a hoax, the children insisting "it doesn't much matter" whether he was real; stage directions and tone — the parents' condescension ("you're not the kind of father a chap could go to"); context — Priestley's 1945 audience is invited to side with the young, who represent the possibility of a fairer post-war society; Gerald as the young man who sides with the old.
:::

::: q 8-9 3.2.2p 3.2.2q
**9.** Compare how poets present the effects of conflict on individuals in *Remains* and in one other poem from the Power and Conflict cluster. (12)
---
Mark against these levels. 11–12: a convincing comparison sustained throughout, with judicious quotation from both poems, precise analysis of methods and considered context. 8–10: a thoughtful, developed comparison with well-chosen references and clear analysis. 5–7: a clear comparison with relevant references and some comment on method. 3–4: some comparison, mostly description. 1–2: simple comments on one or both poems.

Indicative content for *Remains*: colloquial voice ("legs it up the road") that fails to keep the memory casual; the shift from "we" to "I" as guilt isolates the speaker; the enduring image "his bloody life in my bloody hands" with its double meaning; the drink and drugs that "won't flush him out"; the ending outside the war, where the man is "here in my head". Likely partners: *War Photographer* (the private effect of witnessing, "spools of suffering", the detachment of the public); *Bayonet Charge* (the moment terror replaces patriotism, "King, honour, human dignity, etcetera"); *Poppies* (the effect on those left behind); *Exposure* (slow destruction by waiting). Strong answers compare methods — structure, voice, imagery — as well as ideas, and use context (Armitage's interviews with veterans; Duffy's photographer; Hughes's father) to explain rather than decorate.
