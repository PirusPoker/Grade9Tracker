**SECTION A (40 marks).** Read the extract below carefully, then answer all the questions in Section A.

*The extract is an original passage written for this mock. It is set in a terrace of houses in a Welsh town in the early 1950s.*

**The Bicycle**

**Lines 1–6**

Mr Ellery had lived at number fourteen Cardigan Terrace for thirty-one years, and for twenty-nine of them he had been a widower. He was a small, neat man who wore a collar and tie even to post a letter, and he kept his front step whitened with a pumice stone every Saturday morning, whatever the weather. Before he retired he had taught arithmetic at the boys' grammar school, and he still corrected the milkman's grammar under his breath.

**Lines 7–13**

The woman who took number twelve that spring was nothing like the Misses Harker, who had lived there before her and who had died within a month of each other, politely, as they had done everything. Mrs Pryce arrived on a Tuesday in a coal lorry, sitting up beside the driver with a cigarette in her mouth and a boy of about eight on her knee. She was perhaps thirty-five. Her hair was tied up in a green scarf, her coat was a man's, and when the lorry stopped she jumped down before the driver could come round to help her and began handing furniture off the back as though she had been doing it all her life. By evening a sign had appeared in her front window, painted in uneven red letters on the lid of a tea chest: BICYCLES MENDED. FAIR PRICES. ASK INSIDE.

**Lines 14–32**

Mr Ellery watched all this from behind the lace curtain in his front room, and he did not like it. He did not like the cigarette, and he did not like the scarf, and he liked the sign least of all. Cardigan Terrace was not a street for signs. It was a street of brass knockers and drawn blinds, where people nodded to one another and did not ask questions, and where the loudest noise on a Sunday was the clock in the hall. He had chosen it for that reason in 1922, when he and Edith were newly married and the trees along the pavement had been no thicker than a walking stick.

Now the trees were taller than the chimneys, and almost everyone he had nodded to was gone. The Bowens had gone to their daughter in Swansea. Old Mr Carver had gone into the hospital and not come out of it. The new people did not whiten their steps. They put their dustbins out on the wrong day and left their wireless sets playing with the windows open, so that dance music drifted down the terrace at all hours like the smell of frying. Some evenings he felt like a man standing on a shrinking island while the tide crept in from every side.

A bicycle shop, he told himself, was the last straw. Grease on the pavement. Boys loitering. Tools clanging after dark. He wrote a letter of complaint to the council that night, in his best copperplate, and read it through three times, and did not post it.

**Lines 33–60**

It was the boy who changed things, in the end. On a wet afternoon in May, Mr Ellery came home from the library to find him sitting on the low wall between their two gardens, crying in the steady, hopeless way of a child who has stopped expecting anyone to come.

"What on earth is the matter?" said Mr Ellery, more sharply than he meant.

The boy held up an exercise book. The page was a battlefield of crossings-out. Long division. Mr Ellery looked at it for a long moment, and something in his chest that had been folded away for a very long time opened a little, like a letter.

"You have forgotten to carry the one," he said. "Here. And here. Come inside out of the rain and I shall show you why it matters."

That was how it began. Every afternoon at four the boy, whose name was Danny, knocked at number fourteen with his satchel, and every afternoon Mr Ellery cleared the good cloth from the dining table and set out two pencils, sharpened to exactly the same length. He was strict. He did not allow guessing. But he found, rather to his surprise, that he laughed more in that hour than he had in the whole of the previous year, and that he had begun to look at the clock after lunch.

Mrs Pryce came to fetch Danny on the third day, wiping her hands on an oily rag.

"He says you're a terror," she said.

"I am," said Mr Ellery.

"Good," she said. "His dad was the same about sums." She did not say where Danny's father was now, and Mr Ellery, who knew the look of a widow when he saw one, did not ask.

She would hear nothing about payment. The following Saturday he found his own bicycle, the old black machine that had stood in his shed since before the war with two flat tyres and a chain like a rusted necklace, leaning against his front railings, oiled and gleaming, its tyres as hard as apples. A label hung from the handlebars: FOR SERVICES RENDERED. NO CHARGE.

**Lines 61–80**

He had not ridden a bicycle for twenty years. He stood looking at it for some time with his hands in his pockets, aware that the curtains of number nine were twitching and that he was, at this moment, very probably being laughed at.

Then he went indoors, fetched his bicycle clips from the drawer where they had lain beside Edith's gloves since 1924, and came out again.

The first few yards were terrible. The handlebars twisted under his hands as though they had opinions of their own, and he wobbled past number twelve with his elbows out and his mouth set in a thin line. Danny shouted something from the window. Mrs Pryce, in her doorway, put a hand over her mouth. But by the time he reached the corner the old balance had come back to him, the way a tune comes back, and he was going faster than was sensible, and the wind was in his face, and the tall trees of Cardigan Terrace were flickering past him one after another like the pages of a book being turned.

That evening he took the letter to the council out of his bureau, read it through once more, and dropped it, very deliberately, into the fire.

On Monday, a little after four, he heard the knock at the door, and found that he had been waiting for it since lunch.

Boys loitering."; the unposted letter, read "three times", hints that his anger is weaker than his loneliness or his politeness. Reward valid alternatives.

**A4 (continued).** (b) How does the writer use language **and** structure to create these impressions in lines 33–60?

You must refer to the language and structure used in the text, using relevant subject terminology where appropriate. (6)

In the real paper (a) and (b) form one 10-mark question; mark (b) in levels. 6: detailed, perceptive analysis of both language and structure (the organisation of events) with accurate terminology. 4–5: clear analysis of language and some comment on structure, mostly accurate terminology. 2–3: some comment on how language or the order of events creates effects. 1: a simple comment. 0: nothing creditable.

Indicative content. Language: "a battlefield of crossings-out" (metaphor: the boy's struggle feels like a war he is losing); the simile "opened a little, like a letter", which echoes the unposted letter and suggests feelings long sealed away; the pencils "sharpened to exactly the same length" (fairness, care, ritual); the very short sentences "He was strict. **A5.** To answer this question, read lines 61–80 and consider the passage as a whole.

A reader said: "By the end of the passage, Mr Ellery has become a completely different man."

To what extent do you agree with this view?

You should write about:
- your thoughts and feelings about how Mr Ellery is presented in lines 61–80 and in the passage as a whole
- how the writer has created these thoughts and feelings.

You must refer to the text to support your answer. (10)
---
Mark in levels. 9–10: a persuasive, sustained evaluation with an overview of the whole passage, convincing and well-chosen references, perceptive comment on how the writer creates thoughts and feelings. 7–8: a critical evaluation with clear engagement, well-selected references and some exploration of the writer's methods. 5–6: an evaluation of the text and its effects with appropriate references and some awareness of the whole passage. 3–4: a personal opinion with straightforward references and limited sense of the whole. 1–2: a simple opinion with basic reference. 0: nothing creditable. The key is a coherent stance, sustained.

Agree: he rides the bicycle despite the twitching curtains, so he no longer lets the street's opinion rule him; he takes the clips from beside Edith's gloves, symbolically letting the past be used again; the long, breathless final sentence of the ride ("and he was going faster ... and the wind ... and the tall trees") shows joy and release, unlike his earlier clipped fragments; the trees that once measured his losses now flicker past "like the pages of a book being turned", suggesting a new chapter; he burns the complaint "very deliberately", a decisive act after his earlier indecision; he now waits eagerly for the knock.

Disagree or qualify: he is still the same careful, formal man (he stands with his hands in his pockets, worries about being laughed at, keeps the letter in his bureau); the change is gradual and was prepared for (the unposted letter, his kindness to Danny, his strictness, which Mrs Pryce values); the "old balance had come back to him, the way a tune comes back", so this is a recovery of who he once was rather than a new man; the final line echoes "look at the clock after lunch" from lines 33–60, showing continuity. Strong answers may argue he has become more himself rather than different.
