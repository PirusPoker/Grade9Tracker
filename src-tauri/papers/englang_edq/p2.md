**SECTION A (40 marks).** Answer all the questions in Section A. Text A is printed below and Text B is printed in question A3.

**Text A**

*An original feature written for this mock in the style of a 21st-century newspaper article. Ruth Ashworth is an invented person.*

**"Up here, nobody can ring you": the retired midwife who flies with the dawn**

At ten past five on a still June morning, in a field outside Hebden Bridge, Ruth Ashworth is doing what she calls "the boring bit". She is checking fuel lines. Then she checks them again. Around her, six friends in fleeces and wellies are hauling a vast red-and-gold envelope across the wet grass, and the burner above her head roars like a dragon clearing its throat.

Ruth is sixty-three. She delivered babies at a Halifax hospital for thirty-four years, and she learned to fly a hot-air balloon at fifty-eight, "because my grandson said I wouldn't dare". Today she is attempting a solo crossing of the Pennines, from West Yorkshire to a farm near Clitheroe, to raise money for the hospice that cared for her husband, Geoff, in his final weeks. So far, sponsors have pledged more than £11,000.

"People think it's an adrenaline thing," she tells me, tightening a strap with fingers stiff from the cold. "It isn't. It's the quietest place I know. Up there, nobody can ring you and nobody needs you to do anything except pay attention."

Paying attention matters. A balloon cannot be steered; it can only be raised or lowered into winds that blow in different directions at different heights. A pilot must read the sky like a map, and the map keeps changing. Ruth's instructor, Dev Sharma, admits he was nervous when she announced the crossing. "The Pennines make their own weather," he says. "You can take off in sunshine and meet fog on the tops within twenty minutes."

At 5.47 the basket lifts. There is no jolt and no sense of speed: the field simply falls away, and her friends become small waving figures, and then dots.

Three hours later, I reach Ruth by radio. Her voice is crackly but delighted. Somewhere over the moors she flew into exactly the fog Dev had warned about ("like being inside a glass of milk") and for eleven long minutes she could see nothing but the rim of her own basket. She climbed slowly, burning fuel she had hoped to save, until she broke out into sunlight above a sea of white. "I've never seen anything like it," she says. "Just me, and the sun, and the tops of the clouds going on for ever. I cried. I'm not ashamed to say it. I thought: Geoff, you should see this."

She lands at 9.22 in a field of startled sheep, two miles short of her target, bumping thirty metres through the grass before the basket tips gently on to its side. The farmer, who has come out in his dressing gown, offers her a cup of tea.

Will she do it again? Ruth laughs, picking grass out of her hair. "Ask me after I've slept," she says. Then, after a moment: "Yes. Obviously yes."

(b) How much money had sponsors pledged for the crossing? (1)

(c) What did the farmer offer Ruth when she landed? (1)

::: q 6-7 2.2c
**A2.** Read Text A again. How does the writer try to show that Ruth's flight is both peaceful **and** dangerous?

You should comment on:
- what is said
- the use of language, tone and structure
- other ways the writer tries to show that the flight is peaceful and dangerous.

You must refer to the text to support your comments, using relevant subject terminology where appropriate. (10)
---
Mark in levels, best fit. 9–10: accurate and perceptive comments on a wide range of well-chosen details; detailed analysis of how language, tone and structure create both sides; subtleties noticed; terminology purposeful. 7–8: accurate comments on a good range of details with clear analysis of effects; terminology accurate. 5–6: comments on a range of relevant details and begins to explain how language and structure create effects. 3–4: selects some relevant details with straightforward comment. 1–2: identifies one or two details. 0: nothing creditable. Answers dealing with only one side (peaceful or dangerous) are unlikely to go above 6.

Peaceful: Ruth's own words ("the quietest place I know", "nobody can ring you"); the take-off with "no jolt and no sense of speed", the field that "simply falls away" (calm, dreamlike movement); the friends shrinking to "dots"; the view above the fog ("Just me, and the sun, and the tops of the clouds going on for ever", a list that slows the pace); her emotion and the address to Geoff; the gentle tipping of the basket; the comic, cosy ending (dressing gown, cup of tea).

Dangerous: repeated checking of fuel lines (short sentences: "Then she checks them again."); the burner that "roars like a dragon" (simile mixing threat and humour); the explanation that a balloon "cannot be steered"; the instructor's warning and admission of nerves (expert voice adds credibility); "read the sky like a map, and the map keeps changing"; the fog "like being inside a glass of milk" and the precise "eleven long minutes" seeing nothing; burning fuel she hoped to save; landing short and bumping thirty metres.

Structure and other ways: the headline quotation sets up the theme of peace; time stamps (5.10, 5.47, three hours later, 9.22) give a sense of a real, tense journey; the warning about fog is placed before the fog arrives, so the reader anticipates danger; the piece ends on laughter and "Obviously yes", leaving us admiring her; the tone is warm and lightly humorous, which balances the risk.

**A3.** Read Text B below.

**Text B**

*An original passage written for this mock in the style of a Victorian magazine account of a balloon ascent from the grounds of the Crystal Palace at Sydenham. The writer and the aeronaut, Mr Hartley, are invented.*

It was a little after four o'clock when Mr Hartley, the aeronaut, pronounced the gas sufficient, and bade the labourers who held the ropes stand ready. I confess that my courage, which had been considerable at luncheon, had by this hour grown somewhat smaller; and when I stepped into the car, which is no more than a wicker basket of the kind in which a laundress carries linen, I gripped its edge with a firmness I hoped the spectators would not observe.

At the word "Away!" the ropes were cast off together. I had expected a violent motion, and had prepared myself for it; but there was none whatever. The earth did not leave us; rather, it seemed that we stood still and the whole of Sydenham sank silently beneath our feet, the crowd shrinking first to a dark carpet, then to a stain, and the great Palace of glass to a toy such as one might buy for a shilling. The cheers of the multitude reached us faintly for some minutes, like the noise of the sea heard in a shell, and then ceased altogether.

I have been asked by many friends to describe the silence of the upper air, and I find that I cannot. It is not the silence of a library, nor of a church at night. It is a silence so entire that a man hears the ticking of his own watch as plainly as a clock upon the mantel, and fancies that he hears his own blood.

At a height which Mr Hartley reckoned at near two miles, we entered the cloud. In an instant the sun was gone, and we were wrapped in a cold grey vapour, which wetted our coats and hung in drops upon the cordage. The car began to turn slowly upon itself, and I own that I was seized by a most disagreeable sensation, as though we had been swallowed by some vast creature and were descending into its interior. Mr Hartley, however, continued to write in his note-book and to observe his instruments, and once remarked, without looking up, that the temperature had fallen eleven degrees.

Presently he threw out two bags of sand; and we rose, and rose, and burst at last into such a scene as I shall not forget while I live. Above us the sky was of the deepest and purest blue; below us, as far as the eye could reach in every direction, lay a boundless plain of snowy cloud, heaped into hills and valleys, upon which the sun shone with a brilliance that made the eyes ache. Our own shadow travelled upon it beneath us, ringed with colours like a rainbow. I could not speak. Mr Hartley, I observed, had laid down his pencil.

Of the descent I will say only that it was more rapid than I could have wished, and that we came to earth in a turnip field in Kent, where the car was dragged some forty yards before the anchor held, to the great delight of a crowd of farm boys and the less evident delight of the farmer. My hat I never saw again.

(a) What does the writer compare the car of the balloon to? (1)

(b) By how much had the temperature fallen when the balloon entered the cloud? (1)

(c) What did Mr Hartley throw out to make the balloon rise above the cloud? (1)

(3)
---
(a) A wicker basket of the kind a laundress uses to carry linen / a laundry basket (1).
(b) Eleven degrees (1).
(c) Two bags of sand / sandbags (ballast) (1).

::: q 6-7 2.2d
**A4.** Read Text B again.

"The writer presents the ascent as a frightening ordeal that he regrets having undertaken."

To what extent do you agree with this view?

You should comment on:
- what the writer says
- how the writer says it.

You must refer to the text to support your comments. (10)
---
Mark in levels (AO4, evaluation). 9–10: a persuasive, detailed evaluation that weighs the view, sustains a clear stance and supports it with convincing, well-selected references; perceptive on how the writer shapes the reader's response. 7–8: a critical evaluation with clear engagement and well-chosen references; explores how the writer creates effects. 5–6: an evaluation supported by a range of references with some comment on the writer's methods. 3–4: limited evaluation of the view with some straightforward references. 1–2: one or two basic details or a simple opinion. 0: nothing creditable.

Agree in part: he admits his courage had "grown somewhat smaller" and he gripped the basket; inside the cloud he felt "a most disagreeable sensation", and the metaphor of being "swallowed by some vast creature" makes the cloud monstrous; the car turning "slowly upon itself" is disorienting; the descent was "more rapid than I could have wished"; the dragging landing and lost hat.

Disagree (the stronger case): the fear is presented with self-mocking humour (courage "considerable at luncheon", hoping spectators would not notice), so he laughs at himself rather than regretting anything; the take-off is gentle and wondrous, with the earth sinking "silently" and the Palace becoming "a toy"; he is awed by the silence, which he says he cannot describe; the climax ("we rose, and rose, and burst at last into such a scene as I shall not forget while I live") is ecstatic, with rich colour imagery and the rainbow-ringed shadow; "I could not speak" and Mr Hartley laying down his pencil show shared wonder; the comic final line ("My hat I never saw again") ends lightly, not regretfully. The best answers note that the brief fear makes the wonder more powerful, and that the formal, measured Victorian style keeps the tone composed.

::: q 8-9 2.2f
**A6.** Both of these texts are about flying in a balloon above the clouds.

Compare:
- what the two writers say about the experience of being above the clouds
- how the writers show what this experience is like.

You must use the text to support your comments and make it clear which text you are referring to. (10)
---
Mark in levels (AO3). 9–10: a detailed, perceptive comparison of both what is experienced and how it is conveyed, with well-chosen references from both texts and sustained comparison. 7–8: a clear comparison exploring valid details and methods in both texts. 5–6: identifies a number of valid points of comparison with some comment on methods. 3–4: some valid details from both texts with simple comparison. 1–2: one or two basic details, perhaps from one text. 0: nothing creditable. Answers that treat the texts separately with no comparison cannot go above 4.

Similarities: in both, the balloon passes through cloud or fog that blots everything out (A's "inside a glass of milk", B's "cold grey vapour" and the swallowing metaphor) before bursting into sunshine; both describe a "sea"/"plain" of white cloud stretching to infinity ("going on for ever"; "as far as the eye could reach"); both writers are moved to silence or tears ("I cried"; "I could not speak"); both stress the quiet.

Differences: A reports Ruth's experience at second hand through radio quotations, so her voice is direct, informal and emotional ("I'm not ashamed to say it"), while B is a first-hand, formal, carefully crafted Victorian account with long balanced sentences and elaborate similes; Ruth's moment is personal (she thinks of her late husband), whereas B's is shared wonder between two men and almost spiritual; B gives more visual detail (deepest blue, snowy hills and valleys, the rainbow-ringed shadow) while A is brief and lets Ruth's words carry the feeling; in A the danger is practical (fuel, steering), in B the fear is imaginative (the "vast creature"); Mr Hartley's laying down of his pencil is a subtle structural signal of awe, while A uses the climax of the radio call.

**SECTION B (40 marks).** Answer both B1 and B2. For each task, 12 marks are for communication and organisation and 8 marks are for vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation. Think about the purpose and audience for your writing, and aim for about 300–400 words for each task.

**B1.** Your local council is considering ending its funding for the town's free summer festival, which includes music, food stalls and activities for children, in order to save money.

Write a letter to the council giving your views on this proposal. (20)
---
Mark AO5 out of 12 and AO6 out of 8, best fit.

AO5. 11–12: sophisticated grasp of purpose and the letter form; sustained awareness of the councillors as readers; register confidently adapted (formal, measured, persuasive); content ambitious and pertinent; ideas convincingly developed with a range of relevant detail; a shapely, sophisticated structure. 8–10: consistent grasp of purpose and form; secure sense of audience; register appropriate throughout; content well judged and detailed; ideas coherently developed; clear paragraphing and structure. 5–7: clear understanding of purpose and form; register mostly appropriate; content developed with reasons given; some shape. 3–4: some awareness of purpose, form and audience; some reasons; limited development. 1–2: basic awareness; thin content; simple sequencing. 0: nothing creditable.

AO6. 8: varied sentence structures used effectively; virtually all sentences controlled; confident, accurate range of punctuation; virtually all spelling correct; tense and agreement secure; ambitious, precise vocabulary. 6–7: varied sentences for effect; secure control; accurate range of punctuation; secure spelling; ambitious vocabulary. 4–5: some variety; control mostly secure; punctuation mostly accurate; most spelling correct. 2–3: some variety and control; some range of punctuation; spelling usually accurate. 1: limited range and control. 0: nothing creditable.

Good answers: open with a clear, courteous statement of position; use a salutation and sign-off appropriate to a formal letter without wasting time on addresses; weigh the council's need to save money (concession) before rebutting it; use specific, plausible benefits (local businesses, community, children's free activities, young people with nowhere to go) and perhaps suggest alternatives (sponsorship, a small charge, volunteers); end with a clear request or call to action. Either view is acceptable.

**B2.** You have been asked to give a talk to Year 10 students at your school/college called "One thing everyone should try before they leave school".

Write what you would say in your talk. (20)
---
Mark AO5 out of 12 and AO6 out of 8, using the same level descriptors as B1, judging purpose and form against a spoken talk to a peer audience.

Good answers: address the audience directly and establish a lively but appropriate register (spoken, engaging, not slangy); choose one specific activity and make a convincing case for it with anecdote, concrete detail and benefits; anticipate objections ("I know what you're thinking...") and answer them; use features of spoken rhetoric for clarity (signposting, rhetorical questions, a memorable triple, a repeated phrase); end with a direct invitation or a strong final line. A talk that drifts into a list of several activities, or into a story with no persuasive purpose, will usually stay in the middle bands for AO5.
