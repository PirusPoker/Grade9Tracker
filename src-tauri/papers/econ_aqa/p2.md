**15.** Read Figure 1.

**Figure 1 — Borrowing gets dearer**

Over eighteen months the central bank of an economy raised its base rate from 0.5% to 5%. Banks passed the rises on: the typical interest rate on a five-year business loan went from about 3% to about 8%. A survey of firms found that many had postponed plans to buy new machinery or open new sites. Firms with large existing loans on variable rates reported that interest payments were taking a much bigger share of their revenue. At the same time, some cash-rich firms said they were happy to leave money in the bank, where it now earned a worthwhile return.

Using Figure 1, analyse how the rise in interest rates may affect firms' decisions to borrow and invest. (6)
---
AO2 = 2, AO3 = 4. Levels of response.

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 5–6 | Full analysis: one or two developed, logical chains from higher interest rates to firms' borrowing, saving and investment decisions, using Figure 1. |
| 2 | 3–4 | A chain is started but not fully developed; application mostly relevant; some use of Figure 1. |
| 1 | 1–2 | Isolated points (e.g. "loans cost more") with no chain; little use of the context. |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing worthy of credit. |

Indicative content:
- Cost of borrowing: the loan rate rose from about 3% to 8%, so the interest on a new loan is more than two and a half times as high → fewer investment projects are expected to earn enough to cover the cost → firms postpone machinery or new sites (as the survey found).
- Existing variable-rate debt: higher interest payments reduce profit → less retained profit to fund investment → possible cuts in spending or jobs.
- Opportunity cost of using savings: with 5% available on deposits, cash-rich firms may prefer to save rather than invest in risky projects.
- Expectations: higher rates reduce consumer spending → firms expect lower sales → even less reason to expand capacity.
- Wider effects: less investment → lower productivity and growth in the long run.

**21.** Read Figure 2.

**Figure 2 — Sharing out the income**

In the UK, income before taxes and benefits is spread very unequally: the richest fifth of households earn many times as much as the poorest fifth. After income tax, National Insurance and cash benefits, the gap narrows considerably, but wealth, such as property, shares and pensions, is spread even more unequally than income.

Some politicians argue for higher income tax rates on the highest earners, with the extra revenue spent on benefits and public services. They say this is fair and that high earners spend a smaller share of their income, so the economy would lose little spending.

Others warn that higher rates might lead some high earners to work less, move abroad or find ways to avoid the tax, so that the extra revenue could be smaller than expected. They argue that inequality is better tackled by raising skills in poorer areas, raising the minimum wage, or taxing wealth rather than income.

Using Figure 2, assess whether raising income tax rates on high earners is the best way to reduce income inequality in the UK. (9)
---
AO2 = 3, AO3 = 6. Levels of response.

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 7–9 | Well-developed analysis of how the policy would redistribute income AND of its limits or of alternatives, using Figure 2; a justified conclusion that follows from the argument. Accurate terms (progressive, redistribution, disposable income, incentives). |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some analysis, but partly developed or largely one-sided; some use of Figure 2; a conclusion that is only partly supported. |
| 1 | 1–3 | Descriptive or isolated points; assertion rather than judgement; little use of the context. |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing worthy of credit. |

Indicative content — for:
- A more progressive tax takes a larger share from the top, directly narrowing the gap in disposable income; revenue can fund benefits and public services (health, education) that are worth more, relative to income, to poorer households — Figure 2 shows taxes and benefits already narrow the gap considerably.
- Little loss of spending if high earners have a lower propensity to spend.

Indicative content — against or limits:
- Disincentive effects: less work, emigration, avoidance → revenue may be lower than expected, so less to redistribute.
- It does not touch wealth, which Figure 2 says is even more unequal; a wealth or property tax may do more.
- It treats the symptom; skills and training raise the earning power of the poor (a supply-side approach) and may reduce inequality more permanently, though slowly; a higher minimum wage helps low earners directly but may cost some jobs.
- Conflict with other objectives, e.g. growth, if investment and enterprise are discouraged.

Judgement: e.g. it is an effective short-run tool if rates are not so high that avoidance soars, but combining it with skills policy is better for the long run. Credit any supported conclusion.

**Section B**

**Item A — Inflation and interest rates**

The UK government sets the Bank of England a target of 2% for inflation, measured by the Consumer Price Index. The Bank's main tool is Bank Rate, which affects the interest rates that banks charge borrowers and pay savers. Table 4 shows invented figures for four years in an economy with the same kind of target.

**Table 4 — Inflation, interest rates and unemployment**

| | CPI inflation (%) | Bank Rate (%) | Unemployment rate (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 2.5 | 0.5 | 4.0 |
| Year 2 | 9.0 | 2.0 | 3.8 |
| Year 3 | 6.5 | 4.5 | 4.2 |
| Year 4 | 3.0 | 4.5 | 4.8 |

In Year 2 inflation jumped when world energy and food prices rose sharply, at a time when consumer spending was already recovering strongly. The central bank raised Bank Rate over two years. Critics said it acted too late; others said that rates cannot do much about rising world energy prices, and that higher rates would hurt borrowers and slow the economy.

**Item B — Winners and losers**

Households with variable-rate mortgages saw their monthly payments rise by hundreds of pounds, leaving less to spend in shops. Savers earned more interest, though in Year 3 the interest was still below the rate of inflation. Firms delayed investment, and growth slowed. The government also faced higher interest payments on its own debt, leaving less to spend on public services. Some economists argued that the government should help by raising taxes or cutting its spending, while others said supply-side policies to improve energy supply would work better in the long run.

**22.** Define the term "demand-pull inflation". (2)

**23.** In Year 3 a saver deposits £8,000 in an account paying 4.5% interest a year.

Using Table 4, calculate by how much the saver's money has gained or lost in purchasing power after one year, in pounds. (3)

(5)
---
**22.** AO1 = 2. A complete definition for 2 marks: a sustained rise in the general price level (1) caused by total demand (spending) in the economy growing faster than the economy's ability to supply goods and services — "too much money chasing too few goods" (1). One mark for a partial answer such as "inflation caused by high demand".

**23.** AO2 = 3.
- Interest = £8,000 × 4.5% = £360, so the balance is £8,360 (1).
- To keep the same purchasing power with 6.5% inflation, the saver would need £8,000 × 1.065 = £8,520 (1).
- £8,360 − £8,520 = −£160: the money has lost about £160 of purchasing power (1).

Accept the real interest rate method: 4.5% − 6.5% = −2%, and 2% of £8,000 = £160 lost (3 marks). Three marks for "lost £160" with no working. Two marks for a correct method with an arithmetic slip; one mark for £360 interest only.

**24.** Explain two policies, other than raising interest rates, that the government could use to reduce inflation. (6)
---
AO2 = 2, AO3 = 4. Marked as two separate explanations of up to 3 marks each.

| Marks | Descriptor (for each policy) |
|---|---|
| 3 | A full, precise explanation of how the policy reduces inflation, with a clear chain of reasoning applied to the context. |
| 2 | A partial explanation: the mechanism is given but not fully developed. |
| 1 | The policy is identified, or a fragmented point is made. |
| 0 | Nothing worthy of credit. |

Possible policies:
- Contractionary fiscal policy — raise income tax or VAT: households have less disposable income → consumer spending falls → less demand-pull pressure → firms find it harder to raise prices.
- Cut government spending: directly reduces total demand in the economy; also reduces borrowing (Item B mentions this option).
- Supply-side policies — e.g. investment in energy supply or in training: raise the economy's capacity and lower firms' costs → reduces cost-push pressure; takes a long time.
- Reducing costs directly, e.g. cutting fuel duty (a cost-push remedy), though this adds to the budget deficit.
- Credit is given for a policy only once; interest-rate changes and other monetary policy score 0.

**26.** Do you think raising interest rates is the best way to bring inflation back down to its 2% target?

Use Item A and Item B, and your own economic knowledge, to justify your view. (15)
---
AO2 = 5, AO3 = 10. Levels of response.

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 13–15 | A justified conclusion with sustained evaluation, weighing interest rates against alternatives and the type of inflation; judgements fully supported by evidence from both Items. Well-developed logical chains throughout; wide, accurate use of concepts and terms. |
| 4 | 10–12 | A partially justified conclusion; evaluation mostly coherent but some judgements are not fully supported. Logical chains on both sides but not all fully developed; strong application. |
| 3 | 7–9 | A conclusion with some evaluation, not well integrated. Chains started on both sides, or developed on one side only; good use of the Items. |
| 2 | 4–6 | A basic conclusion with little evidence. Relevant points without chains of reasoning; narrow range of concepts. |
| 1 | 1–3 | Simple points with no conclusion; limited use of the Items; non-specialist language. |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing worthy of credit. |

Indicative content — for interest rates:
- Transmission: higher Bank Rate → dearer borrowing and mortgages, more reward for saving → consumer spending and investment fall → total demand falls → demand-pull pressure eases. Table 4: inflation fell from 9.0% to 3.0% as Bank Rate rose to 4.5%.
- Speed and flexibility: the Bank can change rates at any monthly meeting; independent of political pressure, so its commitment to the 2% target is credible and helps anchor expectations.
- A stronger pound (from higher rates) makes imports cheaper, reducing imported inflation.

Indicative content — against or limits:
- Much of the Year 2 inflation was cost-push (world energy and food prices), which interest rates cannot directly reduce; they reduce demand at the cost of output and jobs.
- Time lags — the effect can take a year or more; critics said the bank acted too late.
- Costs (Item B): mortgage holders hit hard; firms delay investment; growth slows; unemployment rose from 3.8% to 4.8%; higher government debt interest.
- Distribution: savers gain, borrowers lose — though savers still lost in real terms in Year 3.
- Alternatives: contractionary fiscal policy (hits demand but is slow and politically hard, and may conflict with public services); supply-side policies (tackle cost-push causes but take years).

Evaluation: depends on the cause of inflation (demand-pull versus cost-push), how high rates must go, time lags and the size of the conflict with growth and employment. A top answer might conclude that interest rates are the best single short-run tool for demand-pull inflation and for keeping expectations anchored, but work best alongside supply-side measures when inflation comes from world energy prices.
