Firms and figures are invented for practice. The paper ramps from grade 4–5 to grade 8–9.

**14.** The price of a monthly bus pass in a town rises from £50 to £55. The number of passes sold falls from 8,000 to 7,600 a month.

**14.1** Calculate the price elasticity of demand for the monthly bus pass. Show your working. (2)

**14.2** Explain one factor that could make the demand for the bus pass price inelastic. (2)

(4)
---
**14.1** AO2 = 2. Percentage change in quantity demanded = −400 ÷ 8,000 × 100 = −5%, and percentage change in price = 5 ÷ 50 × 100 = +10% (1). PED = −5% ÷ 10% = −0.5 (1). Accept 0.5 without the sign. Two marks for the correct answer with no working; one mark for correct method with one slip.

**14.2** AO1 = 2. Identify a factor (1) and explain it in the context of the bus pass (1), e.g.: few close substitutes — passengers without a car have no other way to get to work or school (1), so they keep buying the pass when its price rises (1); it is a necessity for commuting; habit; the price is a small share of income for some passengers; in the short run people cannot quickly change job or buy a car. Do not credit a factor that makes demand elastic.

**15.** Oakridge Furniture is a firm that makes wooden chairs. Table 2 shows its workforce and output.

**Table 2 — Output at Oakridge Furniture**

| Year | Number of workers | Chairs made per year | Chairs per worker |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023 | 20 | 6,000 | 300 |
| 2024 | 20 | 7,000 | 350 |
| 2025 | 22 | 8,800 | 400 |

In 2024 the firm bought new computer-controlled cutting machines and sent its staff on a training course.

Using Table 2, analyse how the rise in labour productivity may benefit Oakridge Furniture. (6)
---
AO2 = 2, AO3 = 4. Levels of response.

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 5–6 | Full analysis: one or two well-developed, logical chains from higher productivity to its effects on the firm, supported by accurate use of Table 2. |
| 2 | 3–4 | Some analysis: a chain of reasoning is started but not fully developed; application to Oakridge mostly relevant; some use of the data. |
| 1 | 1–2 | Isolated points about productivity with no chain of reasoning; little or no use of the data. |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing worthy of credit. |

Indicative content:
- Use of data: output per worker rose from 300 to 400 chairs, a rise of one third (33.3%), while the workforce grew only 10%; output rose from 6,000 to 8,800 (46.7%).
- Wages are spread over more chairs, so the labour cost of each chair falls → average cost falls → at the same price each chair earns more profit, or Oakridge can cut its price and win sales from rivals.
- Lower average cost → more price competitive → higher sales and market share → more revenue.
- Higher output with a small rise in staff → the firm can meet rising demand without large recruitment and training costs.
- Higher profit can be reinvested in more machinery, raising productivity further; the firm may be able to pay higher wages, improving motivation and keeping skilled staff.
- The rise came after investment in machines and training, so the benefits must be set against those costs (the machines are a cost that is spread over the larger output, which is itself an economy of scale).
- Good answers link at least two steps: productivity ↑ → unit cost ↓ → price or profit effect.

**21.** Read Figure 1.

**Figure 1 — The cost of cheap clothes**

"Fast fashion" firms sell large volumes of low-priced clothing and bring out new ranges every few weeks. Shoppers benefit from low prices and a wide choice, and the industry employs many people in shops, warehouses and delivery.

Critics say the prices do not reflect the full cost of producing and throwing away clothes. Making fabric uses large amounts of water and energy, dyeing can pollute rivers, and many garments are worn only a few times before being sent to landfill or burned. Councils pay to collect and dispose of textile waste.

Some campaigners want a small tax on each new item of clothing, with the money spent on recycling schemes. Others argue that a tax would hit low-income families hardest, and that better labelling, so shoppers can see the environmental cost of what they buy, or regulation of how much waste firms may send to landfill would work better. Because demand for cheap clothing is thought to be quite price inelastic, a small tax might change buying habits very little.

Using Figure 1, assess whether a tax on new clothing would be the best way for the government to reduce the misallocation of resources caused by fast fashion. (9)
---
AO2 = 3, AO3 = 6. Levels of response.

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 7–9 | Well-developed, logical analysis of both the case for the tax and its limits or alternatives, using Figure 1 throughout; a conclusion that follows from the argument and is justified (e.g. "it depends on…"). Precise use of terms such as external cost, social cost, price inelastic. |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some analysis, but chains are only partly developed or the answer is largely one-sided; some use of Figure 1; a conclusion is given but only partly supported. |
| 1 | 1–3 | Descriptive or isolated points with little chain of reasoning; a bare assertion rather than a judgement; limited use of the context. |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing worthy of credit. |

Indicative content:
- Market failure: the price of fast fashion reflects only private costs; external costs (water use, river pollution, landfill, council disposal costs) fall on third parties, so social cost exceeds private cost and too many clothes are produced and bought — a misallocation of resources.
- For a tax: it raises the price towards the full social cost, so quantity bought falls; the polluter pays; revenue can fund recycling, cutting landfill further; it gives firms an incentive to make longer-lasting clothes.
- Against or limits: demand is price inelastic, so a small tax reduces quantity only a little — it raises revenue but may not change behaviour much; the right size of tax is hard to measure; it is regressive, hitting low-income families; firms may absorb it or shift production.
- Alternatives: labelling tackles imperfect information and changes behaviour without raising prices for the poor, but shoppers may ignore labels; regulation of landfill targets the waste directly but needs enforcement and may raise costs anyway.
- Judgement: e.g. a tax is most effective only if large enough and combined with labelling and recycling spending; or regulation is better because demand is inelastic. Credit any supported conclusion.

**Section B**

**Item A — The UK grocery market**

Four large supermarket chains sell most of the food bought in the UK. Table 4 shows invented figures for a market like this, worth £120 billion a year.

**Table 4 — Grocery market, one year**

| Firm | Market share (%) | Number of stores | Annual revenue (£ billion) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Firm W | 27 | 800 | 32.4 |
| Firm X | 17 | 600 | 20.4 |
| Firm Y | 14 | 500 | 16.8 |
| Firm Z | 12 | 400 | 14.4 |
| All other firms | 30 | — | 36.0 |

The large chains buy in enormous quantities, run their own distribution centres and spend heavily on advertising and loyalty-card schemes. They regularly cut prices on everyday items such as bread and milk to attract shoppers, but their profit margins are often only a few pence in every pound of sales. Many small independent grocers have closed over the last twenty years. In recent years two discount chains, which sell a narrower range of own-brand products at low prices, have opened hundreds of new stores and taken market share from the big four.

**Item B — Suppliers and workers**

Farmers and food manufacturers complain that the big chains use their buying power to push down the prices they pay, and sometimes change orders at short notice. A dairy farmer may receive less for a litre of milk than it costs to produce. The chains reply that they have signed long-term contracts with many farmers and that low prices are what shoppers want. The supermarkets are also among the largest employers in the country; most of their shop-floor staff are paid at or a little above the legal minimum wage.

**22.** Define the term "oligopoly". (2)

**23.** Using Table 4, calculate the average revenue per store for Firms W, X, Y and Z taken together. Give your answer to the nearest £ million. (3)

(5)
---
**22.** AO1 = 2. A complete definition for 2 marks: a market dominated by a small number of large firms (1) that are interdependent / each has a large market share / barriers to entry are high / products are often differentiated (1). One mark for a partial definition such as "a market with a few big firms". Do not credit "one firm controls the market" (that is monopoly).

**23.** AO2 = 3.
- Total revenue = 32.4 + 20.4 + 16.8 + 14.4 = £84.0 billion (1).
- Total stores = 800 + 600 + 500 + 400 = 2,300 (1).
- £84,000 million ÷ 2,300 = £36.5 million, which is £37 million to the nearest £ million (1).

Three marks for £37 million without working; two marks for £36.52 million (correct but not rounded as asked); one mark for either total correct with a wrong final answer. Averaging the four firms' own revenue-per-store figures (£40.5m, £34m, £33.6m, £36m → £36.0m) scores one mark only, because it ignores the different numbers of stores.

**24.** Using Item A, analyse how economies of scale might allow the largest supermarket chains to charge lower prices than small independent grocers. (6)
---
AO2 = 2, AO3 = 4. Levels of response.

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 5–6 | One or two full, logical chains from named economies of scale to lower average cost to lower prices, applied to the grocery context in Item A. |
| 2 | 3–4 | A chain is started — e.g. a named economy of scale linked to lower costs — but the link to price is missing or undeveloped; application mostly relevant. |
| 1 | 1–2 | Economies of scale named or defined, with no chain of reasoning; little application. |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing worthy of credit. |

Indicative content:
- Economies of scale: as output rises, average (unit) cost falls.
- Purchasing: the chains "buy in enormous quantities", so suppliers give bulk discounts → the cost of each item bought falls → the chain can sell below the price a small grocer must charge and still cover its costs.
- Technical: its own distribution centres and large lorries carry goods at a lower cost per item; automated tills and warehouses.
- Managerial: specialist buyers and managers negotiate better deals and run stores efficiently.
- Financial: large firms borrow more cheaply to build stores.
- Marketing: advertising and loyalty schemes cost less per item sold when spread over 2,300 stores.
- Lower average cost → can cut prices on everyday items (bread, milk) and still make a profit; small grocers with higher unit costs cannot match those prices, which helps explain their closure.
- Evaluation is not required for 6 marks, but good answers may note thin profit margins show that the cost advantage is passed on to shoppers.

**25.** Explain two ways the growth of the discount supermarket chains described in Item A could affect consumers. (6)
---
AO2 = 2, AO3 = 4. Marked as two separate explanations of up to 3 marks each.

| Marks | Descriptor (for each way) |
|---|---|
| 3 | A full, precise explanation of the effect on consumers, with a clear chain of reasoning applied to the grocery market. |
| 2 | A partial explanation: the effect is explained but lacks development or application. |
| 1 | The effect is identified, or a fragmented point is made. |
| 0 | Nothing worthy of credit. |

Possible ways:
- Lower prices: more competition forces the big four to cut prices to stop losing market share → consumers pay less for their shopping and have more real income to spend on other things.
- More choice of where to shop: new stores in more towns → consumers can switch to the cheapest seller, which weakens the market power of the large chains.

**26.** Do you think a grocery market dominated by a small number of large firms brings more benefits than costs?

Use Item A and Item B, and your own economic knowledge, to justify your view. (15)
---
AO2 = 5, AO3 = 10. Levels of response.

| Level | Marks | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 13–15 | A justified conclusion with sustained evaluation that weighs benefits against costs for different groups; judgements fully supported by evidence from both Items. Well-developed logical chains throughout; wide, accurate use of economic concepts and terms. |
| 4 | 10–12 | A partially justified conclusion; evaluation is mostly coherent but some judgements are not fully supported. Logical chains are established on both sides but not all are fully developed; strong application. |
| 3 | 7–9 | A conclusion with some evaluation, but not well integrated with the argument. Chains of reasoning are started on both sides, or developed on one side only; good use of the Items. |
| 2 | 4–6 | A basic conclusion based on simple judgements with little evidence. Relevant points made without chains of reasoning; narrow range of concepts. |
| 1 | 1–3 | Simple points with no conclusion; limited or irrelevant use of the Items; non-specialist language. |
| 0 | 0 | Nothing worthy of credit. |

Indicative content — benefits:
- Economies of scale (purchasing, distribution, marketing) lower average costs; with price cutting on everyday items, consumers pay low prices — thin margins suggest much of the saving is passed on.
- Wide range of products, convenience, loyalty rewards; investment in stores and online delivery funded by large-scale profits.
- Oligopolists compete hard on price and non-price factors, so consumers are not simply exploited.
- Large employers create many jobs; long-term contracts give some farmers security.

Indicative content — costs:
- Market power: 70% share for four firms (Table 4); barriers to entry; risk of higher prices or tacit collusion if competition weakens.
- Buying power squeezes suppliers: farmers paid less than cost may leave the industry, reducing supply and choice in future; moral and ethical concerns.
- Closure of independent grocers reduces local choice and harms communities; workers paid close to the minimum wage.
- Less incentive to innovate if rivals match each other.

Evaluation:
- Depends on how competitive the oligopoly is — the growth of discount chains shows barriers to entry can be overcome, limiting market power.
- Depends on whose view: consumers gain more clearly than suppliers or workers.
- Short run versus long run: low prices now, but if suppliers leave the industry, supply and prices may suffer later.
- Regulation (e.g. a code on how supermarkets treat suppliers, competition authority) can keep the benefits while limiting the costs.

A top-level answer reaches a clear judgement, e.g. "benefits outweigh costs for consumers as long as new entrants keep the market contestable, but the costs to suppliers mean some regulation is needed".
