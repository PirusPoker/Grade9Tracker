intro: Pearson Edexcel GCSE Business 1BS0 Paper 2 style: 1 hour 45 minutes, 90 marks, all questions compulsory, calculator allowed, no formulae given. Section A (questions 1–3, 35 marks) covers all of Theme 2, and Theme 1 knowledge can be used anywhere. Section B (questions 4–6, 30 marks) is about Larkspur Outdoor Ltd, a UK business; Section C (question 7, 25 marks) is about Kaito Bikes, a global business. Both are fictional, and the case information that the real paper puts in a source booklet is printed at the start of each question. Command words: explain = one point plus two linked developments; outline = one point plus one development, using the case; discuss and analyse (6) = develop one or two points into chains of reasoning; justify (9) = choose one option and weigh it, ending with a conclusion that adds something new; evaluate (12) = both sides, then a supported judgement. In Sections B and C use the case in every answer, including your conclusions. Show your working in calculations. Allow about a minute a mark.

**Section A**

**1.** (a) Which **one** of the following is an extension strategy?

A Cutting the advertising budget for the product
B Selling the product in a new market overseas
C Withdrawing the product from sale
D Charging a high price when the product is launched

Select one answer. (1)

(b) Which **one** of the following is the best definition of a **tariff**?

A A limit on the number of goods that can be imported
B A tax on imported goods
C A group of countries that agree to trade freely with each other
D A payment from the government to exporters

Select one answer. (1)

(c) Explain **one** benefit to a business of using quality assurance. (3)

(d) Explain **one** disadvantage to a business of employing staff on temporary contracts. (3)

(8)
---
(a) B (1). A new market brings new customers and extends the maturity phase; A and C are what a business does in decline, and D is a launch pricing strategy.

(b) B (1). A is a quota and C is a trade bloc.

(c) 1 mark for a benefit, plus up to 2 for linked developments. For example: faults are prevented at every stage rather than found at the end (1), so less material is wasted and fewer products have to be reworked or scrapped (1), which lowers costs and allows a more competitive price or higher profit (1). Or: workers are responsible for checking their own work (1), which can make them feel trusted and more motivated (1), raising productivity (1).

(d) 1 mark for a disadvantage, plus up to 2 for linked developments. For example: temporary staff may be less committed to the business (1), because they know their job will end (1), so their productivity and quality of work may be lower (1). Or: the business must recruit and train new staff each time a contract ends (1), which takes time and money (1), increasing its costs (1).

**2.** (a) Which **two** of the following would usually appear in a **person specification**?

B Hours of work
C Experience required

Select two answers. (2)

(b) Which **two** of the following are uses of technology in promotion?

A Viral advertising through social media
B Sponsoring a local football team
C Sending e-newsletters to customers
D Offering free product trials in a shop
E Delivering printed leaflets

Select two answers. (2)

(c) A business has the following figures for 2026.

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Sales revenue | £725,000 |
| Cost of sales | £462,000 |

Using the information in the table, calculate, to 2 decimal places, the business's gross profit margin. You are advised to show your workings. (2)

(d) Explain **one** advantage to a business of using flow production. (3)

(e) Explain **one** disadvantage to a business of growing through a takeover. (3)

(12)
---
(a) A and C (1 mark each). Hours, salary and job title belong in the job description.

(b) A and C (1 mark each).

(c) Gross profit = £725,000 − £462,000 = £263,000 (1). Gross profit margin = £263,000 ÷ £725,000 × 100 = **36.28%** (1). Award 2 marks for the correct answer without working; 36.3% or 36% does not meet the instruction to give 2 decimal places, so award 1 mark.

(d) 1 mark for an advantage, plus up to 2 for linked developments. For example: products are made continuously on a production line, often by machines (1), so output is very high and the cost of each unit is low (1), which lets the business charge competitive prices and still make a profit (1). Or: every product is identical (1), so quality is consistent (1), which builds customer trust in the brand (1).

(e) 1 mark for a disadvantage, plus up to 2 for linked developments. For example: buying another business is very expensive (1), so the business may need a large loan or to sell shares (1), increasing interest costs or diluting the owners' control (1). Or: the two businesses may have different cultures and ways of working (1), so staff in the business taken over may resist the change (1), lowering motivation and productivity (1).

**3.** (a) Which **one** of the following is a **financial** method of motivation?

A Job rotation
B Autonomy
C Commission
D Job enrichment

Select one answer. (1)

(b) A business is considering buying a machine that costs £50,000. The machine is expected to generate a total profit of £26,000 over four years.

Calculate the average rate of return on the machine. You are advised to show your workings. (2)

(c) Explain **one** disadvantage to a business of using just in time (JIT) stock control. (3)

(d) Explain **one** reason why a business may provide ongoing training for all of its employees. (3)

(e) Discuss the impact on a business of a pressure group campaign against one of its products. (6)

(15)
---
(a) C (1). The other three are non-financial methods.

(b) Average annual profit = £26,000 ÷ 4 = £6,500 (1). ARR = £6,500 ÷ £50,000 × 100 = **13%** (1). Award 2 marks for the correct answer without working.

(c) 1 mark for a disadvantage, plus up to 2 for linked developments. For example: there is no buffer stock (1), so if a supplier delivers late, production has to stop (1), which means orders are not met and customers may go to competitors (1). Or: the business orders small quantities frequently (1), so it loses bulk-buying discounts (1), which can raise the cost of each unit (1).

(d) 1 mark for a reason, plus up to 2 for linked developments. For example: to keep skills up to date as technology changes (1), so employees can use new equipment and software efficiently (1), which raises productivity (1). Or: training shows employees that the business values them (1), which increases motivation (1), so staff are more likely to stay, lowering recruitment costs (1).

(e) Indicative content (AO1b understanding, AO3a analysis):
- A campaign can attract media and social media attention, damaging the business's reputation, so some customers stop buying the product and sales fall.
- The business may have to change its marketing mix: reformulate or redesign the product (for example less plastic packaging), which raises costs, or spend more on promotion to rebuild its image.
- Changing suppliers or materials to meet the campaign's demands may increase costs and reduce profit in the short term.
- But responding well can become a selling point: the business can promote its changes, attract ethically minded customers and gain a competitive advantage.
- The impact depends on how large the campaign is, how loyal customers are, and whether the product has close substitutes.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): accurate understanding throughout with business terms used well; two developed lines of reasoning with logical, linked chains.
- Level 2 (3–4): mostly accurate understanding; some chains of reasoning, though links may be incomplete.
- Level 1 (1–2): some knowledge of pressure groups; isolated points with little development.

**Section B**

**Extract A: Larkspur Outdoor Ltd**

Larkspur Outdoor Ltd designs and sells waterproof jackets, fleeces and walking trousers. It began as a single shop in Keswick in 2008 and now has 38 stores across the UK, mainly in towns near national parks, as well as a website. Its jackets are priced above those of most high-street rivals, and every product carries a lifetime repair promise: customers can return a damaged item to be repaired free of charge.

Since 2023, 60% of the fabric in Larkspur's jackets has been made from recycled plastic bottles, and the business aims to reach 100% by 2028. Recycled fabric costs Larkspur about 15% more than new fabric.

Larkspur gives its store managers the freedom to choose some of the stock they sell and to run local events such as guided walks.

**4.** (a) Outline **one** benefit to Larkspur of differentiating its products. (2)

(b) Analyse the impact on Larkspur of using recycled materials in its products. (6)

(8)
---
(a) 2 marks for a linked point in context; 1 mark without development or context. For example: the lifetime repair promise makes Larkspur's jackets stand out from high-street rivals (1), so customers are willing to pay its higher prices, which raises the profit on each jacket (1).

(b) Indicative content (AO2 application, AO3a analysis):
- Recycled fabric costs about 15% more than new fabric, so Larkspur's cost of sales rises; unless prices rise too, its gross profit margin falls.
- Larkspur's customers are walkers who visit national parks and are likely to care about the environment, so recycled jackets match their values and strengthen the brand, encouraging loyalty and repeat purchases.
- The recycling message can be used in promotion, differentiating Larkspur further and supporting its premium prices, so the higher cost may be passed on to customers.
- The target of 100% by 2028 may be hard to meet if supplies of recycled fabric are limited, and failing publicly could damage its reputation.
- There may be a trade-off between environmental aims and profit in the short term, but a long-term gain in reputation.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): the case is used throughout (for example 60%, 15% higher cost, premium prices, national-park customers); developed, logical chains of reasoning.
- Level 2 (3–4): sound use of the case with some gaps; some developed reasoning.
- Level 1 (1–2): generic points about the environment with weak links to Larkspur.

An answer with no application to Larkspur cannot score above 3 marks.

**5.** Larkspur Outdoor Ltd (Extract A) had the following results.

| | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Sales revenue | £41.0m | £46.2m |
| Cost of sales | £18.9m | £22.4m |
| Other operating expenses and interest | £17.6m | £19.9m |
| Share of sales made online | 28% | 36% |

(a) Using the information in the table, calculate Larkspur's net profit for 2026. You are advised to show your workings. (2)

(b) Using the information in the table and your answer to (a), calculate, to 2 decimal places, Larkspur's net profit margin for 2026. (2)

(c) Analyse the impact on Larkspur of selling a growing share of its products online. (6)

(10)
---
(a) Gross profit = £46.2m − £22.4m = £23.8m (1). Net profit = £23.8m − £19.9m = **£3.9m** (1). Award 2 marks for the correct answer without working.

(b) Net profit margin = £3.9m ÷ £46.2m × 100 (1) = **8.44%** (1). Allow own figure from (a).

(c) Indicative content (AO2 application, AO3a analysis):
- Online sales rose from 28% to 36% of revenue, so Larkspur reaches customers across the UK who do not live near one of its 38 stores, which helps explain the rise in revenue from £41.0m to £46.2m.
- Selling online avoids some store costs such as staff and rent per sale, but needs spending on the website, warehousing and delivery, so the effect on costs is mixed.
- Customers cannot try jackets on before buying, so returns may rise, which adds costs.
- If online sales keep growing, some stores in quiet towns may become unprofitable, and Larkspur may need fewer of them, though stores support the brand through local events such as guided walks.
- The data supports caution: revenue grew by about 12.7%, but cost of sales grew faster (from £18.9m to £22.4m, about 18.5%) and net profit fell from £4.5m in 2025 to £3.9m, so the growth in online sales has not yet made Larkspur more profitable.

Levels:
- Level 3 (5–6): the case and data are used throughout; developed, logical chains of reasoning.
- Level 2 (3–4): sound use of the case with some gaps; some developed reasoning.
- Level 1 (1–2): generic points about e-commerce with weak links to Larkspur.

An answer with no application to Larkspur cannot score above 3 marks.

**6.** Larkspur Outdoor Ltd (Extract A) wants to grow. It is considering two options.

Option 1: open 10 new stores in UK towns near national parks and coastal walking routes.

Option 2: take over Fellstride, a small Cumbrian business that makes leather walking boots and sells them through 120 independent shops.

(a) State **one** way Larkspur could provide good post-sales service. (1)

(b) Outline **one** benefit to Larkspur of having a decentralised organisational structure. (2)

(c) Justify which **one** of these two options Larkspur should choose. (9)

(12)
---
(a) Any one, for example: the lifetime repair promise; an easy returns process; advice on caring for a waterproof jacket; following up a purchase by email to ask for feedback (1).

(b) 2 marks for a linked point in context; 1 mark without development or context. For example: store managers choose some of their own stock (1), so each store can sell what local walkers want, which raises sales (1). Or: being trusted to run local events motivates store managers (1), so they work harder to make their store successful (1).

(c) Indicative content (AO2 application, AO3a analysis, AO3b evaluation):

Option 1, 10 new stores (organic growth):
- Larkspur knows how to run stores in this kind of town, so the risk is lower and it keeps control of quality and its brand.
- Each store brings the lifetime repair service and local events closer to more customers, building loyalty.
- But growth is slow and each store has high fixed costs (rent, staff) to cover; with online sales rising from 28% to 36%, new stores may not be the best use of money.
- It needs finance, possibly a loan, adding interest costs that reduce net profit from its £3.9m.

Option 2, taking over Fellstride (external growth):
- Larkspur immediately gains a new product, walking boots, widening its range so customers can buy a full outfit from one brand.
- It also gains Fellstride's 120 independent shops as a new channel for its own clothing, and Fellstride's making skills.
- But a takeover is expensive, and the two businesses may have different cultures: one designs and retails clothing, the other makes leather footwear.
- Leather may not fit Larkspur's environmental image built on recycled fabric, risking its reputation with its customers.

A conclusion should decide, for example the takeover, because it adds a product and a sales channel at once and strengthens Larkspur's differentiation, provided the price is affordable and Fellstride's products can meet Larkspur's environmental aims; or the stores, because they are lower risk and build on what already works, provided the new towns have enough walkers. A good conclusion adds a deciding factor rather than repeating earlier points. Either option can score full marks.

Levels:
- Level 3 (7–9): the case is used throughout, including the conclusion; developed chains of reasoning; a well-supported judgement that weighs the chosen option's benefits against its drawbacks.
- Level 2 (4–6): sound use of the case with some inconsistencies; some developed reasoning; a judgement with some support.
- Level 1 (1–3): limited use of the case; simple points; a judgement that is asserted or missing.

A one-sided answer cannot reach Level 3, and listing the benefits of both options with no comparison is not evaluation.

**Section C**

**Extract B: Kaito Bikes**

Kaito Bikes is a manufacturer of electric bicycles (e-bikes) based in Osaka, Japan. It employs 2,800 people, has factories in Japan and Vietnam, and sells in 30 countries through independent bike shops and its own websites. Its bikes are known for light frames and long-lasting batteries, and they are priced in the middle of the market.

Kaito ships bikes to Europe by sea, which takes about six weeks. Some countries charge a tariff on e-bikes imported from Asia, and shipping costs have risen. Kaito is about to launch the K-Fold, a lightweight folding e-bike designed for commuters who take their bike on trains.

| Kaito's e-bike sales (thousand units) | 2024 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Europe | 180 | 260 |
| North America | 120 | 150 |
| Asia | 310 | 340 |
| Australia | 25 | 40 |

**7.** (a) State **one** responsibility of a supervisor in one of Kaito's factories. (1)

(b) Using the information in the table, identify the region with the largest percentage increase in Kaito's sales between 2024 and 2026. (1)

(c) Outline **one** impact of tariffs on Kaito Bikes. (2)

(d) Kaito plans to launch the K-Fold in the UK and is considering two pricing strategies.

Option 1: price skimming, launching at £2,400.

Option 2: penetration pricing, launching at £1,300.

Justify which **one** of these two options Kaito should choose. (9)

(13)
---
(a) Any one, for example: checking the work of the production team; making sure targets are met; allocating tasks to workers; ensuring health and safety on the production line; reporting problems to managers (1).

(b) Australia (1). Australia rose by 60% (25 to 40 thousand); Europe by about 44%, North America 25%, Asia about 10%.

(c) 2 marks for a linked point in context; 1 mark without development or context. For example: a tariff adds a tax to each e-bike Kaito sends to those countries (1), so Kaito must either raise its mid-market prices and risk losing sales, or absorb the cost and make less profit on each bike (1).

(d) Indicative content (AO2 application, AO3a analysis, AO3b evaluation):

Option 1, price skimming at £2,400:
- The K-Fold is new and has features (lightweight, folding, long-lasting battery) that early adopters may pay a premium for, so Kaito earns a high profit per bike and quickly recovers its development costs.
- A high price can signal quality and help build the K-Fold's image.
- But Kaito's brand is mid-market, so its usual customers may not pay £2,400; rival folding e-bikes may be cheaper, and sales volume may be low.
- Tariffs and rising shipping costs to Europe raise costs, which a high price helps cover.

Option 2, penetration pricing at £1,300:
- A low price attracts large numbers of commuters quickly, building market share in a fast-growing region (European sales rose from 180 to 260 thousand).
- High volume could give Kaito economies of scale in production.
- But the profit per bike is low, and with tariffs and shipping costs it may be very thin or negative.
- Raising the price later may anger customers, and a low price could cheapen Kaito's reputation for quality.

A conclusion should decide, for example skimming, because the K-Fold has distinctive features, Kaito faces extra costs from tariffs and shipping, and the price can be lowered later as rivals appear; it depends on how many competing folding e-bikes are already on sale in the UK. Or penetration, if the market is already crowded and Kaito's priority is market share. A good conclusion adds a deciding factor rather than repeating earlier points.

Levels:
- Level 3 (7–9): the case is used throughout, including the conclusion; developed chains of reasoning; a well-supported judgement that weighs the chosen option's benefits against its drawbacks.
- Level 2 (4–6): sound use of the case with some inconsistencies; some developed reasoning; a judgement with some support.
- Level 1 (1–3): limited use of the case; simple points; a judgement that is asserted or missing.

**7.** (e) Kaito Bikes (Extract B) is considering moving part of its production from Vietnam to a new factory in Poland, to be closer to its European customers. Wages in Poland are higher than in Vietnam.

Evaluate whether Kaito should open the new factory in Poland. You should use the information provided as well as your knowledge of business. (12)
---
Indicative content (AO1b understanding, AO2 application, AO3a analysis, AO3b evaluation):

For the factory in Poland:
- Europe is Kaito's fastest-growing large market (180 to 260 thousand units, about 44%), so producing there puts supply close to demand.
- Delivery to European bike shops falls from about six weeks by sea to days by road, so Kaito can respond faster to changes in demand, hold less stock and improve customer satisfaction.
- Bikes made inside the EU avoid the tariffs some European countries charge on e-bikes imported from Asia, and shipping costs fall, which lowers the cost per bike sold in Europe.
- Being seen as a local producer may help Kaito's reputation and marketing in Europe.

Against:
- Wages in Poland are higher than in Vietnam, so labour costs per bike rise, which may cancel out the savings on tariffs and shipping.
- Building and equipping a new factory needs a large investment, and new workers must be recruited and trained, so productivity may be low at first.
- Moving production away from Vietnam could mean job losses there and damage Kaito's reputation with staff and the local community.
- Running factories in three countries is harder to manage and communicate across; quality must be kept consistent.

A conclusion should weigh the case, for example: open the factory, because Europe's sales are growing quickly and the savings on tariffs, shipping and six-week delivery times are likely to outweigh higher wages; it depends on how large the tariff and shipping savings are compared with the wage difference, and on whether European growth continues. The conclusion should be justified with evidence from the case.

Levels:
- Level 3 (9–12): accurate understanding throughout; detailed use of the case; developed, logical chains of reasoning; a valid, well-reasoned conclusion that weighs both sides and identifies what the decision depends on.
- Level 2 (5–8): mostly accurate understanding; sound use of the case with some gaps; some chains of reasoning; a conclusion based on some evaluation.
- Level 1 (1–4): some knowledge with limited terminology; limited use of the case; simple points; a conclusion that is asserted or generic.
