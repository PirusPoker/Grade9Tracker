**Section A: Britain: Health and the people, c1000 to the present day**

**Source A** - an engraving printed in a British medical textbook in 1882, described for this mock: an operation in a hospital theatre. The surgeons wear their ordinary dark coats; no one wears gloves, a mask or a gown. A steam-powered spray stands beside the table and sends a fine mist of carbolic acid over the patient and the wound. Instruments lie in a tray of liquid. A row of students watches from benches close behind.

**1.** How useful is Source A to an historian studying surgery in the late 19th century? Explain your answer using Source A and your contextual knowledge. (8)
---
AO3. Levels of response.
Level 4 (7-8): complex evaluation of the source using content and provenance with detailed contextual knowledge, leading to a sustained judgement about its usefulness.
Level 3 (5-6): developed evaluation based on content and/or provenance.
Level 2 (3-4): simple evaluation based on content and/or provenance.
Level 1 (1-2): basic analysis; description of the source.

Indicative content:
- **Content - change:** it shows Lister's antiseptic method in use: Lister began using carbolic acid in 1865 and published his results in 1867; the spray to kill germs in the air and instruments soaked in carbolic follow his methods, and they reduced deaths from infection.
- **Content - continuity:** surgeons in everyday coats and no gloves, with spectators close by, show that aseptic surgery (sterile gowns, gloves from the 1890s, steam-sterilised instruments) had not yet arrived; the spray was unpleasant and Lister later dropped it.
- **Provenance:** a medical textbook was produced to teach doctors approved methods, so it shows what was considered best practice in 1882 - useful for that, but it may show an ideal rather than what happened in every hospital; many surgeons were slow to adopt Lister's ideas.
- **Context:** germ theory (Pasteur, 1861) explained why antiseptics worked; chloroform (Simpson, 1847) had already dealt with pain, which led to longer operations and, at first, more infection.
- **Judgement:** e.g. very useful for how surgery was changing after germ theory, but it shows one moment of a transition and one kind of hospital.

::: q 4-5 2AA.4b
**2.** Explain the significance of the Liberal social reforms of 1906-1911 for the health of the British people. (8)
---
AO1 2, AO2 6. Levels of response.
Level 4 (7-8): complex explanation of aspects of significance, such as significance at the time and over time, supported by precise knowledge.
Level 3 (5-6): developed explanation of two or more aspects of significance.
Level 2 (3-4): simple explanation of one aspect of significance.
Level 1 (1-2): basic explanation.

Indicative content:
- **Why they were needed:** Booth's survey of London and Rowntree's survey of York showed that poverty was widespread and often not the fault of the poor; many volunteers for the Boer War (1899-1902) were rejected as unfit.
- **What they did:** free school meals (1906) and school medical inspections (1907); old age pensions (1908); the National Insurance Act (1911) gave insured workers sick pay and free treatment from a doctor on a panel.
- **Significance at the time:** for the first time the government took responsibility for the health of the poor, moving away from laissez-faire.
- **Limits:** National Insurance covered the worker but not his wife or children; hospital care was not included; pensions only started at 70.
- **Significance over time:** they set the principle of state responsibility that the Beveridge Report (1942) and the NHS (1948) later extended to everyone.

::: q 6-7 2AA.1 2AA.2
**3.** Explain two ways in which the Black Death (1348-49) and the Great Plague (1665) were similar. (8)
---
AO1 4, AO2 4. Levels of response.
Level 4 (7-8): complex explanation of two similarities, supported by precise knowledge of both events.
Level 3 (5-6): developed explanation of two similarities, or one in depth.
Level 2 (3-4): simple explanation of one similarity.
Level 1 (1-2): basic identification of similarity.

Indicative content:
- **Similar ideas about cause:** in both, people blamed God's punishment for sin, the position of the planets and miasma (bad air); nobody knew that the disease was carried by fleas on rats, so explanations stayed similar across 300 years.
- **Similar treatments and prevention:** in both, people carried sweet-smelling herbs or posies, lit fires or burned tar to clean the air, prayed and used remedies such as bleeding; none worked.
- **Similar responses from authorities:** in both, towns tried to clean streets and keep away strangers; in 1665 these became more organised (houses shut up with a red cross, watchmen, searchers of the dead, plague pits), showing some development but the same basic thinking.
- **Similar impact:** both killed a large share of the population of the places they hit, and people fled the towns if they could.
Answers that explain one similarity with real depth across both events can reach Level 3.

**4.** Has war been the main factor in the development of medicine in Britain? Explain your answer with reference to war and other factors. Use a range of examples from across your study of Health and the people: c1000 to the present day. (16 marks, plus 4 for spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terms) (20)
---
AO1 8, AO2 8, plus SPaG 4. Levels of response.
Level 4 (13-16): complex explanation of the stated factor and other factors, leading to a sustained judgement, with examples from across the whole period.
Level 3 (9-12): developed explanation of war and at least one other factor.
Level 2 (5-8): simple explanation of war or other factors.
Level 1 (1-4): basic explanation; general knowledge.
SPaG: high performance 4 (accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar, a wide range of specialist terms); intermediate 2-3; threshold 1.

Indicative content:
- **War helped:** Paré's battlefield work led him to replace boiling oil with a soothing ointment and to use ligatures; the First World War advanced plastic surgery (Gillies), blood transfusion and blood banks, and the use of X-rays; the Second World War led governments in Britain and the USA to fund the mass production of penicillin; the Boer War recruits exposed the nation's poor health and helped prompt the Liberal reforms; the Second World War's evacuation and the Beveridge Report helped create the NHS.
- **War did not always help:** medieval warfare added little to ideas about disease; most breakthroughs in understanding came in peacetime.
- **Science and technology:** the microscope and germ theory (Pasteur 1861, Koch's identification of bacteria) transformed understanding; X-rays, keyhole surgery and transplants depended on technology.
- **Individuals:** Vesalius, Harvey, Jenner, Lister, Simpson and Fleming.
- **Government:** the 1848 and 1875 Public Health Acts, compulsory smallpox vaccination, the Liberal reforms and the NHS.
- **Religion and superstition:** often held back progress (support for Galen, the Black Death as divine punishment) but also provided care in hospitals.
- **Chance:** Fleming's discovery of penicillin (1928); Pasteur's discovery of attenuation with chicken cholera.
- **Communication:** printing spread Vesalius's work; journals spread Lister's methods.
- **Judgement:** e.g. war was a powerful accelerator in the 20th century, especially in surgery and in turning penicillin into a mass-produced drug, but science and government were the main long-term factors; the best answers show factors working together (war plus government plus science behind penicillin).
:::

**Section B: Elizabethan England, c1568-1603**

**Interpretation A** - written for this mock in the style of a modern historian in a popular history of Tudor England published in 2015:
"The playhouses of Elizabethan London were among the great achievements of the age. Purpose-built theatres such as the Theatre and later the Globe drew thousands of Londoners of every class, apprentices standing in the yard while gentlemen sat in the galleries. Writers like Marlowe and Shakespeare wrote for this mass audience, and the Queen herself enjoyed plays performed at court. Not everyone approved, but by 1600 the theatre had become part of everyday life."

**1.** How convincing is Interpretation A about the Elizabethan theatre? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretation A. (8)
---
AO4. Levels of response.
Level 4 (7-8): complex evaluation of the interpretation, testing several of its claims against detailed contextual knowledge, leading to a sustained judgement.
Level 3 (5-6): developed evaluation of the interpretation based on contextual knowledge.
Level 2 (3-4): simple evaluation of one aspect.
Level 1 (1-2): basic analysis; the interpretation is paraphrased.

Indicative content:
- **Convincing - purpose-built theatres:** James Burbage built the Theatre in Shoreditch in 1576; others followed, including the Rose and, in 1599, the Globe on Bankside, built partly from the Theatre's timbers.
- **Convincing - a mixed audience:** a penny bought standing room in the yard for apprentices and labourers ("groundlings"); more paid for seats in the covered galleries; the theatre was cheap entertainment for many.
- **Convincing - writers and royal approval:** Marlowe and Shakespeare wrote for the public stage; the companies needed noble patrons (such as the Lord Chamberlain's Men) and performed for the Queen at court, which protected them.
- **Less convincing - "not everyone approved":** this understates the opposition. Puritans called the theatres sinful; the City of London authorities blamed them for spreading plague, crime and idleness among apprentices, which is why most theatres were built outside the City's control; theatres were closed during plague outbreaks.
- **Judgement:** e.g. convincing about the theatre's popularity and achievements, but it plays down how controversial and fragile it was.

::: q 6-7 2BC.3a
**2.** Explain what was important about the arrival of Mary, Queen of Scots in England in 1568. (8)
---
AO1 4, AO2 4. Levels of response.
Level 4 (7-8): complex explanation of consequences, showing how they developed over time and linking them, supported by precise knowledge.
Level 3 (5-6): developed explanation of two or more consequences.
Level 2 (3-4): simple explanation of one consequence.
Level 1 (1-2): basic description.

Indicative content:
- **A Catholic heir in England:** Mary was Elizabeth's closest heir and a Catholic; her arrival after fleeing Scotland gave English Catholics and foreign powers a focus for plots to replace Elizabeth.
- **A dilemma for Elizabeth:** releasing her to France or Scotland was dangerous; executing an anointed queen set a bad precedent; so she was kept in captivity in England for nearly 19 years.
- **The Northern Rebellion (1569):** the northern earls planned to free Mary and marry her to the Duke of Norfolk.
- **The plots:** Ridolfi (1571), Throckmorton (1583) and Babington (1586) all aimed to put Mary on the throne; Walsingham's spy network uncovered them.
- **Parliament and execution:** Parliament pressed Elizabeth to deal with Mary; after the Babington Plot Mary was tried and executed in February 1587.
- **Wider consequences:** her execution removed the Catholic heir but gave Philip II a further reason to launch the Armada in 1588.

::: q 8-9 2BC.3b
**3.** Write an account of the ways in which the Spanish Armada was defeated in 1588. (8)
---
AO1 4, AO2 4. Levels of response.
Level 4 (7-8): a coherent narrative that analyses causation, showing how each stage contributed to the defeat, supported by precise knowledge.
Level 3 (5-6): a developed narrative with analysis of causes.
Level 2 (3-4): a simple narrative with some analysis.
Level 1 (1-2): basic description.

Indicative content:
- **Spanish plan and problems:** the fleet under the Duke of Medina Sidonia, who had little naval experience, was to sail up the Channel and meet the Duke of Parma's army from the Netherlands, but there was no deep-water port to collect the troops; Drake's raid on Cadiz in 1587 had destroyed ships and barrel staves, delaying the fleet.
- **In the Channel:** the Spanish kept a tight crescent formation; the English ships, faster and more manoeuvrable, fired from a distance but did little damage at first.
- **Calais:** the Armada anchored off Calais to wait for Parma, who was not ready; that night the English sent in fireships, the Spanish cut their anchor cables and the formation broke.
- **Gravelines:** the next day the English attacked the scattered fleet at closer range with their guns, causing serious damage.
- **The voyage home:** the wind prevented a return through the Channel, so the Armada sailed round Scotland and Ireland, where storms wrecked many ships; a large part of the fleet did not return.
- **Linking:** each stage led to the next: the failure to meet Parma made the fleet wait at Calais, which made the fireships possible, which broke the formation before Gravelines.

::: q 8-9 2BC.4 2BC.1
**4.** "The main change Robert Dudley made to Kenilworth Castle was turning it into a place to entertain Elizabeth I." How far does a study of Kenilworth Castle support this statement? Explain your answer. You should refer to Kenilworth Castle and your contextual knowledge. (16)
---
AO1 8, AO2 8. Levels of response.
Level 4 (13-16): complex explanation of the site and its context, testing the statement against other changes and purposes, leading to a sustained judgement.
Level 3 (9-12): developed explanation of the stated change and at least one other aspect of the site.
Level 2 (5-8): simple explanation of the stated change or other features.
Level 1 (1-4): basic description of the site.

Indicative content:
- **Support - built for the Queen:** Elizabeth granted Kenilworth to Dudley in 1563 and he rebuilt it for her visits. Leicester's Building, a new tower of lodgings with very large windows, was built to house the Queen; the privy garden was laid out for her famous visit of July 1575, which lasted 19 days and included pageants on the mere, fireworks and hunting; the visit was Dudley's most lavish attempt to impress her, and may have been a last attempt to win her hand.
- **Other purposes - Dudley's own status:** the new gatehouse, the remodelled apartments and the display of his badge and arms advertised his wealth and power as a leading courtier and favourite; Kenilworth showed off the prestige that came from royal patronage.
- **Other changes - fashion and comfort:** like other great Elizabethan houses, it moved from a defensive fortress towards a comfortable palace with glass windows, galleries and gardens - reflecting the prosperity and fashions of the age and the rise of the courtier class.
- **Continuity:** the medieval keep, curtain walls and the great mere were kept, so the castle still looked like an ancient fortress and linked Dudley to its long history.
- **Wider context:** patronage at court, the competition for the Queen's favour, and the issue of her marriage.
- **Judgement:** e.g. entertaining the Queen was the main driver of the changes, but it was inseparable from Dudley's wish to display his own status, and the building also followed wider Elizabethan fashions.
:::
