::: q 4-5 1AB.1b
**Section A: Germany, 1890-1945: Democracy and dictatorship**

Read Interpretations A and B. Both were written for this mock.

**Interpretation A** - written in the style of a historian in a general history of the Weimar Republic published in 2008:
"Between 1924 and 1929 the Republic found its feet. The new Rentenmark ended the chaos of 1923, and American loans under the Dawes Plan restarted factories and paid for new housing, schools and swimming baths. Abroad, the Locarno treaties and entry to the League of Nations made Germany a respected power again. The extreme parties lost ground in the 1928 election. For the first time, many Germans could believe that democracy worked."

**Interpretation B** - written in the voice of a man who ran a small grocer's shop in Berlin in the 1920s, recalling the period in a radio interview in 1965:
"People talk now about the golden twenties, but that is not how it felt behind my counter. The inflation had wiped out every pfennig my parents had saved, and nobody gave it back. Prices were steady, yes, but the money in the banks came from America and we all knew it could be called in. The farmers who came into town were in debt and angry. And the new nightclubs and shameless shows told us the country had lost its way."

**1.** How does Interpretation B differ from Interpretation A about Germany in the years 1924-1929? Explain your answer based on what it says in Interpretations A and B. (4)
---
AO4. Levels of response.
Level 2 (3-4): a developed explanation of a difference in what the interpretations say, supported by the content of both. One difference, well explained, is enough for 4 marks.
Level 1 (1-2): a simple difference identified, or a feature of one interpretation described, with little support.

Indicative content:
- **Overall view:** A says Germany genuinely recovered and Germans gained confidence in democracy ("found its feet"), while B says the recovery was shallow and many people did not feel it ("not how it felt behind my counter").
- **The economy:** A presents American loans as the engine of recovery; B sees the same loans as a weakness, because they "could be called in".
- **Who benefited:** A talks about the nation as a whole (factories, housing, international respect); B points to groups left behind - savers ruined by the inflation and indebted farmers.
- **Culture:** A does not treat culture as a problem; B sees the new culture as a sign of moral decline.

**2.** Why might the authors of Interpretations A and B have a different interpretation about Germany in the years 1924-1929? Explain your answer using Interpretations A and B and your contextual knowledge. (4)
---
AO4. Levels of response.
Level 2 (3-4): a developed explanation of why the interpretations differ, based on their provenance (who wrote them, when and why), supported by contextual knowledge.

Indicative content:
- **Purpose and audience:** A is a historian writing an overview decades later for a general reader; he looks at national evidence such as the end of hyperinflation, the Dawes Plan, Locarno (1925), League membership (1926) and the 1928 election, so he judges the period as a whole.
- **Experience:** B lived through it as a small shopkeeper - exactly the kind of middle-class saver ruined in 1923 and never compensated - so his personal losses shape his view.
- **Hindsight:** B speaks in 1965, knowing the American loans were recalled after the Wall Street Crash of 1929 and that the Depression followed; this makes him stress how fragile the recovery was.
- **Values:** B's comment on nightclubs reflects the conservative, older Germans who disliked Weimar culture in Berlin.

::: q 6-7 1AB.1b
**3.** Which interpretation gives the more convincing opinion about Germany in the years 1924-1929? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what it says in Interpretations A and B. (8)
---
AO4. Levels of response.
Level 4 (7-8): complex evaluation of both interpretations using detailed contextual knowledge, leading to a sustained judgement about which is more convincing. May weigh how far each is convincing rather than simply choosing one.
Level 3 (5-6): developed evaluation of both interpretations against contextual knowledge.
Level 2 (3-4): simple evaluation of one interpretation, or of both in a basic way.
Level 1 (1-2): basic analysis; interpretations described with little use of knowledge.

Indicative content:
- **A is convincing on:** the Rentenmark (late 1923) and the Reichsmark (1924) ended hyperinflation; the Dawes Plan (1924) brought large American loans and phased reparations; industrial output recovered to roughly its pre-war level by 1928; Locarno (1925) and League membership (1926) ended Germany's isolation; the Nazis won only 12 seats in 1928 while the moderate parties held a majority.
- **A is less convincing on:** it says nothing about the dependence on short-term American loans, the continuing unemployment, or that the largest party in the 1928 election, the SPD, still needed fragile coalitions.
- **B is convincing on:** middle-class savers lost everything in 1923 and were not compensated; farmers suffered falling prices and debt from the mid-1920s; the economy did rest on loans that were recalled after October 1929; Weimar culture (cabaret, Bauhaus, new art and film) did offend many conservatives.
- **B is less convincing on:** it is one person's experience in Berlin; prices and wages were stable and many workers were better off; it ignores Stresemann's success abroad.
- **Judgement:** e.g. A is more convincing about political and diplomatic recovery, but B is more convincing about how fragile and uneven the economic recovery was - which events after 1929 confirmed.

::: q 6-7 1AB.1a
**4.** Describe two problems Kaiser Wilhelm II faced in ruling Germany, 1890-1914. (4)
---
AO1. Levels of response.
Level 2 (3-4): knowledge and understanding of two problems, each with relevant supporting detail.
Level 1 (1-2): knowledge of one or two problems, stated with little detail.

Indicative content:
- **The rise of socialism:** industrialisation created a large working class; the SPD, which wanted far-reaching reform, grew to become the largest party in the Reichstag in 1912, which alarmed the Kaiser and the conservative elites.
- **The power of the Reichstag:** the Kaiser appointed the Chancellor, but the elected Reichstag had to approve the budget and new laws, so governments had to bargain for support - for example over the cost of the navy.
- **Prussian militarism and the elites:** the army and the Prussian landowning Junkers expected to dominate, which clashed with demands for more democracy.
- **The Kaiser's own personality:** he was impulsive and wanted personal rule, which made consistent government difficult.
Credit other valid problems, such as the cost of the Navy Laws and social reform.

::: q 6-7 1AB.3b
**5.** In what ways were the lives of young people in Germany affected by Nazi policies in the years 1933-1939? Explain your answer. (8)
---
AO1 4, AO2 4. Levels of response.
Level 4 (7-8): complex explanation of changes, showing how and why lives were affected for different young people, supported by precise knowledge.
Level 3 (5-6): developed explanation of two or more ways, with supporting knowledge.
Level 2 (3-4): simple explanation of one way.
Level 1 (1-2): basic description of changes.

Indicative content:
- **Youth movements:** the Hitler Youth and the League of German Maidens grew until membership was made compulsory in 1936 (enforced more strictly from 1939); rival youth groups were closed down. Boys did sport, camping and military drill; girls trained for motherhood.
- **Education:** teachers had to join the Nazi Teachers' League; textbooks were rewritten; subjects such as race studies were added, and more time was given to PE; Jewish teachers were dismissed.
- **Different experiences for boys and girls:** boys were prepared to become soldiers; girls were encouraged towards marriage and home rather than university or careers.
- **Persecution of some young people:** Jewish children were forced out of state schools by 1938 and suffered humiliation in class.
- **Limits:** some young people found the Hitler Youth boring or too regimented, and a minority later formed groups such as the Edelweiss Pirates and the Swing Youth.

::: q 6-7 1AB.2
**6.** Which of the following was the more important reason why Hitler was able to establish a dictatorship in the years 1933-1934:
- the Reichstag Fire
- the Night of the Long Knives?

Explain your answer with reference to both bullet points. (12)
---
AO1 6, AO2 6. Levels of response.
Level 4 (10-12): complex explanation of both bullets, leading to a sustained judgement about which was more important, supported by precise knowledge.
Level 3 (7-9): developed explanation of both bullets.
Level 2 (4-6): simple explanation of one or both bullets.
Level 1 (1-3): basic explanation; general knowledge.

Indicative content:
- **The Reichstag Fire (27 February 1933):** blamed on a Dutch communist, Marinus van der Lubbe; Hitler used it to persuade Hindenburg to sign an emergency decree that suspended civil liberties, allowing thousands of communists to be arrested before the March 1933 election. The atmosphere of fear helped the Nazis win more seats, and banning the communist deputies helped him pass the Enabling Act (23 March 1933), which let him make laws without the Reichstag. Trade unions (May) and other parties (July) were then banned.
- **The Night of the Long Knives (30 June 1934):** Hitler had Röhm and other SA leaders, and old opponents such as von Schleicher, killed. This removed the main threat from inside the Nazi movement and won the support of the army, which feared the SA. When Hindenburg died in August 1934, Hitler combined the posts of President and Chancellor as Führer, and the army swore an oath of loyalty to him personally.
- **Judgement:** e.g. the Reichstag Fire was more important because it began the legal destruction of democracy and led directly to the Enabling Act; or the Night of the Long Knives was more important because without the army's support Hitler could not have become Führer. The best answers link the two: the Fire gave him legal power, the Purge secured physical power.

**Section B: Conflict and tension: the inter-war years, 1918-1939**

**Source A** - a cartoon published in a British newspaper in October 1938, described for this mock: Chamberlain is drawn as a waiter in a restaurant, carrying a large dish labelled "Czechoslovakia" to a table where Hitler sits with a napkin tucked in. Hitler is already reading the menu, on which "Poland" and "Danzig" are written. On the wall a clock is labelled "Peace for our time".

**1.** Source A is critical of the Munich Agreement. How do you know? Explain your answer using Source A and your contextual knowledge. (4)
---
AO3. Levels of response.
Level 2 (3-4): developed analysis of the source's content and/or provenance, supported by relevant contextual knowledge, to explain why it is critical.
Level 1 (1-2): simple analysis, identifying a critical feature of the source with little support.

Indicative content:
- **Content:** Chamberlain is shown serving Czechoslovakia to Hitler like a meal, suggesting Britain handed over another country's territory without being asked by its people - at Munich (29-30 September 1938) the Sudetenland was given to Germany and Czechoslovakia was not invited.
- **Content:** Hitler is already reading "Poland" and "Danzig" on the menu, suggesting appeasement will only increase his appetite; knowledge that Hitler went on to occupy the rest of Czechoslovakia in March 1939 and to demand Danzig supports this.
- **Content:** the clock mocks Chamberlain's claim of "peace for our time".
- **Provenance:** a British newspaper in October 1938, published straight after Munich, when a minority (such as Churchill) were already attacking the agreement although many people welcomed it.

::: q 8-9 1BB.2b
**Source B** - a summary, written for this mock, of the points made by Haile Selassie, Emperor of Abyssinia, in his speech to the Assembly of the League of Nations in Geneva, June 1936: he described the use of poison gas against his soldiers and civilians; he said Abyssinia had trusted the League's promise of collective security and had been left to face Italy alone; he warned that if the League abandoned a small member state, no small nation would be safe in future.

**Source C** - a cartoon from a British magazine, December 1935, described for this mock: Mussolini stands over a map of Abyssinia holding a knife and fork. Two men labelled "Hoare" and "Laval" bow as they offer him a large slice of the country on a plate. Behind them a woman in a robe labelled "League of Nations" covers her eyes.

**2.** How useful are Sources B and C to an historian studying the Abyssinian crisis? Explain your answer using Sources B and C and your contextual knowledge. (12)
---
AO3. Levels of response.
Level 4 (10-12): complex evaluation of both sources, using content and provenance with detailed contextual knowledge, leading to a sustained judgement about their usefulness for the enquiry.
Level 3 (7-9): developed evaluation of both sources based on content and/or provenance.
Level 2 (4-6): simple evaluation of one or both sources.
Level 1 (1-3): basic analysis of the sources; description of content.

Indicative content:
- **Source B content:** useful for the human cost of the invasion (Italy did use poison gas) and for showing why the crisis destroyed faith in collective security - the League had named Italy the aggressor but its sanctions were weak and did not include oil.
- **Source B provenance:** the Emperor was the victim and was appealing for help, so he stresses the League's failure; but that is exactly what makes it useful - it shows how the League looked to the small states it was meant to protect. By June 1936 Italy had captured Addis Ababa and the League was about to drop sanctions (July 1936).
- **Source C content:** useful for the Hoare-Laval Pact (December 1935), the secret plan by the British and French foreign ministers to give Italy much of Abyssinia; the League covering her eyes shows that its leading members had betrayed it.
- **Source C provenance:** a British magazine shows that the plan caused outrage in Britain when it was leaked - Hoare resigned - so it is useful for British public opinion, though it is a satire, not a neutral account.
- **Limitations and context:** neither source shows why Britain and France held back: fear of driving Mussolini towards Hitler after the Stresa Front, the economic weakness of the Depression, and the decision not to close the Suez Canal.
- **Judgement:** together they are very useful for why the crisis was so damaging - B from the victim's side, C from the British public's - but less useful for the motives of the governments involved.

::: q 8-9 1BB.1
**3.** Write an account of how the different aims of the Big Three led to disagreements at the Paris Peace Conference in 1919. (8)
---
AO1 4, AO2 4. Levels of response.
Level 4 (7-8): a coherent narrative that analyses causation, showing how one development led to another, supported by precise knowledge.
Level 3 (5-6): a developed narrative with analysis of how the aims caused disagreement.
Level 2 (3-4): a simple narrative with some analysis of causes.
Level 1 (1-2): basic description of the aims or the conference.

Indicative content:
- **Clemenceau** wanted security for France and revenge after the destruction of northern France: a crippled German army, large reparations, and the Rhineland separated from Germany.
- **Wilson** wanted a fair peace based on the Fourteen Points: self-determination, disarmament for all, free trade and a League of Nations; he feared a harsh treaty would make Germany seek revenge.
- **Lloyd George** was caught between them: the British public wanted Germany to pay ("make Germany pay" was a 1918 election theme), but he wanted Germany to recover as a trading partner and as a barrier to communism, and wanted to protect Britain's navy and empire.
- **How the aims clashed:** Wilson and Clemenceau clashed over the Rhineland and the Saar; the compromise was a demilitarised Rhineland occupied by Allied troops for 15 years and the Saar run by the League; Lloyd George resisted the most extreme French demands but backed reparations and the loss of the German colonies.
- **Outcome:** the Treaty of Versailles (June 1919) was a compromise that fully satisfied none of them - too harsh for Wilson, too soft for many French.

::: q 8-9 1BB.2a 1BB.2b
**4.** "The main reason why the League of Nations failed to keep the peace was that the USA never joined it." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. (16 marks, plus 4 for spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terms) (20)
---
AO1 8, AO2 8, plus SPaG 4. Levels of response.
Level 4 (13-16): complex explanation of the stated factor and other factors, leading to a sustained judgement, supported by precise knowledge.
Level 3 (9-12): developed explanation of the stated factor and at least one other factor.
Level 2 (5-8): simple explanation of the stated factor or other factors.
Level 1 (1-4): basic explanation; general knowledge.
SPaG: high performance 4 (accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar, a wide range of specialist terms); intermediate 2-3; threshold 1.

Indicative content:
- **The USA's absence (agree):** the US Senate refused to join, so the League lacked the world's richest power; economic sanctions were weaker because members could still trade with the USA; the USA could not supply troops; and Britain and France, weakened by war, were left to lead. During the Abyssinian crisis, one reason given for not banning oil sales was that the USA, a non-member, would keep supplying Italy.
- **Other membership problems:** Germany was not admitted until 1926 and left in 1933; the USSR joined only in 1934; Japan and Italy left after their aggression.
- **Structure and powers:** decisions of the Council and Assembly needed unanimity; the League had no army of its own; sanctions took a long time to agree.
- **The self-interest of Britain and France:** they put their own interests first, shown most clearly by the Hoare-Laval Pact (1935) and their unwillingness to act over Manchuria.
- **The Depression:** it made countries reluctant to impose sanctions that would damage their own trade, and pushed Japan, Italy and Germany towards expansion.
- **Successes in the 1920s:** the Aaland Islands (1921), Upper Silesia (1921) and Bulgaria (1925) show the League could work against smaller powers even without the USA - suggesting the deeper problem was facing down Great Powers.
- **Judgement:** e.g. the USA's absence weakened the League from the start, but it failed in the 1930s mainly because the Depression encouraged aggression and Britain and France would not use force against Great Powers.
:::
