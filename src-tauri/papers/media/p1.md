In Question 4(c) you are rewarded for drawing together knowledge and understanding from across your full course of study, including different areas of the theoretical framework and media contexts.

(c) Explain how and why audiences use Desert Island Discs. Refer to the Uses and Gratifications theory to support your points. (12)

(12)
---
Level-marked, 12 marks (AO1):
- 10–12: excellent, detailed knowledge and understanding of how and why audiences use the programme; the theory applied accurately and relevantly throughout; draws together industry, audience and context (synoptic).
- 7–9: good understanding; theory applied appropriately; some linking of areas.
- 4–6: satisfactory; theory named with some application.
- 1–3: basic; theory absent or inaccurate.

**2. Representation: magazines** (continued)

Question 2(b) is based on the GQ front cover (August 2019) from the set products, and on the unseen cover described below.

> **Unseen resource: *PEAK* men's fitness magazine front cover (2024).** *PEAK* is a monthly fitness and lifestyle title. The masthead runs across the top in heavy, black capital letters on a white background. The cover star is a white British actor in his early forties, photographed in a mid shot against a plain grey studio backdrop. He is shirtless, with a muscular, oiled torso, and his arms are folded; he looks straight at the camera without smiling. The main cover line reads "Built to last: how I got the best body of my life at 42". Other cover lines, in red and black, read "Dad bod to beast mode in 8 weeks", "The protein plan that actually works", "Your best-ever arms" and, in a small box in the bottom corner, "It's OK not to be OK: men open up about stress". A red flash in the top corner promises "Free 12-week workout guide".

You will be assessed on the quality of your written response, including the ability to construct and develop a sustained line of reasoning which is coherent, relevant, substantiated and logically structured.

(b) Compare the representation of men in the GQ and *PEAK* front covers.

In your answer, you must consider:
- the choices the producers have made about how to represent men
- how far the representation of men is similar in the two magazine covers
- how far the representation of men is different in the two magazine covers. (25)

(25)
---
Level-marked, 25 marks (AO2 analysis and comparison):
- 21–25: excellent, perceptive comparison; detailed analysis of both covers; judgements about how far they are similar and different are fully supported; relevant theory (such as selection and construction, stereotypes, or ideas about changing masculinities) used to sharpen points; sustained, logically structured argument.
- 16–20: good comparison with reasonably detailed analysis of both; clear judgements; appropriate terminology and some theory.
- 11–15: satisfactory; some comparison, but analysis less developed or uneven between the covers.
- 6–10: basic; mainly description of each cover in turn with little comparison.
- 1–5: minimal; very limited reference to either cover.

Indicative content. Similarities: both select a single male star in direct address, a convention that positions him as a role model to admire; both foreground a muscular, idealised body, reinforcing the expectation that men should be strong and physically disciplined (a body-focused masculinity); both use cover lines that sell self-improvement and consumerism (style tips in GQ; workout and protein plans in *PEAK*); both construct masculinity as success and control. Differences: GQ's star is a black British footballer framed through the cover line as a moral leader who stands up to racism, so masculinity is linked to principle and social influence, while *PEAK* uses a white actor whose achievement is personal and physical; GQ's gold, black and white palette, high fashion styling and angel wings connote luxury, heroism and sophistication for an affluent ABC1 reader, while *PEAK*'s plain studio, heavy font and red flashes connote effort, discipline and a mass-market "gym" identity; *PEAK* addresses age and fatherhood ("dad bod"), challenging the idea that fitness belongs to the young; *PEAK*'s small mental-health line hints at a more open, emotional masculinity, but its size and placement make it secondary to the body-focused message, while GQ mixes fashion with politics and current affairs. Better answers judge how far each cover reinforces or challenges traditional masculinity and explain the producers' purposes and target audiences.
