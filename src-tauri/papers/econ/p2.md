**1.** (a) Define capital as a factor of production. (2)

(b) Explain, with an example of each, the difference between labour-intensive and capital-intensive production. (2)

(4)
---
(a) Man-made goods used to produce other goods and services (1), such as machinery, factories and tools (1).

(b) Labour-intensive uses a high proportion of labour to capital, e.g. hairdressing or hand-made furniture (1); capital-intensive uses a high proportion of machinery, e.g. car assembly or oil refining (1).

::: q 4-5 2.2 2.5
**2.** (a) State two factors that would shift the demand curve for electric cars to the right. (2)

(b) Explain the effect of this shift on the equilibrium price and quantity of electric cars. (2)

(4)
---
(a) Any two: a rise in incomes; a rise in the price of petrol cars (a substitute); a fall in the price of home chargers (a complement); a change in tastes towards green products; a government subsidy to buyers (2).

(b) At the old price there is a shortage, so price rises (1); firms extend supply, so equilibrium quantity rises too (1).

::: q 4-5 2.7
**5.** (a) Define price elasticity of supply. (1)

(b) Explain why the supply of fresh fish is price inelastic. (2)

(3)
---
(a) The responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price (1).

(b) Fish cannot be stored for long, so stocks cannot be released when price rises (1); catching more takes time — boats, crews and quotas cannot be increased quickly (1).

::: q 6-7 3.4
**6.** (a) Explain two types of economy of scale a supermarket chain might enjoy. (4)

(b) Explain one diseconomy of scale it might suffer. (2)

(6)
---
(a) Any two of: purchasing — buying in bulk gets lower prices from suppliers, cutting average cost (2); technical — large warehouses and automated tills spread the cost of equipment over more sales (2); managerial — specialist buyers and accountants; financial — cheaper borrowing; marketing — one national advert serves all stores (2 each).

(b) Communication becomes slower and harder across many stores and layers of management (1); decisions are delayed or mistaken, so average cost rises (1).
:::

::: q 6-7 3.3
**7.** Explain, using demand and supply, why doctors earn more than cleaners. (4)
---
Supply of doctors is small because years of training and high qualifications are needed (1); supply of cleaners is large because little training is needed, so many people can do the job (1); demand for doctors is high and inelastic because they are essential and have no close substitutes (1); the equilibrium wage is therefore far higher for doctors than for cleaners (1).
:::

::: q 6-7 4.3 4.1
**8.** (a) Explain how a rise in the interest rate can reduce the rate of inflation. (4)

(b) Explain one conflict between macroeconomic aims. (2)

(6)
---
(a) Borrowing becomes more expensive so households take fewer loans and spend less (1); saving becomes more rewarding, again cutting spending (1); firms invest less; total demand falls (1); with less demand, firms cannot raise prices as fast, so inflation falls (1).

(b) Low unemployment against low inflation: policies that raise demand create jobs but push up prices (1); or growth against the environment, or growth against a current account surplus as imports rise (1).
:::

::: q 6-7 5.3
**9.** (a) Explain two reasons why birth rates fall as a country develops. (2)

(b) Explain one economic consequence of an ageing population. (2)

(4)
---
(a) Any two: more women in education and work delay having children; contraception is available; fewer children die, so families need fewer births; children are a cost rather than a source of farm labour; pensions replace children as support in old age (2).

(b) A higher dependency ratio — fewer workers support more pensioners (1); so taxes rise or pensions and healthcare spending strain the budget, and the labour force may shrink (1).
:::

::: q 6-7 2.10 2.9
**10.** Explain why street lighting is provided by the government rather than by private firms. (3)
---
Street lighting is a public good: non-excludable — people cannot be stopped from using it if they do not pay (1); non-rival — one person's use does not reduce another's (1); so everyone would free-ride, no firm could make a profit and the market would fail to provide it, so the government funds it from taxes (1).
:::

::: q 8-9 3.7
**11.** Analyse the effects on consumers when a firm becomes a monopoly. (6)
---
With no competitors the monopolist can raise prices above the competitive level (1); output is restricted, so consumers get less (1); with no rival to switch to, quality and service may fall and choice is reduced (1); however, the monopolist may achieve economies of scale that lower costs, and some of that may be passed on (1); its profits can fund research and new products (1); the overall effect depends on regulation and on whether the market is contestable (1).
:::

::: q 8-9 4.4 4.6
**12.** Discuss whether supply-side policy is the best way to reduce unemployment. (6)
---
For: education and training reduce structural unemployment by giving workers the skills firms need (1); cutting income tax and benefits increases the incentive to work (1); reducing regulation makes firms more willing to hire; effects last because productive capacity rises (1). Against: it takes years to work, so it does nothing for cyclical unemployment in a recession — fiscal or monetary policy raising demand acts faster (1); it can be costly and may widen inequality if benefits are cut (1). Judgement: best for structural and frictional unemployment; for a demand-side slump, demand-side policy is needed alongside (1).
:::

::: q 8-9 5.4 6.1
**13.** Discuss whether specialising in one export crop always benefits a developing country. (6)
---
For: it can exploit its comparative advantage — climate and cheap labour (1); larger output brings economies of scale and export earnings that fund development (1); trade gives access to goods it cannot make cheaply (1). Against: dependence on one crop leaves the country exposed to price swings and bad harvests (1); primary product prices tend to fall relative to manufactured goods, worsening the terms of trade, and little value is added at home (1). Judgement: specialisation helps in the short term, but diversification into processing and manufacturing is needed for lasting development (1).
:::
