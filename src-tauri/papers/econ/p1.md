::: q 4-5
**2.** The table shows the market for bicycles.

| Price | 100 | 150 | 200 | 250 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity demanded | 900 | 700 | 500 | 300 |
| Quantity supplied | 300 | 500 | 700 | 900 |

(a) State the equilibrium price and quantity. (2)

(b) Calculate the shortage or surplus at a price of 100 and explain what will happen to the price. (2)

(c) Explain the effect of a government subsidy to bicycle makers on the equilibrium price and quantity. (2)

(6)
---
(a) Price 150 (1); quantity 500 (1).

(b) Shortage of 600 (1); consumers bid the price up until demand contracts and supply extends to equilibrium (1).

(c) Supply increases (shifts right) (1); price falls and quantity rises (1).

::: q 6-7
**3.** The price of a magazine rises from 4 to 5 and weekly sales fall from 2000 to 1700.

(a) Calculate the price elasticity of demand and state whether demand is elastic or inelastic. (3)

(b) Explain the effect of the price rise on the publisher's total revenue. (3)

(6)
---
(a) % change in Qd = −15%; % change in price = +25% (1); PED = −0.6 (1); inelastic (1).

(b) Revenue rises from 8000 to 8500 (1); because demand is inelastic, the percentage fall in sales is smaller than the percentage rise in price (1); so revenue increases (1).
:::

::: q 6-7
**4.** A chemical factory releases waste into a river used by fishermen.

(a) Explain, using the idea of external costs, why the market produces too much of the chemical. (4)

(b) Explain one policy a government could use to correct this. (2)

(6)
---
(a) The damage to the fishermen is an external cost not paid by the factory (1); the factory's private cost is below the social cost (1); the price is too low and output too high compared with the socially optimal level (1); resources are misallocated — market failure (1).

(b) A tax equal to the external cost raises the firm's costs and cuts output (1); or regulation setting limits on waste, with fines (1).
:::

**5.** A country's consumer price index rises from 125 to 130 in a year.

(a) Calculate the rate of inflation. (2)

(b) Explain two consequences of inflation for the country. (4)

(6)
---
(a) $5 \div 125 \times 100$ (1) $= 4\%$ (1).

(b) Exports become less competitive, worsening the current account (2); savers lose as the real value of savings falls, or uncertainty reduces investment, or menu costs (2).

::: q 6-7
**6.** (a) Explain the difference between cyclical and structural unemployment. (4)

(b) Explain one policy a government could use to reduce cyclical unemployment. (2)

(6)
---
(a) Cyclical: caused by a fall in total demand during a recession, affecting all industries (2); structural: caused by the decline of an industry or a mismatch of skills, e.g. mining (2).

(b) Expansionary fiscal policy — cutting taxes or raising government spending (1) to raise total demand so firms produce more and hire workers (1).
:::

::: q 8-9
**7.** Analyse how a rise in a country's interest rate might affect its exchange rate and its current account. (6)
---
Higher interest rates attract foreign savers seeking better returns (1); demand for the currency rises, so it appreciates (1); exports become more expensive abroad and imports cheaper (1); export sales fall and import spending rises (1); the current account moves toward deficit (1); the size of the effect depends on price elasticities of demand for exports and imports (1).
:::

::: q 8-9
**8.** Discuss whether a government should protect its industries with tariffs. (8)
---
For: protects jobs in declining industries and allows infant industries to grow (1); protects strategic industries and against dumping (1); raises tax revenue and can improve the current account (1). Against: consumers pay higher prices with less choice (1); other countries may retaliate, reducing exports (1); protected firms become inefficient (1); resources are kept in industries where the country lacks comparative advantage (1). Judgement: justified for a limited time for infant or strategic industries, but long-term protection usually costs more than it saves (1).
:::

::: q 8-9
**9.** Discuss whether economic growth always improves living standards. (8)
---
For: higher incomes allow more goods and services (1); more tax revenue funds health and education (1); jobs are created and poverty can fall (1). Against: growth may be unequally shared, so many see no gain (1); pollution, congestion and resource depletion reduce quality of life (1); longer working hours or stress; GDP ignores leisure and unpaid work (1); HDI may not rise if growth is not spent on health and education (1). Judgement: growth usually raises living standards on average but not always for everyone or sustainably — it depends on how it is distributed and used (1).
:::
