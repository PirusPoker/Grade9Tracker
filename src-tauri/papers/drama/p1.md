**Section A: Theatre roles and terminology**

**01.** During a run of performances, who is responsible for calling the lighting and sound cues and making sure every performance runs as rehearsed?

A The director
B The stage manager
C The playwright

Write the letter of the correct answer. (1)
---
B, the stage manager (1). The director's work is largely finished by press night; the stage manager (or deputy stage manager on the book) runs each performance and calls the cues.
:::

::: q 4-5 3.1.1b
**02.** In a studio theatre the audience sits in two blocks facing each other, with the acting area running down the middle between them. What type of staging is this?

A Thrust
B End on
C Traverse

Write the letter of the correct answer. (1)
---
C, traverse (1). Thrust has the audience on three sides; end on has them on one side only.
:::

::: q 4-5 3.1.1b
**03.** On a proscenium arch stage, an actor stands close to the front edge of the stage, on the actor's own left as they face the audience. What is the actor's stage position?

A Downstage left
B Upstage right
C Downstage right

Write the letter of the correct answer. (1)
---
A, downstage left (1). Stage left and right are always the actor's left and right when facing the audience; downstage is the area nearest the audience.
:::

**Section B: The Crucible**

The extract for Question 05 is the opening of **Act Two**, from the start of the act, when Proctor comes in from planting and finds Elizabeth upstairs with the children, to the moment Mary Warren enters. Find it in your copy of the play. Answer 05.1, 05.2 and 05.3, then either 05.4 or 05.5.

**05.1** You are designing a costume for Elizabeth to wear in a performance of this extract.

The costume must reflect the context of *The Crucible*, set in a Puritan community in Salem, Massachusetts, in 1692. Describe your design ideas for the costume. (4)
---
Mark as a single judgement, 1 mark per level. 4: an excellent, precise design entirely right for the brief, showing secure knowledge of the play and of costume (fabric, colour, cut, condition and accessories all specified and justified by context). 3: a good design with a number of precise details and clear links to context. 2: a reasonable design with a few precise details, some relevance to the Puritan setting. 1: a limited design with minimal detail or weak appropriateness (for example "a long old-fashioned dress").

Indicative content: an ankle-length, long-sleeved dress in coarse homespun wool or linen, in a sober colour such as dark brown, muted grey-green or faded indigo (Puritans avoided show; full black was costly and more usual for Sunday best); a plain white linen coif covering all her hair, reflecting the expectation that a married woman is modest; a wide, plain white collar or kerchief closed at the throat; a stained, well-used apron because she has been cooking and runs a working farm; no jewellery, lace or ribbons, as ornament was seen as vanity; sturdy, low-heeled leather shoes worn at the toe; the fabric slightly faded and patched to show a household that works the land; tight, buttoned bodice suggesting restraint and her controlled, cool manner towards John in this scene.
:::

**05.2** You are performing the role of Proctor.

Near the end of the extract, after Elizabeth tells him that the magistrate who judges him sits in his own heart, Proctor answers her with a bitter laugh and the line ending "your justice would freeze beer".

Describe how you would use your vocal and physical skills to perform this line and explain the effects you want to create. (8)
---
Levels. 7–8: exact description of both vocal and physical skills with precise detail throughout; the intended effects are entirely right for Proctor and for the moment, and are fully explained. 5–6: clear description with a number of precise details; effects are appropriate and clearly explained. 3–4: reasonably clear description with a few details; effects have some relevance. 1–2: limited description (for example "say it angrily") with effects unexplained. A response covering only voice or only movement cannot go above the 3–4 band.

Indicative content: vocal - a short, humourless laugh through the nose before the line, then the opening "Oh, Elizabeth" on a falling pitch, weary rather than shouted; a sardonic, clipped tone; stress on "justice" and a pause before "freeze beer" so the rural image lands with sarcasm; volume kept low and controlled to show anger he is suppressing, then a slight rise at the end that betrays hurt; a flat Massachusetts-farmer register rather than received pronunciation. Physical - he has been standing close to her trying to win her round; on the laugh he turns his back and moves away towards the hearth or door, breaking the proxemics; a dismissive gesture of the hand towards her; jaw tight, eyes to the ceiling on "justice"; shoulders dropping as the fight leaves him. Effects - the audience sees his guilt turning into resentment of her coldness; the humour is bitter, not comic; a moment of tension that is immediately broken by Mary Warren's entrance, so the pause after the line should be held.
:::

**05.3** You are performing the role of Elizabeth.

Focus on the part of the extract that runs from the moment Elizabeth learns that John was alone with Abigail to the end of the extract.

Explain how you might use the performance space and interact with the actor playing Proctor to show the audience the distrust between husband and wife. (12)
---
Levels. 10–12: an exact, well-developed explanation, entirely right for the brief, with precise detail throughout (specific positions, moves, proxemics and reactions tied to specific moments). 7–9: a clear, developed explanation with a number of precise details. 4–6: a reasonably clear explanation with a few details and some relevance. 1–3: limited, generalised explanation with minimal detail. Answers that ignore the space (only voice) or never refer to Proctor cap at 4–6.

Indicative content: use of the space - Elizabeth stays at or behind the table, using it as a barrier between them; at the discovery she stops still in the middle of a task, then moves slowly upstage away from him so he must turn his back to the audience to follow her; staging on a diagonal or at opposite ends of the room to widen the physical distance as the argument grows; levels - she remains seated, keeping her composure, while he paces and stands over her, showing his agitation and her quiet control; interaction - she avoids eye contact while he talks, then holds his gaze steadily on her calm, accusing lines; flinching or stepping back when he moves in; not responding to his attempt to touch her; she keeps her hands occupied (folding cloth, clearing the bowl) to show she is holding back emotion; a long pause before she speaks about the magistrate in his heart; her final line delivered without moving towards him, so his bitter reply and turn away create the widest gap on stage just as Mary Warren enters. Effects - the audience sees a marriage still frozen seven months after the affair, and the lack of trust that Abigail will exploit.
:::

**05.4** You are performing the role of Proctor.

Describe how you would use your acting skills to interpret Proctor's character.

Explain why your ideas are appropriate for:
- this extract
- the performance of your role in the play as a whole.

**or**

**05.5** You are a designer working on one aspect of design for this extract (lighting, sound, set, costume or puppets).

Describe how you would use your design skills to create effects which support the action.

Explain why your ideas are appropriate for:
- this extract
- your chosen design skill in the play as a whole.

(20)
---
Levels (either option). 16–20: excellent, exact and well-developed ideas entirely right for the extract, with an extensive range of skills precisely targeted at the character or action and excellent knowledge of the whole play; precise detail throughout. 11–15: clear, secure ideas with a wide range of skills, a good degree of appropriateness and good knowledge of the whole play. 6–10: reasonably clear ideas with a fair range of skills, some relevance and some knowledge of the whole play. 1–5: limited ideas with a narrow range, little detail and little sense of the whole play. An answer that never moves beyond the extract cannot go above 11–15; one that retells the plot without skills stays in 1–5.

Indicative content, 05.4 Proctor: a farmer in his thirties, physically strong and used to authority in his own house; voice - a deep, steady register with a rural Massachusetts accent, warm when he praises the stew and talks about the farm, clipped and rising in volume as Elizabeth's suspicion surfaces; physical - heavy, deliberate gait of a man who has been in the fields all day; the secret seasoning of the stew played as a small, guilty gesture that shows he is trying to please her; the awkward kiss on the cheek, received coolly; restless movement and hands gripping the back of a chair when she questions him; in the play as a whole - the same guilt drives his outburst in Act One and his confession of lechery in Act Three, where the voice must crack under strain; in Act Four the stooped, filthy, exhausted prisoner regains his height and strength of voice as he tears up the confession to keep his name. The extract should plant the tension between pride and guilt that the whole play resolves.

Indicative content, 05.5 design: lighting - late afternoon light through a small upstage window, warm amber but low in intensity, with a firelight flicker from the hearth (orange gel, flicker unit); as the quarrel grows, a slow, barely noticeable cross-fade to a colder, bluer state and shadows lengthening across the table, so the home feels less safe; sound - birdsong and distant cattle at the start to establish the farm and a moment of peace, the crackle of the fire and the scrape of the spoon; the outside sounds fade to silence under the argument so every pause is exposed; the sound of Mary Warren's footsteps on the path before she enters. Set - a low-ceilinged room of dark, rough-sawn timber, a long plain table and benches, a hearth with an iron pot, very little decoration, a musket on the wall; space kept wide and bare so the couple look isolated. Costume - Proctor's mud-stained work shirt, breeches and boots against Elizabeth's clean, closed-up dress, showing the gap between them. Whole play - the design shows Salem closing in: from the cramped bedroom in Act One, to the home in Act Two, the stark, high-walled meeting house in Act Three, and the dark cell in Act Four, with light draining away act by act until the final dawn light on Proctor's exit.

**Section C: Live theatre production**

State the title of the live or digital theatre production you saw. Answer **one** of Questions 14, 15 or 16 with reference to that production. You must write about a different play from the one you answered on in Section B.

**14.** Describe how two or more actors used their vocal and physical skills to show a conflict between their characters. Analyse and evaluate how successful they were in showing this conflict to the audience. You could refer to vocal skills (for example pitch, pace and tone), physical skills (for example body language and facial expression), and a scene or section and/or the production as a whole.

**15.** Describe how lighting was used to create mood and atmosphere. Analyse and evaluate how successful the lighting was in creating mood and atmosphere for the audience. You could refer to colour, intensity and direction, special effects and transitions, and a scene or section and/or the production as a whole.

**16.** Describe how the set design was used to establish where and when the play takes place. Analyse and evaluate how successful the set was in communicating this to the audience. You could refer to materials and effects, space, scale, levels and colour, and a scene or section and/or the production as a whole.

(32)
---
Marked in two parts and added together.

AO3 knowledge and understanding (12). 10–12: an excellent range of skills or design elements, precisely described with exact, detailed examples. 7–9: a good range, clearly described with a number of precise details. 4–6: a fair range with a few details, some of it generalised. 1–3: a narrow range, vague description.

AO4 analysis and evaluation (20). 16–20: critical and insightful; explains exactly how each choice communicated meaning and makes convincing, justified judgements about how well it worked, fully supported by specific moments. 11–15: developed and clear analysis and judgements, supported by a number of examples. 6–10: some analysis and judgement but often slips into description; points sound but not always explored. 1–5: mostly description or personal opinion without reasons.

Indicative content, 14: name the actors or characters and the conflict (family, romantic, power, generational); specific vocal choices (raised volume, clipped pace, a sudden drop to a whisper, overlapping lines, sarcastic tone) and physical choices (closing or opening the distance, one standing over another, turning the back, a slammed object, fixed eye contact, stillness); how the conflict built and peaked; judgements on how far it made the audience feel tension, sympathy or discomfort, and why. Indicative content, 15: specific states and cues (colour temperature, gobos, side light, backlight, follow spot, blackouts, snap versus slow fade, strobe or haze); link each to a moment and a mood (dread, intimacy, chaos, calm); judge effectiveness, including where the lighting was less successful. Indicative content, 16: the type of stage and how the set used it; period and place signalled through materials, furniture, colour palette, scale and levels; composite or abstract settings and how scene changes were handled (flying, trucks, revolve, projection); judge how clearly and how imaginatively the audience understood where and when. Top-band answers evaluate throughout rather than adding a judgement at the end, and use the correct terminology.
