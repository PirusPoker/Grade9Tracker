::: q 4-5 3.1.2
**12.** Explain two disadvantages of using fossil fuels to generate electricity. (4)
---
Any two explained points, 2 marks each (point 1, development 1):
- Burning coal, gas or oil releases carbon dioxide (1), a greenhouse gas that contributes to global warming and climate change (1).
- Fossil fuels are finite (1); reserves will run out, and prices rise as they become scarcer (1).
- Burning releases sulfur dioxide and particulates (1), causing acid rain and poor air quality/health problems (1).
- Extraction by mining or drilling damages landscapes and habitats (1), and oil spills pollute seas (1).

::: q 4-5 3.1.3
**13.** Explain what is meant by a smart material. (2)
---
A material with a property that changes significantly in response to an external stimulus such as heat, light, stress, moisture or pH (1); the change is controlled and reversible, so it returns when the stimulus is removed, e.g. thermochromic pigment or shape memory alloy (1).

**15.** A designer needs 40 rectangular panels, each 250 mm × 180 mm. They are cut from standard sheets 1200 mm × 600 mm, which cost £8.50 each. Ignore the width of the cut.

Calculate how many sheets are needed and the total cost of the sheets. Show your working. (4)
---
Panels per sheet: $1200 \div 250 = 4$ (rounded down) and $600 \div 180 = 3$ (rounded down), so $4 \times 3 = 12$ panels per sheet (1) (1). (The other orientation, $6 \times 2 = 12$, gives the same.)

Sheets: $40 \div 12 = 3.33$, so 4 sheets (must round up) (1).

Cost: $4 \times £8.50 = £34.00$ (1).

**19.** Designers are increasingly judged on the ecological and social footprint of the products they create.

Analyse and evaluate how designers can reduce the ecological and social footprint of products. Give examples of products in your answer. (8)
---
Levels of response:
- **7–8:** Detailed analysis of several ways to reduce both the ecological and social footprint, with a clear evaluation (judgements on how effective, what the trade-offs are) and well-chosen product examples throughout.
- **5–6:** Good analysis of several methods; some evaluation; some examples.
- **3–4:** Basic points, mostly descriptive, little or no evaluation; limited or generic examples.
- **1–2:** One or two simple points showing limited understanding.
- **0:** Nothing worthy of credit.

A response with no product examples is capped at 6.

Indicative content:
- Ecological: choose recycled/recyclable or renewable materials; FSC timber against deforestation; reduce material used (lightweighting, nesting); reduce product mileage by sourcing locally; lower-carbon manufacture and renewable energy in factories; design for disassembly and repair; the six Rs.
- Social: safe working conditions and fair wages in factories; fair trade sourcing; reduce pollution of oceans and air that harms communities; avoid harm to users.
- Evaluation: sustainable materials can cost more and raise prices; recycled polymers may be weaker or less consistent; local sourcing may not be possible for all materials; repairable products may sell fewer units, conflicting with business aims; a life cycle assessment is needed to judge overall impact.

**Section C: Designing and making principles**

**20.** Explain how each of the following improves the accuracy of parts made in a workshop.

(a) Working from a datum edge or face (2)

(b) Using a jig (2)

(4)
---
(a) All measurements are taken from one straight, true reference edge or surface (1), so errors do not build up from measuring off rough or uneven edges, and parts are consistent (1).

(b) A jig holds the work and/or guides the tool to the same position every time (1), so repeated operations such as drilling holes are identical without marking out each part, reducing human error (1).

**23.** A card box is made from a net cut from a sheet of A3 card, which measures 420 mm × 297 mm. The net is made of:
- four side panels, each 80 mm × 120 mm
- a base and a lid, each 80 mm × 80 mm
- one glue flap, 15 mm × 120 mm.

(a) Calculate the area of the net in mm². (2)

(b) One net is cut from each A3 sheet. Calculate the percentage of the sheet that is wasted. Give your answer to 1 decimal place. (3)

(c) State one way the manufacturer could reduce the waste. (1)

(6)
---
(a) Sides $4 \times 80 \times 120 = 38\,400$; base and lid $2 \times 80 \times 80 = 12\,800$; flap $15 \times 120 = 1800$ (1). Total $53\,000$ mm² (1).

(b) Sheet $420 \times 297 = 124\,740$ mm² (1). Waste $124\,740 - 53\,000 = 71\,740$ mm² (1). $71\,740 \div 124\,740 \times 100 = 57.5\%$ (1).

(c) Nest/tessellate several nets on a larger sheet or roll; redesign the net so shapes interlock; use offcuts for smaller parts; die cut many nets from one sheet (1).

**24.** A small company needs to make 50 identical bird boxes. It has two methods.

Method 1: one skilled worker cuts every part by hand. Materials cost £4.20 per box. Labour costs £18 per hour, and each box takes 0.75 hours.

Method 2: parts are cut on a CNC router. There is a one-off programming and set-up charge of £240. Materials cost £3.90 per box, because the parts are nested more efficiently. Machine and operator time is 0.2 hours per box at £30 per hour.

(a) Calculate the total cost of making 50 boxes by Method 1. Show your working. (2)

(b) Calculate the total cost of making 50 boxes by Method 2. Show your working. (4)

(c) Which method is more cost effective? Explain one other factor, apart from cost, the company should consider. (2)

(8)
---
(a) Labour per box $0.75 \times 18 = £13.50$; per box $£13.50 + £4.20 = £17.70$ (1). $50 \times 17.70 = £885$ (1).

(b) Machine time per box $0.2 \times 30 = £6.00$ (1); per box $£6.00 + £3.90 = £9.90$ (1); $50 \times 9.90 = £495$ (1); plus set-up $£495 + £240 = £735$ (1).

(c) Method 2 is cheaper by £150 (1). Other factor explained, e.g. CNC parts are more accurate and identical, improving quality; the program can be reused for future batches, spreading the set-up cost further; hand-making keeps a skilled job but is slower (1).

Fair trade guarantees producers in developing countries a fair minimum price and safe working conditions (1); designers may specify fair trade materials such as cotton, and the certification can be used as a selling point for ethically minded consumers (1); but it raises material costs and may limit supply, so the product may need a higher price or a simpler design to stay affordable (1).

::: q 8-9 3.3.4 3.3.6
**27.** Explain how iterative design and avoiding design fixation can lead to a better final prototype. Give examples to support your answer. (6)
---
Levels of response:
- **5–6:** Clear, detailed explanation of both iterative design and design fixation, showing how each improves the outcome, with relevant examples.
- **3–4:** Some explanation of both, or good explanation of one; some examples.
- **1–2:** Basic definitions with little explanation.
- **0:** Nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content:
- Iterative design: a repeating cycle of design, model/prototype, test, evaluate and refine; each loop fixes problems found in testing and responds to user feedback, so faults are found cheaply early (e.g. card models of a phone stand revealing the angle is wrong).
- Design fixation: sticking to a first idea or copying existing products, which limits creativity.
- Ways to avoid it: collaboration and feedback from others, generating many ideas quickly, looking at unrelated products for inspiration, user-centred research, SCAMPER-style changes to an existing idea.
- Effect: more innovative, better-tested, fit-for-purpose prototype that meets the brief.
