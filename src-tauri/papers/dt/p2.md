**11.** Explain two advantages to a manufacturer of using computer aided manufacture (CAM). (4)
---
Any two explained advantages, 2 marks each (point 1, development 1):
- High accuracy and repeatability (1): every part is identical, so fewer rejects and parts fit together (1).
- Faster production (1): machines run continuously, including overnight, lowering unit cost (1).
- Fewer skilled workers needed (1): lower labour costs, and one operator can run several machines (1).
- Complex shapes can be made (1) that would be very hard by hand, e.g. 3D-routed curves (1).
- Designs go straight from CAD to the machine (1), so changes are quick and prototypes are made sooner (1).

::: q 4-5 3.1.5
**12.** A wheelbarrow is a second order lever. The load of 600 N acts 0.4 m from the pivot (the wheel), and the effort is applied at the handles 1.2 m from the pivot.

(a) Calculate the effort needed to lift the load. Show your working. (2)

(b) Calculate the mechanical advantage. (1)

(c) Name one product that uses a third order lever. (1)

(4)
---
(a) Moments balance: $\text{effort} \times 1.2 = 600 \times 0.4$ (1), so effort $= 240 \div 1.2 = 200$ N (1).

(b) $\text{MA} = \text{load} \div \text{effort} = 600 \div 200 = 3$ (1).

(c) Tweezers, tongs, fishing rod, a stapler used as a remover, the human forearm (1).
:::

::: q 4-5 3.1.6.1b 3.1.6.2
**13.** Explain why a thermosetting polymer such as urea-formaldehyde is used for electrical plug casings. (2)
---
It is an electrical insulator, protecting the user from shocks (1); it will not soften or melt when the plug gets hot, because its cross-linked structure does not re-soften on heating, and it is hard and rigid (1).

**Section B: Specialist technical principles**

**14.** For one material category you have studied (papers and boards, timber based materials, metal based materials, polymers or textile based materials):

(a) Name the primary source of the raw material. (1)

(b) Describe the main stages in converting the raw material into a workable form. (4)

(5)
---
(a) Correct primary source, e.g. trees/wood and grasses (paper); trees (timber); metal ores mined from the ground (metals); crude oil (polymers); plants, animals or oil-based chemicals (textiles) (1).

(b) Four accurate stages in logical order, 1 mark each, e.g.
- Paper: logs debarked and chipped; pulped mechanically or chemically to free cellulose fibres; pulp cleaned, possibly bleached and mixed with water; spread on a mesh, pressed, dried and rolled into sheets.
- Timber: tree felled; logs converted (sawn) into planks by through-and-through or quarter sawing; seasoned in air or a kiln to reduce moisture; planed to standard sizes (or veneers/particles glued and pressed into manufactured boards).
- Metals: ore mined and crushed; ore concentrated; metal extracted by heating in a blast furnace with carbon or by electrolysis; refined and cast into ingots, then rolled into stock forms.
- Polymers: crude oil extracted; separated into fractions by fractional distillation; heavy fractions cracked into smaller molecules such as ethene; monomers polymerised into granules or powder.
- Textiles: fibres harvested (cotton picked, wool sheared) or extruded from polymer through a spinneret; cleaned and carded to align fibres; spun and twisted into yarn; woven or knitted into fabric.

::: q 6-7 3.2.5b 3.2.8a
**18.** Choose one of these processes: vacuum forming, die cutting, turning on a centre lathe, soldering a component to a PCB, pleating fabric.

Using notes and/or sketches, describe the stages of your chosen process. (5)
---
Five accurate stages in a logical order, 1 mark each, e.g.
- Vacuum forming: mould made with draft angles and rounded edges; HIPS sheet clamped in the frame; heater brings the sheet to its softening point; platen with mould raised; vacuum pump draws air out so the sheet is pulled over the mould; allowed to cool, removed and trimmed.
- Die cutting: die made from steel rules bent to the net outline, with blunt creasing rules for folds; set in a press; sheet card fed in; press brings die down to cut and crease in one action; nets removed from waste.
- Turning: bar held in a three-jaw chuck; tool set to centre height; lathe speed set for material and diameter; guard closed; facing, then parallel turning in several cuts; tapers or chamfers; check with vernier callipers.
- Soldering: clean board and component legs; insert component through holes; heat pad and leg together with the iron; feed in solder so it flows round to form a cone; remove solder then iron; trim leg.
- Pleating: mark pleat lines with tailor's chalk from the pattern; fold fabric along lines; pin and tack; press with an iron; stitch across the top to hold.

**19.** Analyse and evaluate the use of tolerances and quality control in the commercial manufacture of products. Give examples of products in your answer. (8)
---
Levels of response:
- **7–8:** Detailed analysis of why tolerances are set and how quality control is carried out, with clear evaluation (benefits weighed against costs and limits) and relevant examples from products.
- **5–6:** Good analysis with some evaluation and some examples.
- **3–4:** Basic, mainly descriptive points; little evaluation; generic examples.
- **1–2:** Limited points showing limited understanding.
- **0:** Nothing worthy of credit.

A response with no product examples is capped at 6.

Indicative content:
- Tolerances: a permitted range of sizes (e.g. ±0.1 mm); ensure interchangeable parts fit (flat-pack cam fittings, a polymer lid snapping onto a container, a resistor's ±5% band).
- Tight tolerances raise cost: slower machining, better equipment, more rejects; loose tolerances cheaper but poorer fit. Designers set only as tight as function needs.
- Quality control checks during manufacture: registration marks on printed packaging; go/no-go gauges on timber parts; depth stops; laser settings for acrylic; checking a print repeat on fabric; PCB etching times; sampling a batch rather than checking every item.
- Evaluation: finds faults early, reducing waste and protecting reputation; but costs time and money; sampling can miss faults; automated vision systems cost more to install but check every item at speed; quality assurance across the whole system is more effective than inspection alone.

**24.** A designer is planning the handle of a tool. The table gives anthropometric data for adult palm breadth.

| Percentile | 5th | 50th | 95th |
|---|---|---|---|
| Palm breadth (mm) | 71 | 80 | 89 |

(a) Explain why designers often design for the 5th to the 95th percentile. (2)

(b) The handle length must equal the 95th percentile palm breadth plus 15% clearance. Calculate the handle length to 1 decimal place. (2)

(c) The whole tool is 850 mm long. It is drawn at a scale of 1:5. How long is it on the drawing? (1)

(d) In a survey of 40 users, 26 preferred a rubber grip and the rest did not. Express this as a percentage, and as a ratio of preferred to not preferred in its simplest form. (3)

(8)
---
(a) This range covers 90% of the population (1); designing for the extremes of 0–100% would be impractical or costly, and the 5th and 95th percentiles set the smallest and largest sizes that must be accommodated (1).

(b) $89 \times 1.15$ (1) $= 102.35$, so $102.4$ mm (1).

(c) $850 \div 5 = 170$ mm (1).

(d) $26 \div 40 \times 100 = 65\%$ (1). Not preferred $40 - 26 = 14$ (1). Ratio $26 : 14 = 13 : 7$ (1).
