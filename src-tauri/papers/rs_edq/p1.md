Allow about 30 minutes per theme, with roughly half of it on the (d) answer. Each theme is split into two blocks here, and the paper ramps from grade 4-5 (Relationships) to grade 8-9 (Human Rights).

::: q 4-5 2.1a 2.1b
**1. Issues of Relationships**

**(a)** What is meant by 'cohabitation'? (2)

**(b)** Describe, from one religion, the purpose of marriage. (5)

**(c)** Explain, from either two religions or two religious traditions, attitudes to contraception. (8)

(15)
---
**(a)** One mark for a basic or partial definition; two marks for an accurate definition, or a basic one developed with an example.
- Living together as a couple in a sexual relationship without being married (2).
- "Living together" alone (1). An example such as a couple who share a home and children but have chosen not to marry can lift a basic definition to 2.

**(b)** Three bands (AO1).
- Band 3 (4-5): an excellent, coherent description of the purpose of marriage in one religion, using a range of religious terms and sources of authority accurately; shows how the belief shapes the lives of believers.
- Band 2 (2-3): a good, generally accurate description with some religious terms or a source.
- Band 1 (1): a limited statement, e.g. "marriage is for love".
- 0: nothing relevant.
Indicative content. Christianity: marriage is a lifelong, exclusive covenant made before God; Jesus taught that "the two will become one flesh" and that God made humans male and female (Mark 10:6-8); its purposes are companionship and mutual support, the right setting for sex, and the procreation and raising of children in a stable family; the Church of England's marriage service sets out these purposes and the couple make vows "till death us do part"; for Catholics marriage is a sacrament, a source of grace. Islam: marriage (nikah) is a contract and a religious duty strongly encouraged by the Prophet's example (Sunnah); Allah created spouses so that people might find tranquillity in them and placed love and mercy between them (Qur'an 30:21); it protects against zina, provides a stable home for children and unites families; the mahr (gift to the bride) and the written contract witnessed at the ceremony show that rights and responsibilities are taken seriously. Credit any religion.

**(c)** Four bands (AO1).
- Band 4 (7-8): an excellent, highly detailed explanation of diverse attitudes in two religions or two traditions; specialist terms and sources used extensively and accurately; clear understanding of how belief affects believers.
- Band 3 (5-6): a very good, detailed explanation of both, with accurate terms and sources.
- Band 2 (3-4): a good, generally accurate explanation; one tradition may be much thinner.
- Band 1 (1-2): limited and/or poorly organised; or only one tradition.
- No credit for humanist or other non-religious views.
Indicative content. Catholic Christianity: artificial contraception is wrong because the Church follows Aquinas's Natural Law; the second primary precept is to reproduce, so every sexual act should remain open to new life; Humanae Vitae (1968) restated this; natural family planning is acceptable because it does not block the natural purpose of sex. Protestant/Anglican Christianity: most accept contraception within marriage as responsible parenthood; the Lambeth Conference (1930) first allowed it in some circumstances; a couple can use conscience to plan a family that they can care for; contraception that works after conception may be rejected by some because of the sanctity of life. Islam: sex belongs within marriage only, "do not approach unlawful sexual intercourse" (Qur'an 17:32), so contraception to enable sex outside marriage is haram; within marriage many scholars permit contraception by mutual consent, citing the Prophet's companions practising withdrawal ('azl); permanent methods such as sterilisation are generally discouraged; some Muslims see large families as a blessing and trust Allah to provide. Sunni and Shi'a scholars both generally allow reversible methods.

::: q 4-5 2.1b
**(d)** 'Divorce is always wrong for religious believers.'

Discuss this statement, showing that you have considered more than one point of view. (You must refer to religion and belief in your answer.) (15)

Spelling, punctuation, grammar and the accurate use of specialist terms are assessed in this answer. (6)

(21)
---
Five bands (AO2), 15 marks.
- Band 5 (13-15): an excellent, highly detailed analysis and evaluation, built on detailed knowledge of religion, religious teaching and moral reasoning; judgements are reached and alternative views presented; sources interpreted accurately; clear understanding of how belief affects individuals, communities and society.
- Band 4 (10-12): very good, detailed analysis and evaluation with accurate knowledge; judgements and different viewpoints; terms and sources used in detail.
- Band 3 (7-9): good, generally detailed analysis; reasonable judgements; recognises other views; some terms and sources.
- Band 2 (4-6): limited statements of more than one view; weak judgements; few terms.
- Band 1 (1-3): a basic statement of one view with little or no judgement.
- 0: no relevant point of view.

Indicative content — credit all relevant arguments; a summary conclusion is not required if judgements run through the answer.
Arguments that divorce is wrong:
- Catholic Christianity: marriage is a sacrament and an unbreakable covenant; "what God has joined together, let no one separate" (Mark 10:9), so a valid marriage cannot be dissolved; divorced Catholics who remarry cannot normally receive Holy Communion. An annulment is possible only where a valid marriage never existed.
- Jesus said that anyone who divorces and marries another commits adultery except for sexual immorality (Matthew 19:8-9); Moses permitted divorce only "because your hearts were hard".
- Vows made before God ("till death us do part") are promises to God, not only to a spouse.
- Divorce can harm children and weaken the family, which religions see as the foundation of society.
- Islam: the Prophet is reported to have said that of all permitted things divorce is the most hated by Allah; the Qur'an requires arbitrators from both families to attempt reconciliation first (Qur'an 4:35).
Arguments that divorce is not always wrong:
- Islam permits divorce (talaq) as a last resort; Qur'an 2:229 sets out a process with waiting periods ('iddah) so that reconciliation is possible, and requires a separation to be done "in kindness"; a wife may seek divorce through khul'.
- Most Protestant and Anglican Christians accept divorce when a marriage has irretrievably broken down; since 2002 the Church of England has allowed remarriage in church at the priest's discretion. A loving God offers forgiveness and new beginnings.
- Jesus' exception in Matthew 19:9 shows divorce is not always forbidden; some Christians extend it to abuse or desertion.
- Staying in an abusive or loveless marriage can cause more harm than divorce, especially to children; compassion (agape) may favour divorce.
- Non-religious belief: Humanists see marriage as a human commitment, and if it causes misery it is better ended with care for any children; UK law allows no-fault divorce since 2022.
Evaluation: "always" is the key word; stronger answers judge whether the Catholic position is about divorce or about remarriage, and whether permitting divorce as a last resort (Islam, Protestant Christianity) is consistent with seeing it as wrong.

SPaG (6): high performance 5-6 — spelling and punctuation consistently accurate, grammar under effective control, specialist terms (sacrament, covenant, talaq, annulment, reconciliation) used precisely; intermediate 3-4 — considerable accuracy and general control; threshold 1-2 — reasonable accuracy, errors do not seriously obscure meaning; 0 — nothing written, irrelevant, or errors severely hinder meaning.

**2. Issues of Life and Death**

**(a)** What is meant by 'quality of life'? (2)

**(b)** Describe, from one religion, beliefs about why humans should care for the environment. (5)

**(c)** Explain, from either two religions or two religious traditions, how funerals reflect beliefs about life after death. (8)

(15)
---
**(a)** One mark for a basic definition; two for an accurate definition or a basic one with an example.
- How good or worthwhile a person's life is, judged by their physical and mental well-being, comfort and ability to enjoy life (2).
- "How good your life is" (1). An example, such as the argument that a terminal illness causing great pain lowers quality of life, can lift to 2.

**(b)** Three bands as in 1(b).
Indicative content. Christianity: God made humans in his image and gave them dominion over creation (Genesis 1:28; Psalm 8:6), which most Christians now read as stewardship — looking after the earth on God's behalf; the earth belongs to God ("the earth is the Lord's", Psalm 24:1), so damaging it is a sin against the Creator; humans will be accountable for how they used it; Pope Francis's Laudato Si' (2015) calls care of the earth "our common home" a moral duty and links environmental damage to harm to the poor; churches run eco-schemes such as Eco Church. Islam: humans are khalifah (trustees/vicegerents) of Allah's creation and will be judged on how they cared for it; Allah created the heavens and earth and everything in them is subject to his command (Qur'an 7:54), so creation is a trust (amanah) not a possession; humans have a natural disposition (fitra) to live in harmony with Allah's order; wasting resources is forbidden; the Prophet taught that planting a tree is a charity. Credit any religion.

**(c)** Four bands as in 1(c); two religions or traditions; no credit for humanist funerals in (c).
Indicative content. Christianity: the funeral service in Britain expresses hope in resurrection — the minister often reads Jesus' words "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25); prayers commend the soul to God, reflecting belief that the person will face judgement and the hope of heaven; the coffin may be placed near the Paschal (Easter) candle, a symbol of the risen Christ; burial or cremation are both accepted, the committal speaking of "sure and certain hope of the resurrection"; Catholic funerals include a Requiem Mass and prayers for the dead, reflecting belief in purgatory; Protestant services focus on thanksgiving for the life and trust in God's grace. Islam: the body is washed (ghusl) and wrapped in simple white cloth because all are equal before Allah; burial takes place as soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours, because the soul has returned to Allah and the body awaits resurrection on the Day of Judgement; cremation is forbidden because the body should be returned whole to the earth; the body is buried facing Makkah; the funeral prayer (salat al-janazah) asks Allah's forgiveness for the dead, reflecting belief in judgement and accountability; mourning is restrained because death is the will of Allah and the start of the next life in barzakh, the waiting period before judgement. Shi'a and Sunni practice is similar; some Shi'a visit and pray at graves of family and imams, which some Sunni scholars discourage.

::: q 6-7 2.1d
**(d)** 'Science has shown that God did not create the universe.'

Discuss this statement, showing that you have considered more than one point of view. (You must refer to religious and non-religious beliefs, such as those held by Humanists and Atheists, in your answer.) (15)
---
Five bands as in 1(d), with the Life and Death requirement: Band 5 needs an excellent, highly detailed consideration of non-religious beliefs such as those of Humanists and Atheists; Band 4 very good; Band 3 good; Band 2 limited; Band 1 very basic or none. An answer with no non-religious view cannot reach the top bands.

Indicative content.
Arguments supporting the statement:
- Atheists and Humanists: the Big Bang, about 13.8 billion years ago, explains the origin of the universe from an extremely hot, dense state without any need for a creator; Brian Cox presents the Big Bang and the laws of physics as the best explanation of how the universe developed.
- Evolution by natural selection (Charles Darwin) explains the variety of life without design; Richard Dawkins argues that natural selection makes a designer unnecessary and that belief in God is a hypothesis without evidence.
- A literal reading of Genesis 1 (six days, a young earth) conflicts with geological and cosmic evidence; if scripture is wrong on this it may be wrong about God as creator.
- Humanists base beliefs on reason and evidence; there is no scientific test for God.
Arguments against the statement:
- Science explains how the universe developed, not why there is something rather than nothing; the Big Bang needs a cause. Many Christians see God as the cause who started and sustains the process.
- Most Christians read Genesis 1 and 2 non-literally, as truths about God's purpose and humanity's place; this does not conflict with science. Georges Lemaître, who first proposed the Big Bang theory, was a Catholic priest.
- Islam: Allah is the Creator of everything; the Qur'an asks whether he who created the heavens and the earth is not able to create the like of them (Qur'an 36:81); many Muslims see the Big Bang as consistent with the Qur'an's account of the heavens and earth once being joined and then separated.
- The fine-tuning of the laws of nature suggests design to many believers.
- Some scientists are religious and see no conflict; "science has shown" overstates what science claims, since science cannot prove a negative.
Evaluation: credit judgements on whether science and religion answer different questions; whether a non-literal reading avoids the conflict; whether the burden of proof lies with believers.

**3. Issues of Good and Evil**

**(a)** What is meant by 'free will'? (2)

**(b)** Describe two aims of punishment. (5)

**(c)** Explain, from either two religions or two religious traditions, teachings about forgiveness. (8)

(15)
---
**(a)** One mark for a basic definition; two for an accurate definition or basic plus example.
- The ability to make choices for yourself that are not decided in advance, so that you are responsible for what you choose (2).
- "Choosing what you do" (1).

**(b)** Three bands as in 1(b); the answer should describe two aims, not give a general account of punishment.
Indicative content (any two, developed): retribution — the offender pays for the crime with a punishment that fits it ("eye for eye", Exodus 21:24, read as limiting revenge to proportion), given by lawful authority not by victims; deterrence — the punishment is severe or public enough to put the offender and others off crime; reformation — the punishment helps the offender change, through education, therapy, work or faith, so that they do not reoffend, an aim valued by Christians because people can repent and change; protection — society is kept safe from dangerous offenders, e.g. by imprisonment; justice — the wrong is put right and the victim's rights are respected; Islam teaches that Allah commands justice and good conduct (Qur'an 16:90). Credit religious references where relevant.

**(c)** Four bands as in 1(c).
Indicative content. Christianity: Jesus told Peter to forgive "seventy-seven times" (Matthew 18:21-22), meaning without limit; in the Lord's Prayer Christians ask God to forgive them as they forgive others, and Jesus warned that those who do not forgive will not be forgiven (Matthew 6:14-15); Jesus forgave those who crucified him (Luke 23:34); forgiveness does not cancel justice — a criminal may still be punished; the sacrament of reconciliation (Catholic) shows God's forgiveness; examples such as Gee Walker, who said she forgave the killers of her son Anthony because of her Christian faith. Islam: Allah is al-Ghafur, the Forgiving, and al-Rahim, the Merciful; whatever misfortune befalls people is because of what their own hands have earned, yet Allah pardons much (Qur'an 42:30); believers are urged to pardon, overlook and forgive even within their own families, because Allah is forgiving and merciful (Qur'an 64:14); in Shari'ah, the family of a murder victim may forgive and accept compensation (diyah) instead of qisas; Muslims ask Allah's forgiveness (tawbah, istighfar), especially in Ramadan; forgiving does not mean ignoring justice.

::: q 6-7 2.1h
**(d)** 'A loving God would not allow evil and suffering.'

Discuss this statement, showing that you have considered more than one point of view. (You must refer to religion and belief in your answer.) (15)
---
Five bands as in 1(d).

Indicative content.
Arguments supporting the statement:
- The problem of evil: if God is omnipotent he could stop suffering, and if omnibenevolent he would want to, yet suffering exists — the dilemma set out by Epicurus. Atheists conclude that such a God does not exist.
- Natural evil (earthquakes, disease, children's cancer) is not caused by human choice, so free will cannot explain it.
- The amount of suffering — for example the Holocaust — seems far more than any good purpose could justify.
- Humanists argue that the existence of so much undeserved suffering is evidence that the universe is not run by a loving being.
Arguments against the statement:
- Free will (Augustine): evil entered the world through Original Sin; God gave humans freedom to choose good, which is worth the risk of misuse; a world of robots could not love. Much suffering is caused by human choices.
- Soul-making (Irenaeus, developed by John Hick): the world is a place where humans grow into God's likeness; courage, compassion and forgiveness can only develop where there is real suffering; heaven will make the suffering worthwhile.
- Job: suffering is a test of faith; Job never learns why he suffered but trusts God, and God's ways are beyond human understanding (Job 42:1-6).
- Christians believe God shares suffering through Jesus' crucifixion and will one day end it (Revelation 21:4).
- Islam: life is a test; Allah tests people "with something of fear and hunger and loss" and gives good news to the patient (Qur'an 2:155); Iblis tempts humans but they are free to resist (Qur'an 2:34); al-Qadr means everything happens within Allah's knowledge and will, and the reward of the akhirah outweighs worldly suffering.
Evaluation: credit judgements on whether free will explains natural evil, whether the soul-making defence justifies the amount of suffering, and whether trust in God's wisdom is a reasonable response or an evasion.

**4. Issues of Human Rights**

**(a)** What is meant by 'relative poverty'? (2)

**(b)** Describe an example of a conflict between personal conviction and the laws of a country. (5)

**(c)** Explain, from either two religions or two religious traditions, attitudes to racial prejudice and discrimination. (8)

(15)
---
**(a)** One mark for a basic definition; two for an accurate definition or basic plus example.
- Being poor compared with the usual standard of living in your own society, so that you cannot afford things most people there take for granted (2).
- "Being poorer than others" (1). Contrast with absolute poverty (lacking the basic necessities of life) or a UK example can lift to 2.

**(b)** Three bands as in 1(b). Credit any accurate example; the answer must make clear what the conviction was, what the law required, and what the person did.
Indicative examples: Martin Luther King Jr, a Baptist minister, broke segregation laws in the United States through non-violent civil disobedience (e.g. the Birmingham campaign, 1963) because he believed all people are equal in God's image; he was jailed and wrote that an unjust law is no law at all; Muhammad Ali, a Muslim, refused to be drafted into the US Army in 1967 because of his religious convictions, was stripped of his boxing title and convicted, and his conviction was overturned by the US Supreme Court in 1971; Dietrich Bonhoeffer, a German Lutheran pastor, resisted Nazi laws and was executed in 1945; conscientious objectors in Britain in the World Wars; Rosa Parks refusing to give up her bus seat (1955).

**(c)** Four bands as in 1(c).
Indicative content. Christianity: all humans are made in the image of God (Genesis 1:26-27) and so have equal worth; in Christ "there is neither Jew nor Gentile... for you are all one" (Galatians 3:28); Jesus' parable of the Good Samaritan shows love crossing ethnic boundaries; Martin Luther King taught that people should be judged by the content of their character, not the colour of their skin, and used non-violent protest grounded in agape; churches in Britain support racial justice work (e.g. Racial Justice Sunday); some Christians in the past used scripture to support slavery and apartheid, which the Churches now condemn as sinful. Islam: Allah made people into nations and tribes so that they may know one another, and the most noble is the most righteous (Qur'an 49:13); believers must stand firm for justice even when they dislike a people (Qur'an 5:8); the Prophet's Last Sermon taught that no Arab has superiority over a non-Arab, nor white over black, except by righteousness; the ummah of all ethnicities stands together at Hajj in identical ihram; the Christian/Muslim Forum brings Christians and Muslims in England together to build understanding and challenge prejudice, including Islamophobia.

::: q 8-9 2.1m 2.1k
**(d)** 'It is wrong for religious believers to be rich.'

Discuss this statement, showing that you have considered more than one point of view. (You must refer to religion and belief in your answer.) (15)
---
Five bands as in 1(d).

Indicative content.
Arguments supporting the statement:
- Jesus warned that it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter God's kingdom (Mark 10:25) and told a rich young man to sell everything and give to the poor.
- The rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19-31): the rich man ignored the beggar at his gate and was punished; wealth can make people blind to need.
- The love of money is "a root of all kinds of evil" (1 Timothy 6:10); some Christians, such as monks and nuns, take vows of poverty.
- While anyone lives in absolute poverty, keeping wealth seems incompatible with agape or the ummah; Islamic Relief and Christian Aid show that resources can save lives.
- Islam forbids hoarding wealth and earning through riba (interest); the Qur'an says righteousness includes giving wealth, despite love for it, to relatives, orphans, the needy and travellers (Qur'an 2:177).
Arguments against the statement:
- Wealth is not wrong in itself; what matters is how it is gained and used. The rich man was condemned for ignoring Lazarus, not for being rich.
- Islam teaches that wealth is a gift and a test from Allah; Muslims may earn honestly and be rich as long as they pay zakah (2.5% of savings above the nisab), give sadaqah and avoid haram earnings; the Prophet's wife Khadijah was a successful merchant.
- Wealthy believers can fund hospitals, schools, mosques and charities; John Wesley taught "gain all you can, save all you can, give all you can".
- Some Christians follow the prosperity gospel, seeing wealth as a sign of God's blessing (many other Christians reject this).
- A non-religious view: Humanists judge wealth by its effect on human well-being; it is not wrong to be rich, but there is a moral duty to help reduce poverty.
Evaluation: credit judgements about the difference between being rich and loving money, about how much giving is enough, and about relative versus absolute poverty.
