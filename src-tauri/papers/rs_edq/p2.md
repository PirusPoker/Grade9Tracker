In the real exam these are two separate one-hour papers on different days. Each paper has a Beliefs and Teachings question and a Practices question, each worth 30 marks in four parts: (a) what is meant by a key term (2), (b) describe (5), (c) explain (8) and (d) discuss a statement, considering more than one point of view and reaching judgements (15). Answers stay inside the religion being examined: show diversity within it (e.g. Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox; Sunni and Shi'a) rather than comparing with other religions. Allow about 15 minutes for (a)-(c) and 15 minutes for (d) on each question. The paper ramps from grade 4-5 to grade 8-9.

**Component 2: Study of Christianity**

**1. Beliefs and Teachings**

**(a)** What do Christians mean by 'atonement'? (2)

**(b)** Describe Christian beliefs about the resurrection of Jesus. (5)

**(c)** Explain different Christian beliefs about judgement. (8)

(15)
---
**(a)** One mark for a basic or partial definition; two marks for an accurate definition, or a basic one developed with an example or source.
- The restoring of the relationship between God and humans, broken by sin, through Jesus' death on the cross (2).
- "Making up for sin" or "being at one with God" (1).

**(b)** Three bands (AO1).
- Band 3 (4-5): an excellent, coherent description using a range of specialist terms and sources accurately; shows how the belief influences Christians.
- Band 2 (2-3): a good, generally accurate description with some terms or sources.
- Band 1 (1): a limited statement.
- 0: nothing relevant.
Indicative content: on the third day after the crucifixion the women found the tomb empty and were told "He is not here; he has risen" (Luke 24:1-9); the risen Jesus appeared to Peter, the Twelve, more than five hundred people and Paul (1 Corinthians 15:3-8); Paul taught that if Christ has not been raised Christian faith is useless (1 Corinthians 15:12-14), so the resurrection is the foundation of Christianity; it shows that Jesus is the Son of God, that his sacrifice was accepted and that death has been defeated; it gives Christians hope of their own resurrection; it is celebrated every Sunday and above all at Easter; most Christians believe it was a bodily resurrection, though some liberal Christians understand it as a spiritual experience of the disciples.

**(c)** Four bands (AO1).
- Band 4 (7-8): an excellent, highly detailed explanation of the diversity of Christian belief; specialist terms and sources used extensively and accurately; clear understanding of how belief affects Christians.
- Band 3 (5-6): a very good, detailed explanation of diversity, with accurate terms and sources.
- Band 2 (3-4): a good, generally accurate explanation showing some diversity.
- Band 1 (1-2): limited and/or poorly organised.
Indicative content: Christians believe Jesus will return and judge the living and the dead (Apostles' and Nicene Creeds); the parable of the Sheep and the Goats (Matthew 25:31-46) teaches that people will be judged on how they treated the hungry, the stranger, the sick and prisoners — "whatever you did for one of the least of these... you did for me"; the Rich Man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19-31) shows the consequences of ignoring the poor. Diversity: Catholics believe in a particular judgement immediately after death and a final (general) judgement at the end of time, with purgatory for those who die in God's grace but need cleansing; many Protestants reject purgatory and stress that salvation is by grace through faith in Christ, not earned by good works, so judgement reveals whether a person has accepted Christ; some Christians stress that God's mercy outweighs judgement and some hope that all will eventually be saved (universalism); traditional Christians see judgement as a single future event, while some contemporary Christians see people as judging themselves by how they respond to God's love. Influence: belief in judgement motivates charity and moral living.

::: q 4-5 2.2a
**(d)** 'The existence of evil and suffering shows that God cannot be omnibenevolent.'

Discuss this statement, showing that you have considered more than one point of view. (You must refer to religion and belief in your answer.) (15)

Spelling, punctuation, grammar and the accurate use of specialist terms are assessed in this answer. (6)

(21)
---
Five bands (AO2), 15 marks.
- Band 5 (13-15): an excellent, highly detailed analysis and evaluation built on detailed knowledge of religion, religious teaching and moral reasoning; judgements reached and alternative views presented; specialist terms and sources interpreted accurately; clear understanding of how belief influences believers and society.
- Band 4 (10-12): very good, detailed analysis and evaluation, accurate knowledge, judgements and different viewpoints.
- Band 3 (7-9): good, generally detailed analysis; reasonable judgements; recognises other views.
- Band 2 (4-6): limited statements of more than one view; weak judgements.
- Band 1 (1-3): a basic statement of one view.
- 0: no relevant point of view.

Indicative content.
Arguments supporting the statement:
- Epicurus's dilemma: if God is willing to prevent evil but not able, he is not omnipotent; if able but not willing, he is not good; if both, why is there evil?
- The Bible says God is "compassionate and gracious... abounding in love" (Psalm 86:15) and "so loved the world" (John 3:16), yet innocent people suffer from disease and disasters that a loving God could prevent.
- In Job, God allows Satan to destroy Job's family and health as a test (Job 1:8-12); some readers find this hard to square with perfect love.
- Atheists and Humanists argue that suffering is better explained by a world without God.
Arguments against the statement:
- God's love is shown in giving free will; much suffering results from human choices, and love that forces people is not love.
- God's love is demonstrated in Jesus' suffering on the cross; nothing, not even suffering or death, can separate believers from the love of God (Romans 8:37-39).
- Job is restored and accepts that God's wisdom is beyond human understanding (Job 42:1-6), so suffering does not disprove God's goodness.
- Suffering can develop qualities such as compassion and courage (the soul-making view of Irenaeus and John Hick), and heaven will put right present suffering.
- God's omnipotence is shown in the plagues on Egypt and the parting of the sea (Exodus 7-11, 14:21), acts that rescued his people from suffering; Christians believe God will finally end suffering.
Evaluation: credit judgements on whether free will explains natural suffering, whether omnibenevolence must mean preventing all suffering, and how far faith rather than proof is needed.

SPaG (6): high performance 5-6 — spelling and punctuation consistently accurate, grammar under effective control, specialist terms (omnibenevolent, omnipotent, theodicy, free will, Trinity) used precisely; intermediate 3-4 — considerable accuracy and general control; threshold 1-2 — reasonable accuracy, errors do not seriously obscure meaning; 0 — nothing written, irrelevant, or errors severely hinder meaning.

**2. Practices**

**(a)** What do Christians mean by 'sacraments'? (2)

**(b)** Describe the work of Tearfund. (5)

**(c)** Explain different forms of Christian worship. (8)

(15)
---
**(a)** One mark for a basic definition; two for an accurate definition or a basic one with an example.
- Religious rites or ceremonies that are outward, visible signs of an inward, invisible grace given by God (2), e.g. baptism and the Eucharist.
- "Special Christian ceremonies" (1).

**(b)** Three bands as in 1(b).
Indicative content: Tearfund is a UK-based Christian relief and development charity working through local churches and partners in some of the world's poorest communities; it responds to disasters and conflict with emergency aid (food, clean water, shelter); it supports long-term development, such as clean water and sanitation, farming and savings groups, so communities can lift themselves out of poverty; it works with local churches because they are already part of the community; it campaigns on issues such as climate change and justice for the poor; its work is motivated by Christian belief — loving one's neighbour and caring for the poor as Jesus taught (e.g. Matthew 25:35-40) — and is "Christian beliefs in action".

**(c)** Four bands as in 1(c).
Indicative content: liturgical worship follows a set order of service and set prayers, as in Catholic Mass, Orthodox Divine Liturgy and Anglican services from the Book of Common Prayer or Common Worship; it gives a sense of tradition and unity across the world; informal (non-liturgical) worship has no fixed order, with spontaneous prayer, modern songs and a long sermon, common in Evangelical and Pentecostal churches, where the Holy Spirit is believed to guide worship and gifts such as speaking in tongues may be shown; Quaker (Society of Friends) meetings sit mostly in silence, waiting on God, and anyone may speak if moved by the Spirit, because Quakers believe there is "that of God in everyone" and need no priest; individual (private) worship includes personal prayer, Bible reading and meditation at home; Jesus taught that "where two or three gather in my name, there am I with them" (Matthew 18:20), so communal worship matters; the Lord's Prayer is used in almost all forms, as a set prayer and as a model for informal prayer.

::: q 6-7 2.2g 2.2h
**(d)** 'Pilgrimage is not important for Christians today.'

Discuss this statement, showing that you have considered more than one point of view. (You must refer to religion and belief in your answer.) (15)
---
Five bands as in 1(d) (no SPaG).

Indicative content.
Arguments supporting the statement:
- Pilgrimage is not one of the sacraments and is not required by the Bible; Christians believe God is everywhere, so there is no need to travel to a holy place to meet him.
- Many Protestants are wary of shrines and relics, seeing them as distracting from faith in Christ alone.
- Time and money might be better spent helping the poor, which Jesus clearly commanded (Matthew 25).
- In Britain, where fewer than half the population now describe themselves as Christian (2021 census), few go on pilgrimage; weekly worship and daily prayer are more central.
Arguments against the statement:
- Walsingham, in Norfolk, has drawn pilgrims since the Middle Ages to the shrine of Our Lady, where a replica of the Holy House of Nazareth recalls the incarnation; Catholic and Anglican shrines welcome thousands each year, including a national pilgrimage, and pilgrims pray, take part in processions and seek healing.
- Taizé, an ecumenical monastic community in France, draws large numbers of young people from many denominations each year for simple prayer, chant, silence and Bible study, building reconciliation between Christians.
- Pilgrimage strengthens faith, gives time away from everyday life to reflect, repent and renew commitment, and builds community with other believers.
- It is a living tradition that is growing again in Britain (e.g. walking pilgrimages), showing it still matters.
Evaluation: credit judgements on the difference between Catholic/Anglican and some Protestant views, between "important" and "essential", and on whether Taizé's focus on community makes it more relevant today than shrines.

**Component 3: Study of a World Faith - Islam**

**3. Beliefs and Teachings**

**(a)** What do Muslims mean by 'prophethood' (Risalah)? (2)

**(b)** Describe the five roots of Usul ad-Din in Shi'a Islam. (5)

**(c)** Explain Muslim beliefs about angels. (8)

(15)
---
**(a)** One mark for a basic definition; two for an accurate definition or basic plus example.
- The belief that Allah has chosen human messengers (prophets) to communicate his guidance to humanity (2), e.g. Adam, Ibrahim, Musa, Isa and finally Muhammad.
- "Belief in the prophets" (1).

**(b)** Three bands as in 1(b).
Indicative content: Tawhid — the oneness of Allah, the foundation of all belief; Adalat — Allah's justice, that Allah is always fair and will judge everyone fairly, so humans are responsible for their actions; Nubuwwah — prophethood, belief in the prophets from Adam to Muhammad, the Seal of the Prophets; Imamah — belief in the twelve Imams, descendants of Muhammad through Ali and Fatimah, divinely chosen and protected from sin, who guide the community, the twelfth being in hiding (occultation) and expected to return; Mi'ad (Qiyamah) — belief in the Day of Judgement and the resurrection. Shi'a Muslims see these as the roots (usul) from which practice (the branches, furu' ad-Din) grows; Imamah is the distinctively Shi'a root.

**(c)** Four bands as in 1(c).
Indicative content: angels (malaikah) are created by Allah from light; they have no free will and always obey Allah, unlike humans and jinn; belief in angels is one of the six articles of faith in Sunni Islam, and the Messenger and believers all believe "in Allah and His angels and His books and His messengers" (Qur'an 2:285); angels carry Allah's messages and carry out his commands; Jibril brought the Qur'an down to Muhammad's heart by Allah's permission (Qur'an 2:97-98), starting on the Night of Power, so revelation depends on him; whoever is an enemy to Jibril and Mika'il is an enemy to Allah (Qur'an 2:98); Mika'il is in charge of plants and rain, providing for creation, which shows Allah's mercy; Israfil will blow the trumpet to announce the Day of Resurrection; angels record every person's deeds, so Muslims know they are accountable; the angel of death takes souls. Influence: belief in angels reminds Muslims that Allah is close and that every action is recorded, encouraging good conduct. Credit Sunni and Shi'a perspectives: Shi'a Muslims also believe in angels, although angels are not one of the five roots of Usul ad-Din.

::: q 6-7 2.3e
**(d)** 'Belief in al-Qadr means that humans have no free will.'

Discuss this statement, showing that you have considered more than one point of view. (You must refer to religion and belief in your answer.) (15)
---
Five bands as in 1(d) (no SPaG). This paper is about Islam: credit diversity within Islam (Sunni, Shi'a, different schools of thought), not comparisons with other religions.

Indicative content.
Arguments supporting the statement:
- Al-Qadr (predestination) is one of the six articles of faith: Allah knows everything that will happen, has written it, wills it and creates it; nothing happens outside his will.
- Muslims say "insha'Allah" (if Allah wills) about the future, showing that events depend on Allah not on human choice.
- If Allah has already written what each person will do, it is hard to see how humans could choose otherwise; some early Muslim schools (e.g. the Jabriyya) held that humans have no real choice.
Arguments against the statement:
- Allah's knowing what humans will choose does not force them to choose it; Allah is outside time and sees all at once.
- Humans are responsible and accountable on the Day of Judgement (Akhirah); judgement would be unjust if humans had no free will, and Allah is perfectly just.
- Shi'a Islam stresses Adalat (divine justice): because Allah is just, humans must be genuinely free and responsible; many Shi'a scholars teach that Allah determines the framework while humans choose within it.
- The Qur'an repeatedly commands humans to choose right and warns of consequences, which only makes sense if they can choose; Iblis chose to disobey (Qur'an 2:34).
- Many Sunni scholars hold that Allah creates actions but humans "acquire" them (kasb) by choosing, so responsibility remains.
- Angels have no free will; humans are different, having been given the trust (amanah) of choice.
Evaluation: credit judgements on whether foreknowledge equals causation, whether Sunni and Shi'a emphases differ, and whether the tension is a mystery to be accepted in faith.

::: q 8-9 2.3f 2.3g
**4. Practices**

**(a)** What do Muslims mean by 'ummah'? (2)

**(b)** Describe how Muslims perform salah. (5)

**(c)** Explain the importance of Hajj for Muslims. (8)

(15)
---
**(a)** One mark for a basic definition; two for an accurate definition or basic plus example.
- The worldwide community of all Muslims, united by their shared faith regardless of race, nationality or language (2), e.g. shown when all pilgrims wear identical ihram at Hajj.
- "The Muslim community" (1).

**(b)** Three bands as in 1(b).
Indicative content: salah is performed five times a day at set times (Fajr, Zuhr, Asr, Maghrib, Isha); the call to prayer (adhan) is given from the mosque; Muslims first perform wudu (ritual washing of hands, mouth, nose, face, arms, head, ears and feet); they face the qiblah, the direction of the Ka'bah in Makkah; prayer is in Arabic and made up of set units (rak'ahs) of standing, reciting Surah al-Fatihah, bowing (ruku), prostrating (sujud) and sitting; prayer can be offered at home, at work or in the mosque on a clean surface such as a prayer mat, though praying together in the mosque is encouraged; on Friday men are required to attend Jummah prayer at the mosque with a sermon (khutbah); women may attend or pray at home; Shi'a Muslims often combine prayers into three sessions and many place their forehead on a small tablet of earth (turbah). Sources: prayer restrains from immorality and wrongdoing (Qur'an 29:45); Muslims are to glorify their Lord and be among those who prostrate (Qur'an 15:98-99).

**(c)** Four bands as in 1(c).
Indicative content: Hajj is the fifth pillar of Sunni Islam and one of the Ten Obligatory Acts in Shi'a Islam; every adult Muslim who is physically and financially able must perform it at least once; it takes place in the month of Dhul-Hijjah in and around Makkah; it follows in the footsteps of Ibrahim, Hajar and Isma'il, and the Ka'bah is the House Allah made a place of return and safety, with the standing place of Ibrahim as a place of prayer (Qur'an 2:125); pilgrims wear ihram, showing equality of the ummah before Allah; tawaf around the Ka'bah shows Allah at the centre of life; running between Safa and Marwah recalls Hajar's search for water; standing at Arafat in prayer is the most important rite, a preparation for the Day of Judgement and a time of forgiveness; stoning the pillars at Mina rejects the temptations of Shaytan; the sacrifice at Id-ul-Adha recalls Ibrahim's obedience; a successful Hajj is believed to wipe away sins and pilgrims return with a renewed faith and the title hajji. Issues for British Muslims: high cost, time off work, crowds and heat; some save for years; Shi'a Muslims also make pilgrimage (ziyarah) to the shrines of the Imams, such as Karbala, which are important but do not replace Hajj.

::: q 8-9 2.3h 2.3i
**(d)** 'For Muslims in Britain today, Greater Jihad is more important than Lesser Jihad.'

Discuss this statement, showing that you have considered more than one point of view. (You must refer to religion and belief in your answer.) (15)
---
Five bands as in 1(d) (no SPaG).

Indicative content.
Arguments supporting the statement:
- Jihad means "struggle" or "striving" in the way of Allah; Greater Jihad is the personal struggle to live as a good Muslim — to pray five times a day, fast, resist temptation and be honest — and it applies to every Muslim every day.
- A well-known hadith (whose chain of narration some scholars regard as weak) describes returning from battle to the "greater jihad" of struggling against one's own self.
- In Britain Muslims face particular challenges in keeping a Muslim lifestyle — praying at work or school, finding halal food, fasting through long summer days in Ramadan, avoiding alcohol and gambling culture, responding to Islamophobia with patience — so Greater Jihad is constant.
- Lesser Jihad (military struggle) has strict conditions: it must be declared by a legitimate authority, be in self-defence or to protect the oppressed, never target civilians, and stop when the enemy stops; permission was given to those who were fought against because they were wronged (Qur'an 22:39) and believers must "not transgress" (Qur'an 2:190). These conditions do not apply in Britain, so for British Muslims it is largely irrelevant.
- Terrorist groups who misuse the term distort Islam; emphasising Greater Jihad corrects the misconception.
Arguments against the statement:
- Lesser Jihad is a genuine duty in the Qur'an and Hadith when its conditions are met; defending the oppressed and the right to worship is part of faith.
- Lesser Jihad can include non-violent struggle for justice, such as campaigning and speaking out against oppression, which is relevant in Britain.
- For Shi'a Muslims, jihad is one of the Ten Obligatory Acts, and the commemoration of Husayn's sacrifice at Karbala at Ashura is a powerful example of standing against injustice even at the cost of life.
- The two cannot be separated: Lesser Jihad is only legitimate for those who have first succeeded in Greater Jihad, so both matter.
Evaluation: credit judgements on the meaning of "more important" (more frequent, more relevant, or of higher spiritual value), on the reliability of the hadith, and on how festivals such as Id-ul-Fitr celebrate success in the Greater Jihad of Ramadan.
