**1.1** B. Cohabitation (1).

**1.2** One mark for each of two correct points; only the first two count. For example: to have children (procreation), following God's command to "be fruitful and increase in number" (Genesis 1:28) (1); to give children a stable, secure and loving home (1); to protect children and care for the vulnerable, including elderly relatives (1); to bring children up in the faith, e.g. through baptism, prayer at home or learning the Qur'an (1); to teach children right from wrong (1); to support one another as a unit that is the basis of a stable society (1).

**1.3** Two contrasting beliefs, each: simple explanation of a relevant, accurate contrasting belief (1), detailed (2). The two beliefs must contrast, and the answer must refer to Christianity and one or more other religious traditions; a non-religious view may be added but cannot be the only contrast. If two similar beliefs are given, credit only one.
- The Catholic Church teaches that artificial contraception is wrong (1); every sexual act within marriage should be open to the possibility of new life, as set out in Humanae Vitae (1968), so only natural family planning is accepted (1).
- The Church of England and most other Protestant churches accept contraception (1); since the Lambeth Conference of 1930 Anglicans have accepted it within marriage, and many Christians now see responsible family planning as good stewardship of the family's health and resources (1).
- Many Muslims accept contraception within marriage for good reasons (1), such as protecting the mother's health or spacing births; the Prophet did not forbid the withdrawal method ('azl), so many scholars allow reversible methods agreed by both husband and wife (1).
- Many Muslims oppose permanent methods such as sterilisation, or contraception simply to avoid ever having children (1); children are a gift from Allah, and the Qur'an forbids killing children for fear of poverty (Surah 17:31) (1).
A non-religious view, such as the humanist view that contraception is a responsible personal choice, may be added to develop a point but cannot be the only contrast.

**1.4** Two beliefs, each: simple (1), detailed (2); plus relevant and accurate reference to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching (1).
- Christians believe marriage is a gift from God and a lifelong union of one man and one woman (1); the couple make vows before God to love and be faithful "till death us do part" (1). (Some churches now also bless or conduct same-sex marriages; credit accurate references to this.)
- For Catholics, marriage is a sacrament (1): it gives God's grace to the couple, and so the Catholic Church teaches it cannot be dissolved (1).
- One purpose of marriage is to have and bring up children (1); the Church of England marriage service says marriage is given so that children may be born and brought up in security and stability (1).
- Marriage provides companionship, love and support (1); the couple "become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24), sharing their lives completely (1).
- For Muslims, marriage (nikah) is a contract between a man and a woman, witnessed and with a mahr (dowry) given to the bride (1); it is the right setting for sex and children and brings "love and mercy" between the couple (Surah 30:21) (1).
- Muslims see marriage as a duty and part of following the Prophet's example (sunnah) (1); a well-known hadith describes marriage as half of faith (1).
Sources might include: "That is why a man leaves his father and mother and is united to his wife, and they become one flesh." (Genesis 2:24); "Therefore what God has joined together, let no one separate." (Mark 10:9); 1 Corinthians 13 (love is patient, love is kind); Surah 30:21 (spouses created so that you may find tranquillity in them, with love and mercy between you); Surah 4:1.

Indicative content — credit all relevant arguments.
Arguments in support:
- Men and women are created equal in the image of God: "male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27); "there is neither ... male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28).
- Many Christians and churches now reflect equality in practice: the Church of England has ordained women as priests since 1994 and as bishops since 2015, and many couples share earning, housework and childcare.
- In Islam, men and women are equal before Allah and will be rewarded equally for their faith and deeds (Surah 33:35); Khadijah, the Prophet's first wife, was a successful businesswoman.
- Both parents are commanded to be honoured (Exodus 20:12) and both are responsible for bringing children up in the faith.
- Humanists and many non-religious people argue that roles should depend on the abilities and choices of each couple, not on gender; UK law (the Equality Act 2010) protects against sex discrimination.
- Fixed gender roles can limit people's talents and have been used to justify discrimination against women.
Arguments for a different view:
- Many Christians and Muslims believe men and women are equal in worth but have different, complementary roles given by God.
- Some Christians point to Ephesians 5:22–25: wives should submit to their husbands, and husbands should love their wives as Christ loved the Church — a relationship of mutual care with different responsibilities.
- The Catholic Church teaches equal dignity but values motherhood as a special calling, and ordains only men as priests.
- Many Muslims believe the husband is responsible for providing for and protecting the family, while the mother has the vital role of nurturing children — "paradise lies at the feet of mothers" (a hadith) — though a woman may work and keeps her own earnings.
- Some argue there are natural differences, for example only women can give birth and breastfeed, so some difference in roles is sensible.
- Some families freely choose traditional roles; "same roles" could remove that freedom of choice.
Conclusion: for example, that men and women should have equal value and equal opportunity, but each couple may divide roles as suits them. The conclusion must follow from the arguments.

**2.1** D. Stewardship (1).

**2.2** One mark for each of two correct points; only the first two count. For example: God created the universe (1); God created it out of nothing (ex nihilo) (1); "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth" (Genesis 1:1) (1); some Christians read Genesis literally — creation in six days (1); many Christians read Genesis as a symbolic account and accept that God caused the Big Bang (1); Georges Lemaître, a Catholic priest, first proposed the idea later called the Big Bang theory (1); Muslims believe Allah created the heavens and the earth and simply says "Be" and it is (Surah 2:117) (1); some Muslims see Surah 21:30, which says the heavens and earth were once joined together and then separated, as consistent with the Big Bang (1).

**2.3** Two contrasting beliefs, each: simple (1), detailed (2). The two beliefs must contrast, and the answer must refer to Christianity and one or more other religious traditions; a non-religious view may be added but cannot be the only contrast. If two similar beliefs are given, credit only one.
- Many Christians accept animal experiments for medical research (1); humans were given dominion over animals (Genesis 1:28) and are of greater value, so research that saves human lives can be justified if suffering is kept to a minimum (1).
- Other Christians oppose animal experiments, especially where there are alternatives (1); animals are part of God's creation and humans are stewards, not owners, so causing them suffering is a failure of stewardship (1). (Testing cosmetics on animals has been banned in the UK since 1998, and most Christians agree with this.)
- Many Muslims accept animal experiments only where genuinely necessary, for example to develop medicines (1); humans are khalifah (trustees) of creation and will be judged on how they treated animals, so unnecessary cruelty is forbidden (1).
- Muslim teaching stresses kindness to animals (1); hadith tell of a man forgiven for giving water to a thirsty dog and a woman punished for starving a cat, so many Muslims oppose experiments that cause suffering for trivial reasons (1).
- A non-religious view may be added to develop the contrast, but cannot replace the Christian and other religious views: many humanists accept research that follows the "3Rs" (replace, reduce, refine), while animal rights campaigners oppose all experiments.

**2.4** Two beliefs, each: simple (1), detailed (2); plus relevant and accurate reference to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching (1).
- Christians believe human life is sacred because it is created by God in his image (1); every person has special value, so life should be protected from conception to natural death (1).
- Only God has the right to give and take life (1); so Christians oppose murder and many oppose abortion and euthanasia, following "You shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13) (1).
- God knows and plans each life before birth (1): "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you" (Jeremiah 1:5), so many Christians believe the unborn child has value (1).
- Muslims believe life is a gift from Allah and belongs to him (1); only Allah decides when a person dies, so Muslims oppose suicide and euthanasia (1).
- The Qur'an teaches that taking one innocent life is like killing all of humanity, and saving one life is like saving all of humanity (Surah 5:32) (1); so Muslims have a duty to preserve life (1).
Sources might include: "So God created mankind in his own image" (Genesis 1:27); "For you created my inmost being; you knit me together in my mother's womb." (Psalm 139:13); "your bodies are temples of the Holy Spirit" (1 Corinthians 6:19); Surah 5:32; Surah 17:33 (do not take life, which God has made sacred, except by right); Surah 6:151.

Indicative content — credit all relevant arguments.
Arguments in support:
- Autonomy: a person's life belongs to them, so a mentally competent adult should be able to choose to end unbearable suffering; Humanists UK campaigns for assisted dying for terminally ill or incurably suffering adults, with safeguards.
- Quality of life: when illness leaves someone in constant pain or without dignity, keeping them alive may not be the most loving action.
- Some Christians support voluntary euthanasia: they argue that compassion (agape) and "love your neighbour as yourself" (Mark 12:31) mean ending suffering; Lord Carey, a former Archbishop of Canterbury, has said he supports assisted dying.
- Some countries allow assisted dying under strict rules (for example the Netherlands, Belgium, Canada, and assisted suicide in Switzerland), suggesting safeguards can work.
- Many Christians and Muslims already accept that a dying patient may refuse burdensome treatment and be allowed to die naturally (passive euthanasia is distinguished from killing), and that pain relief may be given even if it shortens life.
Arguments for a different view:
- Sanctity of life: life is a gift from God and only God should decide when it ends: "The Lord gave and the Lord has taken away" (Job 1:21); "You shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13).
- The Catholic Church and most other churches oppose euthanasia and assisted suicide; they support the hospice movement, founded in its modern form by the Christian doctor Dame Cicely Saunders (St Christopher's Hospice, 1967), which gives dignity and pain relief to the dying.
- Muslims believe life belongs to Allah, who has fixed the time of each death; suffering may be a test, and the Qur'an commands "do not kill yourselves" (Surah 4:29).
- Belief in life after death means this life is not everything: how a person faces suffering matters to God and may bring spiritual growth.
- Non-religious arguments against: vulnerable people might feel pressured so as not to be a burden; diagnoses can be wrong; the "slippery slope" could lead to non-voluntary euthanasia; doctors' duty is to preserve life.
Conclusion: for example, that respect for life and fear of abuse outweigh individual choice, or that with strict safeguards compassion should allow choice. The conclusion must follow from the arguments.

**3.1** D. Retaliation (1). The just war conditions include just cause, legitimate authority, right intention, last resort, reasonable chance of success and proportionality.

**3.2** One mark for each of two correct points; only the first two count. For example: giving money to religious charities such as Christian Aid, CAFOD or Islamic Relief (1); providing emergency food, water, shelter and medical care in refugee camps (1); helping refugees to settle, for example churches and mosques welcoming refugee families (1); rebuilding homes, schools and hospitals after the fighting (1); offering counselling and spiritual support to people traumatised by war (1); praying for the victims and for peace (1); campaigning for the protection of civilians and refugees (1).

**3.3** Two contrasting beliefs, each: simple explanation of a relevant, accurate contrasting belief (1), detailed (2). The two beliefs must contrast, and the answer must refer to Christianity and one or more other religious traditions; a non-religious view may be added but cannot be the only contrast. If two similar beliefs are given, credit only one.
- Most Christians believe using weapons of mass destruction is always wrong (1); they kill huge numbers of innocent civilians and damage the environment for generations, so they can never meet the just war conditions of proportionality and protecting non-combatants (1).
- Some Christians accept holding nuclear weapons as a deterrent (1); they argue deterrence has helped prevent war between nuclear powers, so possessing them may be the lesser evil, as long as they are never used (1).
- Pope Francis and many other Christian leaders reject even the possession of nuclear weapons (1); in 2019, at Hiroshima, Pope Francis said that possessing atomic weapons, as well as using them, is immoral (1).
- Most Muslims believe using weapons of mass destruction is forbidden (haram) (1); the rules of lesser jihad forbid harming women, children, the elderly and crops, and weapons that cannot tell soldiers from civilians break those rules (1).
- Some Muslims argue that possessing such weapons as a deterrent may be allowed (1), to defend a Muslim country against attack, since the Qur'an permits self-defence (Surah 22:39) — but never their first use against civilians (1).

**3.4** Two beliefs, each: simple (1), detailed (2); plus relevant and accurate reference to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching (1).
- Christians believe Jesus taught his followers to be peacemakers (1); peace is more than the absence of war — it is harmony with God and others, which is why Christians work for reconciliation (1).
- Jesus is called the "Prince of Peace" (Isaiah 9:6) (1); so Christians believe peace is God's will for the world and try to follow Jesus' example of non-violence (1).
- Peace depends on justice (1); many religious believers work to remove the causes of conflict, such as poverty and inequality, because injustice leads to violence (1).
- Muslims believe Islam is a religion of peace (1); the word Islam is linked to salam (peace), and Muslims greet one another with "as-salamu alaykum" (peace be upon you) (1).
- One of the 99 names of Allah is As-Salam, the Source of Peace (Surah 59:23) (1); so Muslims believe they should seek peace, and the Qur'an teaches that if the enemy inclines towards peace, Muslims should too (Surah 8:61) (1).
Sources might include: "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called children of God." (Matthew 5:9); "Peace I leave with you; my peace I give you." (John 14:27); "Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44); Surah 8:61; Surah 59:23.

Indicative content — credit all relevant arguments.
Arguments in support:
- Some groups use religious language and claims to justify violence: for example the so-called Islamic State group presented its violence as religious, and some past conflicts, such as the Crusades, were fought in the name of religion.
- Religion can create strong "us and them" identities; when religious groups also differ in politics or land, disagreements can harden into violence.
- Holy war is a concept in both Christian and Muslim history, so religion has at times been used to justify war.
- Many atheists and some humanists argue that believing you have absolute truth from God makes compromise harder and can make conflict more likely.
Arguments for a different view:
- Most wars have non-religious causes: greed (for land, oil or other resources), self-defence, retaliation and political power, which the specification names as reasons for war.
- Where religion appears involved, other causes are usually at work: the conflict in Northern Ireland, for example, was mainly about national identity, politics and civil rights, even though the communities were labelled Catholic and Protestant.
- The vast majority of Christians and Muslims condemn terrorism; Muslim scholars stress that killing innocent people breaks the conditions of lesser jihad (Surah 5:32 teaches that killing one innocent person is like killing all humanity).
- Religions teach peace and many believers are peacemakers: Archbishop Desmond Tutu chaired South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission; in Nigeria, Pastor James Wuye and Imam Muhammad Ashafa, once on opposite sides of violence, founded the Interfaith Mediation Centre to reconcile Christians and Muslims.
- Jesus taught "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9) and "all who draw the sword will die by the sword" (Matthew 26:52); Quakers and many other Christians are pacifists.
- Religious organisations such as Christian Aid and Islamic Relief help the victims of war, showing religion as a response to war rather than its cause.
- Wars with no religious motive, for example conflicts fought by openly atheist regimes, show that war does not need religion.
Conclusion: for example, that religion is sometimes used to justify war but is rarely its main cause, and is more often a force for peace. The conclusion must follow from the arguments.

- Most Christians in Britain oppose the death penalty (1); life is sacred and only God should take it, and the Catholic Church teaches (Catechism, revised 2018) that the death penalty is "inadmissible" because it attacks the dignity of the person and removes the chance of reform (1).

- Many Muslims accept the death penalty for certain crimes such as murder (1); the Qur'an allows life to be taken "by right" (Surah 17:33) and sets out retribution (qisas) for murder, so it can be just punishment under Shari'ah law (1).
- Islam also allows the victim's family to forgive the murderer (1); the family may accept compensation (diyah) instead of execution (Surah 2:178), so many Muslims believe mercy is better even where the death penalty is permitted (1).
- Some Muslims in Britain oppose the death penalty in practice (1), because they doubt that any justice system is fair enough to use it without mistakes (1).

**4.4** Two beliefs, each: simple (1), detailed (2); plus relevant and accurate reference to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching (1).
- Christians believe they must forgive others because God forgives them (1); in the Lord's Prayer they ask God to "forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive those who trespass against us" (1).

- Jesus forgave the people who crucified him (1): "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34), and some Christians follow this example — Gee Walker publicly forgave the men who murdered her son Anthony in a racist attack in 2005 (1).
- Muslims believe Allah is merciful and forgiving and they should be too (1); the Qur'an says that although a wrong may be repaid equally, whoever forgives and makes peace will be rewarded by Allah (Surah 42:40) (1).
- Muslims are encouraged to pardon others in the hope of being pardoned by Allah (1) (Surah 24:22), and in cases of murder the victim's family may choose to forgive (Surah 2:178) (1).
Sources might include: the Lord's Prayer; Matthew 18:21–35 (the parable of the unforgiving servant); Luke 23:34; Surah 42:40; Surah 24:22; Surah 3:134 (those who restrain their anger and pardon people).

Arguments in support:
- Christians believe everyone can change with God's grace: Jesus said, "I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance" (Luke 5:32), and the parable of the lost son shows a father welcoming back the one who went wrong.
- Every person is made in the image of God and has value, so punishment should help offenders become better people rather than simply make them suffer; Christians work as prison chaplains and visitors ("I was in prison and you came to visit me", Matthew 25:36), following reformers such as the Quaker Elizabeth Fry.
