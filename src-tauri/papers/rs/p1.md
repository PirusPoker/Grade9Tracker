**1.1** B. Omnipotent (1).

**1.2** One mark for each of two correct points; only the first two answers count. For example: God created the world out of nothing (ex nihilo) (1); God created the world by his word — "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth" (Genesis 1:1) (1); all three persons of the Trinity were involved — the Spirit of God was "hovering over the waters" (Genesis 1:2) and the Word (the Son) was there in the beginning, "through him all things were made" (John 1:3) (1); creation is good — God saw that it was good (1); humans were made in the image of God (Genesis 1:27) (1); some Christians read Genesis literally (six days), others see it as a symbolic account of why the world exists, compatible with the Big Bang (1).

**1.3** Two ways, each: simple explanation of a relevant influence (1), detailed explanation showing the influence (2). To be "detailed", the influence on the believer must be clear.
- Christians worship God as Father, Son and Holy Spirit (1); so their prayers and creeds, such as the Nicene Creed, are addressed to all three, and many prayers end "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit" (1).
- Christians are baptised in the name of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit (Matthew 28:19) (1); so the Trinity marks the moment a person joins the Church and shapes the promises made at baptism (1).
- The Trinity is a relationship of love between three persons (1); so it inspires Christians to live in loving community with others and to value family and church life (1).
- Christians believe the Holy Spirit lives in believers (1); so they pray for the Spirit's guidance when making decisions and believe they are given strength to live a Christian life (1).
- Belief in God the Son means God became human in Jesus (1); so Christians follow Jesus' example and teaching, such as loving their neighbour (1).

**1.4** Two beliefs, each: simple (1), detailed (2); plus relevant and accurate reference to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching (1).
- Jesus' death was a sacrifice for the sins of humanity (1); it is the atonement that repairs the relationship between God and humans broken by sin, so that people can be forgiven (1).
- The crucifixion shows God's love (1); Christians believe God gave his Son willingly — "For God so loved the world that he gave his one and only Son" (John 3:16) (1).
- Jesus was fully human and truly suffered (1); on the cross he cried out, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34), so Christians believe God understands human suffering (1).
- Jesus forgave even as he died (1): "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34), an example Christians try to follow (1).
- The crucifixion was not the end: it was followed by the resurrection, which shows that Jesus' sacrifice was accepted and that death is defeated (1).
Sources might include: "But God demonstrates his own love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us." (Romans 5:8); "For the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord." (Romans 6:23); the Apostles' Creed ("suffered under Pontius Pilate, was crucified, died, and was buried"); the Gospel accounts of the crucifixion (e.g. Mark 15, Luke 23).

**1.5** Levels of response (AO2).
Level 4 (10–12): a well-argued response with reasoned consideration of different points of view; logical chains of reasoning leading to a judgement, supported by knowledge and understanding of relevant evidence; references to religion applied to the issue.
Level 3 (7–9): reasoned consideration of different points of view; logical chains of reasoning drawing on relevant evidence; clear reference to religion.
Level 2 (4–6): reasoned consideration of one point of view, with a logical chain of reasoning; OR recognition of different points of view, each supported by relevant reasons. Maximum Level 2 if there is no reference to religion.
Level 1 (1–3): a point of view with reason(s) stated in support.
0: nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content — credit all relevant arguments.
Arguments in support:
- God is omnibenevolent: "God is love" (1 John 4:8); a perfectly loving parent would never want their child to suffer for ever.
- God does not want anyone to be lost; he is "not wanting anyone to perish, but everyone to come to repentance" (2 Peter 3:9). Some Christians (universalists) believe that in the end all people will be reconciled to God.
- Eternal punishment for sins committed in a limited lifetime seems out of proportion, which does not fit a God who is both loving and just.
- Jesus died for the sins of the whole world (John 3:16), so salvation is offered to all; the parable of the lost son (Luke 15) shows a father who welcomes back the child who went astray.
- Many modern Christians see hell not as a place of fire but as a state of separation from God, and some Christians hope that it may in the end be empty.
Arguments for a different view:
- The Bible teaches that hell exists: in the parable of the sheep and the goats those who ignored the needy "will go away to eternal punishment, but the righteous to eternal life" (Matthew 25:46); see also the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31).
- God is also just: a judge who ignored evil such as murder or cruelty would not be good, so justice requires consequences.
- God does not "send" people to hell: because God gives humans free will, people choose to reject him. The Catechism of the Catholic Church describes hell as a state of "definitive self-exclusion" from communion with God (CCC 1033); C. S. Lewis argued that those in hell have, in effect, chosen it.
- God's love is shown in offering salvation through Jesus; those who refuse the offer separate themselves.
- Catholics believe in purgatory, a state of cleansing for those who die in God's friendship but are not yet ready for heaven — so God's love and justice are both shown.
- Non-religious view (optional): atheists and humanists see no evidence for hell and may argue the idea has been used to frighten people into obedience.
Conclusion: for example, that a loving God does not "send" anyone to hell but allows people to choose separation from him — so the statement is partly true — or that love and justice together make judgement necessary. The conclusion must follow from the arguments.

**2.1** A. Evangelism (1).

**2.2** One mark for each of two correct points; only the first two count. For example: to grow closer to God (1); to visit a place linked to Jesus, a saint or a vision, such as the Holy Land or Lourdes (1); to pray for healing, as many do at Lourdes (1); to ask forgiveness or show penance for sin (1); to give thanks to God (1); to reflect quietly away from daily life, for example on retreat at Iona (1); to strengthen their faith by worshipping with other Christians (1); to have time for reflection and spiritual renewal (1).

**2.3** Two contrasting beliefs, each: simple explanation of a relevant, accurate contrast (1), detailed (2). If two similar beliefs are given, credit only one.
- Catholics (and Orthodox Christians) believe that Christ is truly present in the bread and wine (1); in the Mass the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ (transubstantiation, for Catholics), so the Eucharist is a sacrament in which believers receive Christ and share in his sacrifice (1).
- Many Protestants, such as Baptists, believe the bread and wine are symbols (1); the service is a memorial of the Last Supper, following Jesus' words "do this in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19), so they remember his death rather than believing the elements change (1).
- Anglicans hold a range of views (1); many believe Christ is really present in a spiritual way without defining how, while others hold a more memorial view (1).
- Quakers and the Salvation Army do not celebrate Holy Communion as an outward ritual (1); they believe every meal and every part of life can be a meeting with God, so no special ceremony is needed (1).

**2.4** Two ways, each: simple (1), detailed (2); plus relevant and accurate reference to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching (1).
- Food banks give emergency food parcels to people who cannot afford to eat (1); many are run by churches in the Trussell Trust network, founded by Christians, and people are usually referred with a voucher from a professional such as a doctor or social worker (1).
- They offer support beyond food (1); volunteers offer a welcome, a hot drink and a listening ear, and signpost people to help with debt, benefits or housing so that the causes of their crisis can be tackled (1).
- Church members donate food and time (1); collecting points in churches and supermarkets let the whole community share with those in need, showing Christian love in action (1).
- Food banks treat people with dignity, without judging (1); Christians believe every person is made in the image of God, so the poor deserve respect as well as help (1).
Sources might include: "For I was hungry and you gave me something to eat, I was thirsty and you gave me something to drink" (the parable of the sheep and the goats, Matthew 25:35); "Love your neighbour as yourself" (Mark 12:31); the parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37); James 2:14–17 (faith without action is dead); Jesus feeding the five thousand.

**2.5** Levels of response (AO2).
Level 4 (10–12): a well-argued response with reasoned consideration of different points of view; logical chains of reasoning leading to a judgement, supported by relevant knowledge; references to religion applied to the issue.
Level 3 (7–9): reasoned consideration of different points of view; logical chains of reasoning drawing on relevant evidence; clear reference to religion.
Level 2 (4–6): reasoned consideration of one point of view with a logical chain of reasoning; OR recognition of different points of view, each supported by relevant reasons. Maximum Level 2 if there is no reference to religion.
Level 1 (1–3): a point of view with reason(s) stated in support.
0: nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content — credit all relevant arguments.
Arguments in support:
- Jesus' last command, the Great Commission, was to go and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit (Matthew 28:19–20).
- Christians believe salvation comes through Jesus (John 14:6), so sharing the gospel is the most loving thing they can do; eternal life matters more than any earthly need.
- Without evangelism the Church would shrink and die; church attendance in Britain has fallen, so mission at home matters. Courses such as Alpha (begun at Holy Trinity Brompton, London) introduce people to the faith.
- Mission includes supporting the persecuted Church: organisations such as Open Doors help Christians who suffer for their faith, so that the faith survives worldwide.
- Many Christians argue that practical help is itself a way of spreading the faith: people see Christian love in action.
Arguments for a different view:
- Jesus taught that loving God and loving your neighbour are the greatest commandments (Mark 12:29–31); helping people in need is at least as important as preaching.
- The parable of the sheep and the goats (Matthew 25:31–46) judges people on how they treated the hungry, the stranger and the prisoner, not on how many they converted.
- The Church's work for justice — Christian Aid tackling the causes of poverty, food banks, Street Pastors caring for people at night — shows faith through action: "faith by itself, if it is not accompanied by action, is dead" (James 2:17).
- Reconciliation is central: Christians are called to restore broken relationships, for example the Community of the Cross of Nails at Coventry Cathedral, which works for peace and reconciliation.
- Worship is the Church's first task: some Christians argue that glorifying God in prayer and the sacraments comes before everything else.
- Pressing faith on others can seem disrespectful in a diverse society; many Christians prefer dialogue and example to direct preaching.
Conclusion: for example, that spreading the faith and serving others cannot be separated, because Christians spread the faith by showing Christ's love in action. The conclusion must follow from the arguments.

**3. Islam: Beliefs**

**3.1** Which one of the following is the Muslim belief in the oneness of God? (1)

**3.2** Give two Muslim beliefs about angels. (2)

**3.3** Explain two ways in which belief in al-Qadr (predestination) influences Muslims today. (4)

**3.4** Explain two Muslim beliefs about the Qur'an.
Refer to sacred writings or another source of Muslim belief and teaching in your answer. (5)

**3.5** "Belief in the Day of Judgement is the most important belief in Islam."

**3.1** D. Tawhid (1).

**3.2** One mark for each of two correct points; only the first two count. For example: angels were created by Allah from light (1); they have no free will and always obey Allah (1); they are messengers between Allah and humans (1); Jibril (Gabriel) brought the Qur'an to the Prophet Muhammad (1); Mika'il (Michael) provides nourishment and rain (1); Izra'il, the angel of death, takes souls at death (1); two recording angels note each person's good and bad deeds for the Day of Judgement (1); Israfil will blow the trumpet to announce the Day of Judgement (1); belief in angels is one of the six articles of faith in Sunni Islam (1).

**3.3** Two ways, each: simple (1), detailed (2), with the influence clear.
- Muslims believe Allah knows and has written everything that will happen (1); so they accept hardship with patience, trusting that it is part of Allah's plan and a test, and say "insha'Allah" (if Allah wills) about the future (1).
- Muslims also believe humans have free will and will be judged on their choices (1); so belief in al-Qadr does not make them careless — they still try to act rightly because they are responsible for what they do (1).
- Belief in al-Qadr brings comfort (1); knowing that nothing happens outside Allah's will helps Muslims cope with illness or bereavement without despair (1).
- It encourages humility and gratitude (1); success is seen as a gift from Allah, so Muslims thank Allah rather than boasting (1).
- It is one of the six articles of faith for Sunni Muslims (1); so it shapes daily prayer and submission to Allah's will; many Shi'a Muslims stress Allah's justice (Adalat), believing Allah knows everything but does not force people's choices (1).

**3.4** Two beliefs, each: simple (1), detailed (2); plus relevant and accurate reference to sacred writings or another source of Muslim belief and teaching (1).
- The Qur'an is the word of Allah (1); it was revealed to the Prophet Muhammad by the angel Jibril over about 23 years, beginning in 610 CE on the Night of Power in the cave on Mount Hira (1).
- The Qur'an is the final and complete revelation (1); it has not been changed since it was revealed, unlike earlier books, so it is the highest authority for Muslims (1).
- It was revealed in Arabic (1); so Muslims learn to recite it in Arabic in prayer, and translations are seen as interpretations of its meaning (1).
- It is treated with great respect (1); many Muslims perform wudu before touching it, keep it on a high shelf and memorise it — someone who knows it by heart is a hafiz (1).
- Muslims believe earlier holy books came from Allah (1): the Tawrat to Musa, the Zabur to Dawud, the Injil to Isa and the scrolls to Ibrahim — but these have been lost or changed, so the Qur'an confirms and completes them (1).
Sources might include: the first revelation, "Read! In the name of your Lord who created" (Surah 96:1–5); "We have sent down the Qur'an Ourself, and We Ourself will guard it" (Surah 15:9, paraphrase acceptable); Muhammad as the Seal of the Prophets (Surah 33:40); the Qur'an's description of the Night of Power (Surah 97).

**3.5** Levels of response (AO2).
Level 4 (10–12): a well-argued response with reasoned consideration of different points of view; logical chains of reasoning leading to a judgement, supported by relevant knowledge; references to religion applied to the issue.
Level 3 (7–9): reasoned consideration of different points of view; logical chains of reasoning drawing on relevant evidence; clear reference to religion.
Level 2 (4–6): reasoned consideration of one point of view with a logical chain of reasoning; OR recognition of different points of view, each supported by relevant reasons. Maximum Level 2 if there is no reference to religion.
Level 1 (1–3): a point of view with reason(s) stated in support.
0: nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content — credit all relevant arguments.
Arguments in support:
- Akhirah (life after death) and the Day of Judgement are among the six articles of faith (Sunni) and the five roots of Usul ad-Din (Shi'a, as Mi'ad/Qiyamah); the Qur'an returns to judgement again and again.
- It gives meaning to life: this life is a test, and "Every soul will taste death" (Surah 3:185); Allah "created death and life to test you" (Surah 67:2, paraphrase).
- It motivates every action: whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it (Surah 99:7–8); the recording angels note every deed.
- It explains Allah's justice: wrongs not put right in this life will be put right on the Day of Judgement, which brings comfort to those who suffer.
- It encourages Muslims to keep the Five Pillars, give charity and avoid sin, because they will be rewarded in paradise (Jannah) or punished in hell (Jahannam).
Arguments for a different view:
- Tawhid, the oneness of Allah, is the foundation of all Muslim belief; it is the first root of Usul ad-Din and the first part of the Shahadah. Without belief in Allah there would be no Judgement; shirk (associating partners with Allah) is the one sin Allah will not forgive if a person dies unrepentant (Surah 4:48).
- Surah 112 sums up faith in Allah as One and unique, not in the Judgement.
- Risalah (prophethood) matters most: without the prophets, and above all Muhammad and the Qur'an, humans would not know how Allah wants them to live or what the Judgement will be based on.
- For Shi'a Muslims, Adalat (Allah's justice) and Imamah (the leadership of the Imams) are also roots of faith; the Judgement depends on Allah being just.
- The articles of faith depend on each other, so it may be wrong to rank one above the rest; Surah 4:136 calls believers to believe in Allah, his messengers, his books, his angels and the Last Day together.
- Some argue that fear of Judgement should not be the main motive: Muslims should obey out of love of and gratitude to Allah.
Conclusion: for example, that the Day of Judgement is essential but depends on Tawhid, which is the most important belief. The conclusion must follow from the arguments.

**4.5** "Hajj is the most important of the Five Pillars."

**4.1** D. Shahadah (1). The Ten Obligatory Acts are Salah, Sawm, Zakah, Khums, Hajj, Jihad, Amr-bil-Maruf, Nahi Anil Munkar, Tawalla and Tabarra.

**4.2** One mark for each of two correct points; only the first two count. For example: attending special Id prayers at the mosque or in the open air (1); sacrificing an animal such as a sheep or goat (qurbani), as Ibrahim sacrificed a ram in place of his son (1); sharing the meat between the family, friends and the poor (1); giving money to charity so that the poor can celebrate too, for example paying for a sacrifice abroad (1); wearing new or best clothes (1); visiting family and friends and exchanging gifts or cards (1); sharing a festive meal (1); remembering Ibrahim's obedience to Allah (1).

**4.3** Two contrasting ways, each: simple explanation of a relevant, accurate contrast (1), detailed (2). If two similar ways are given, credit only one.
- Many Sunni Muslims mark Ashura (the 10th of Muharram) by fasting (1); they follow the example of the Prophet Muhammad, who fasted on this day to remember how Allah saved Musa and the Israelites from Pharaoh; some also remember Nuh leaving the ark (1).
- Shi'a Muslims mark Ashura as a day of mourning (1); it remembers the martyrdom of Husayn, the Prophet's grandson, at the Battle of Karbala in 680 CE, a symbol of standing up against injustice (1).
- Shi'a Muslims hold public processions and gatherings (majlis) (1); they recite poems and sermons about Husayn's suffering, and some re-enact the events of Karbala in passion plays (1).
- Many Shi'a Muslims make a pilgrimage to Husayn's shrine at Karbala in Iraq (1); some also give blood on the day, a practice encouraged by many scholars in place of the self-flagellation a few mourners perform (1).
- For many Sunni Muslims Ashura is a quieter, personal day of voluntary fasting (1), whereas for Shi'a Muslims it is one of the most important days of the year, marked in public by the whole community (1).

**4.4** Two meanings, each: simple (1), detailed (2); plus relevant and accurate reference to sacred writings or another source of Muslim belief and teaching (1).
- Greater jihad is the inner, personal struggle to live as a good Muslim (1); for example resisting temptation, keeping the Five Pillars, studying the Qur'an and controlling anger or selfishness (1).
- Lesser jihad is the outward struggle to defend Islam, which may include military action (1); it is only allowed under strict conditions — it must be in self-defence or to protect the oppressed, declared by a proper religious and political authority, and a last resort (1).
- Lesser jihad has strict rules of conduct (1): innocent civilians such as women, children and the elderly must not be harmed, crops and trees must not be destroyed, and fighting must stop when the enemy seeks peace (1).
- Many Muslims see the greater jihad as more important (1); a hadith (reported in some collections, though many scholars consider its chain weak) records the Prophet saying on returning from battle that Muslims had come back from the lesser jihad to the greater jihad, the struggle against the self (1).
- Terrorism is not jihad (1); the vast majority of Muslim scholars condemn attacks on civilians as a breach of the conditions of lesser jihad (1).
Sources might include: "Fight in God's cause against those who fight you, but do not overstep the limits" (Surah 2:190, paraphrase acceptable); if the enemy inclines towards peace, Muslims must incline towards it too (Surah 8:61); Allah will guide those who strive hard for his cause (Surah 29:69); permission to fight is given to those who have been wronged (Surah 22:39); jihad is one of the Ten Obligatory Acts of Shi'a Islam.

Level 4 (10–12): a well-argued response with reasoned consideration of different points of view; logical chains of reasoning leading to a judgement, supported by relevant knowledge; references to religion applied to the issue.
Level 3 (7–9): reasoned consideration of different points of view; logical chains of reasoning drawing on relevant evidence; clear reference to religion.
Level 2 (4–6): reasoned consideration of one point of view with a logical chain of reasoning; OR recognition of different points of view, each supported by relevant reasons. Maximum Level 2 if there is no reference to religion.
Level 1 (1–3): a point of view with reason(s) stated in support.
0: nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content — credit all relevant arguments.
Arguments in support:
- Hajj is a duty for every adult Muslim who is physically and financially able, at least once in a lifetime (Surah 3:97); it is one of the Five Pillars and one of the Ten Obligatory Acts.
- It is often the high point of a Muslim's spiritual life: pilgrims follow in the footsteps of Ibrahim, Hajar, Isma'il and the Prophet Muhammad, performing tawaf around the Ka'aba and sa'y between Safa and Marwah.
- Standing before Allah on the plain of Arafat is a time of repentance; many Muslims believe that sins are forgiven for a pilgrim who completes Hajj sincerely, so they return spiritually renewed.
- Hajj shows the unity and equality of the ummah: around two million Muslims of every race, class and nation wear the same simple ihram clothing and worship together.
- It demands the greatest sacrifice of time, money and effort, and brings together other pillars — prayer, self-discipline and, through the sacrifice at Id-ul-Adha, generosity.
Arguments for a different view:
- The Shahadah is the foundation of the other pillars: it declares Tawhid and the prophethood of Muhammad, and without it the other pillars would have no meaning. (Shi'a Muslims do not list it among the Ten Obligatory Acts but see belief in it as the basis of everything.)
- Salah is performed five times a day, so it shapes a Muslim's whole life in a way that a once-in-a-lifetime journey cannot; the Qur'an says prayer restrains people from wrongdoing (Surah 29:45), and a well-known hadith says prayer will be the first act a person is asked about on the Day of Judgement.
- Hajj is only required of those who are able, so many Muslims — the poor, the ill — never go, yet they are still good Muslims; a pillar not everyone can perform may not be the most important.
- Zakah and Sawm build the ummah every year: Zakah (2.5 per cent of savings above the nisab) purifies wealth and helps the poor, and Sawm builds self-control and empathy with the hungry.
- The pillars depend on each other like the supports of a building, so ranking them may miss the point: each expresses submission to Allah in a different way.
Conclusion: for example, that Hajj may be the most intense act of worship, but the Shahadah (the basis of all the pillars) or Salah (the most constant) is more important. The conclusion must follow from the arguments.
