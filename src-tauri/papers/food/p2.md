**Section B.** **2.** Maya is 15 and has recently become vegetarian. Her family eats a traditional cottage pie made with:

- 500 g minced beef
- 1 onion and 2 carrots
- 1 beef stock cube
- 1 tablespoon plain flour
- mashed potato topping made with butter and whole milk
- 50 g grated Cheddar cheese

**2.1** Give two reasons why a teenager might choose to follow a vegetarian diet. (2)

**2.2** Explain what is meant by protein complementation. Use a named meal as an example. (3)

**2.3** Explain why iron is especially important for Maya, and give two good vegetarian sources of iron. (4)

**2.4** Maya's brother wants to try a vegan diet. Explain how the cottage pie could be changed to make it suitable for a vegan. (4)

**2.5** Vitamin B12 is found naturally only in foods from animals. Explain why this is a problem for vegans and how they can get enough. (3)

(16)
---
**2.1** Any two (1 each): concern for animal welfare; belief that killing animals is wrong; religion, for example Hinduism or Buddhism; health reasons, such as lower saturated fat; environmental reasons (livestock produce greenhouse gases and use land and water); dislike of the taste or texture of meat; influence of friends, family or social media; cost.

**2.2** Low biological value proteins each lack one or more essential amino acids (1); eating two different LBV proteins together, or during the same day, means the amino acids missing from one are supplied by the other (1); for example, beans on toast, dhal with rice, or hummus with pitta (1).

**2.3** Iron is needed to make haemoglobin in red blood cells, which carries oxygen (1). Teenage girls lose iron through menstruation and are growing fast, so they have a high requirement and are at risk of iron-deficiency anaemia (tiredness, pale skin) (1). Two sources (1 each): lentils, beans, chickpeas, tofu, dark green leafy vegetables such as spinach or kale, fortified breakfast cereals, dried apricots, wholemeal bread, eggs. In place of one source, accept the point that eating vitamin C at the same meal (for example orange juice) improves the absorption of non-haem iron from plant foods.

**2.4** Up to 4 changes, 1 each (4):
- Replace the minced beef with a plant protein such as lentils, soya mince/TVP, beans or mushrooms (not mycoprotein, which usually contains egg).
- Use a vegetable stock cube instead of beef stock.
- Make the mash with a dairy-free spread or olive oil instead of butter.
- Use a plant milk such as soya or oat instead of whole milk.
- Leave out the Cheddar or use a vegan cheese alternative or nutritional yeast.

**2.5** B12 is needed for healthy nerves and to make red blood cells (1); a vegan diet contains no natural source, so vegans risk a deficiency that causes a type of anaemia, tiredness and nerve damage (1); they should eat foods fortified with B12, such as some plant milks, breakfast cereals and yeast extract, or take a supplement (1).

**3.** A café makes its own bread rolls and a cheese and onion quiche with a shortcrust pastry case.

**3.1** Explain how gluten forms when the bread dough is made, and why it matters for the finished rolls. (4)

**3.2** Explain how yeast makes the bread rolls rise. (3)

**3.3** Explain how the egg and milk filling of the quiche sets when it is baked. (3)

**3.4** Complete the table to give two reasons for each problem. Do not repeat your answers.

| Problem | Reasons |
|---|---|
| The quiche filling is watery and curdled | 1. 2. |
| The pastry case has shrunk | 1. 2. |
| The pastry base is soggy | 1. 2. |

(6)

(16)
---
**3.1** Up to 4 from: strong flour contains the proteins glutenin and gliadin (1); when water is added they combine to form gluten (1); kneading stretches and aligns the gluten into long, elastic strands (1); this gluten network traps the carbon dioxide made by the yeast, so the dough can rise (1); in the oven the gluten coagulates, setting the risen structure and giving the rolls their shape and chewy texture (1).

**3.2** Yeast is a living microorganism that feeds on sugars in the warm, moist dough (fermentation) (1); it produces carbon dioxide gas and alcohol (1); the gas bubbles are trapped by the gluten and expand, so the dough rises as it proves and during the first minutes of baking, until the heat kills the yeast (1).

**3.3** Heat denatures the egg proteins, which unfold (1); they then coagulate, bonding together to form a network that traps the liquid (milk) (1); the filling sets at about 60 °C to 70 °C, becoming firm but smooth (1). Credit that egg white sets at a slightly lower temperature than yolk.

**3.4** Two reasons for each, 1 mark each (6):
- Watery and curdled: oven too hot or baked too long, so the proteins over-coagulated, shrank and squeezed out liquid (syneresis); too much milk for the number of eggs; watery vegetables such as onion not cooked first.
- Shrunk: pastry stretched when lining the tin; not rested or chilled before baking, so the gluten relaxed as it heated; too much water added; overhandled, forming too much gluten.
- Soggy base: not baked blind; oven too cool; filling too wet or left standing in the case before baking; tin placed too high in the oven, or a glass or ceramic dish that conducts heat slowly.

**4.** A dairy farm sells milk, and a local business turns some of the milk into yoghurt.

**4.1** Explain the difference between primary and secondary processing. Give an example of each linked to milk. (4)

**4.2** Explain how yoghurt is made from milk. (4)

**4.3** Explain how ultra heat treatment (UHT) affects the sensory qualities and the nutritional value of milk. (4)

**4.4** Some spreads and milks are fortified or modified. Give two advantages and two disadvantages of genetically modified (GM) foods. (4)

(16)
---
**4.1** Primary processing changes the raw material straight after harvest or collection so it can be sold or used, without changing it into a different product (1): for example, pasteurising, homogenising or UHT-treating milk (1). Secondary processing turns a primary-processed ingredient into a different food product (1): for example, making milk into yoghurt, cheese or butter (1).

**4.2** Up to 4 from: the milk is heat-treated (pasteurised) to kill harmful bacteria (1); it is cooled to about 37 °C to 44 °C (1); a starter culture of harmless bacteria, such as Lactobacillus bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus, is added (1); it is kept warm (incubated) for several hours while the bacteria ferment lactose into lactic acid (1); the acid lowers the pH, which makes the milk proteins coagulate, thickening the milk and giving a sharp flavour (1); the yoghurt is then chilled to stop fermentation, and fruit or flavourings may be added (1).

**4.3** Sensory, up to 2: milk is heated to about 135 °C for 1 to 2 seconds (1); this gives a slightly cooked or caramelised flavour and a slightly darker, creamier colour (1). Nutritional, up to 2: some heat-sensitive vitamins, such as vitamin C and some B vitamins (thiamin, B12, folate), are partly destroyed (1); but the protein, calcium and fat content hardly change, so it is still a good source of calcium (1). Credit that it can be stored unopened at ambient temperature for months.

**4.4** Advantages, any two (1 each): crops can be made resistant to pests, disease or drought, so yields increase; less pesticide may be needed; nutrients can be added, for example beta-carotene in Golden Rice; longer shelf life; lower cost; may help food security. Disadvantages, any two: concerns about long-term effects on health are not fully known; cross-pollination with wild plants and loss of biodiversity; seed companies may control farmers' supplies; consumer distrust and ethical objections; some people and religions object to "interfering with nature"; labelling and trade restrictions.

**5.** A family is preparing a cold buffet for 30 guests at a summer garden party. The menu includes cooked chicken drumsticks, egg mayonnaise sandwiches, rice salad, a soft cheese and cooked ham platter, and fresh cream desserts. The food is made in the morning and served from 2 pm to 5 pm.

**5.1** Name the food poisoning bacterium most closely linked to each source:
- (a) the hands, nose and skin of food handlers (1)
- (b) soft cheeses and pâté (1)
- (c) undercooked raw poultry (1)

(3)

**5.2** State the temperature to which any leftover chicken must be reheated. (1)

**5.3**
- Analyse the food safety risks when preparing and serving this buffet.
- Evaluate how the family can control these risks to prevent food poisoning.

(12)

(16)
---
**5.1** (a) Staphylococcus aureus (1); (b) listeria (1); (c) campylobacter, or salmonella (1).

**5.2** 75 °C or above in the centre, and reheat only once (1).

**5.3** Levels of response (12):
- **Level 4 (10–12):** a detailed analysis of several risks. The high-risk foods are named, and the risks are explained through bacterial growth conditions (temperature, time, moisture, protein), cross-contamination and personal hygiene. The evaluation of controls is precise and justified, using accurate FSA temperatures and times, and ends in a clear judgement on which controls matter most.
- **Level 3 (7–9):** a good analysis of a range of risks with some scientific explanation, and relevant controls with correct temperatures, mostly justified.
- **Level 2 (4–6):** some risks identified with limited explanation; the controls are general ("keep it cold", "wash hands") and temperatures are missing or inaccurate.
- **Level 1 (1–3):** a few basic points with little explanation.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (risks):
- Almost every item is high-risk: moist, high in protein and ready to eat, with no further cooking before eating (chicken, egg mayonnaise, ham, soft cheese, fresh cream).
- Cooked rice can carry the spores of bacillus cereus, which survive cooking. If the rice cools slowly, the spores germinate and produce toxins that reheating does not destroy.
- Time and temperature are the main risk. Food made in the morning and left out on a hot afternoon sits in the danger zone (5 °C to 63 °C) for hours. Bacteria can double every 10 to 20 minutes in warm conditions.
- There is cross-contamination from raw chicken to ready-to-eat foods, through boards, knives, hands and fridge shelves.
- Undercooked chicken may still hold campylobacter or salmonella.
- Guests' hands and serving spoons spread bacteria. Handlers may carry staphylococcus aureus.
- Pests such as flies and wasps are drawn to food outdoors.
- A crowded domestic fridge cannot hold 30 portions below 5 °C.
- Soft cheese and ham carry a listeria risk for pregnant or elderly guests.

Indicative content (controls):
- Cook the chicken until the core reaches 75 °C, checked with a clean probe.
- Cool cooked food quickly, within 90 minutes, then chill below 5 °C. Spread the rice in a shallow tray to cool fast and keep it chilled.
- Keep raw and cooked foods apart. Use colour-coded boards and store raw meat on the bottom shelf.
- Wash hands often. Cover cuts with blue plasters, tie hair back and don't prepare food when ill.
- Keep high-risk food in the fridge until serving. Put out small amounts at a time and replenish from the fridge.
- Serve cold food for no more than 4 hours in total, then throw it away. Keep it in the shade, covered with nets or lids, and serve it on ice packs.
- Use serving tongs and spoons.
- Check the use-by dates on the ham and cheese. Label soft cheese and pâté for vulnerable guests.
- Clear leftovers promptly: do not re-chill food that has been out all afternoon.
- Judgement: temperature control over time is the single most important control, because the buffet is served for three hours in summer. It must be backed by the prevention of cross-contamination, since chilling slows bacteria but does not kill them.

**6.** A manufacturer sells a frozen pepperoni pizza. Its front-of-pack label gives the following per 100 g. The traffic-light criteria for 100 g of food are also shown.

| Per 100 g | Pizza | Green (low) | Amber (medium) | Red (high) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fat | 12.0 g | 3.0 g or less | more than 3.0 g up to 17.5 g | more than 17.5 g |
| Saturates | 5.8 g | 1.5 g or less | more than 1.5 g up to 5.0 g | more than 5.0 g |
| Sugars | 3.2 g | 5.0 g or less | more than 5.0 g up to 22.5 g | more than 22.5 g |
| Salt | 1.7 g | 0.3 g or less | more than 0.3 g up to 1.5 g | more than 1.5 g |

**6.1** State the traffic-light colour the pizza would show for each of fat, saturates, sugars and salt. (4)

**6.2** The manufacturer develops a lower-salt pizza and uses a taste panel to compare it with the original. Give four ways to make sure the test is fair and the results are reliable. (4)

**6.3**
- Analyse how food labelling and marketing influence the food choices of teenagers.
- Evaluate whether food labelling helps teenagers to eat a healthier diet.

(8)

(16)
---
**6.1** Fat: amber (1); saturates: red (1); sugars: green (1); salt: red (1).

**6.2** Any four (1 each): code the samples with random letters or numbers; serve equal portion sizes at the same temperature on identical plates; use a quiet area or booths so tasters are not influenced by each other; give water or a plain cracker between samples to clear the palate; use a reasonable number of tasters (for example 10 or more); randomise the order in which samples are tasted; use the same lighting, and red lighting if colour differences must be hidden; give clear instructions and the same scoring sheet (rating or ranking) to all tasters.

**6.3** Levels of response (8):
- **Level 4 (7–8):** a detailed analysis of both labelling and marketing influences, specific to teenagers, and a balanced evaluation that reaches a supported judgement. Terms such as reference intake, traffic-light labelling and point of sale are used accurately.
- **Level 3 (5–6):** a clear analysis of several influences and a reasonable evaluation, which may be less balanced.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** some relevant points with limited development; the evaluation is brief or one-sided.
- **Level 1 (1–2):** simple, list-like points.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- Labelling:
  - Traffic-light colours give a quick visual judgement of fat, saturates, sugars and salt.
  - Percentage reference intakes show how much of a day's needs one portion provides.
  - Allergen information is emphasised in the ingredients list.
  - Claims such as "high protein", "low fat" or "one of your 5 a day" attract health-conscious teenagers.
  - Provenance and ethical logos (Fairtrade, Red Tractor, vegan) appeal to their values.
- Marketing:
  - Social media influencers, celebrity and sports endorsements and online advertising reach teenagers directly.
  - Meal deals and multi-buys offer value to those on limited pocket money.
  - Point-of-sale displays near tills prompt impulse buys.
  - Packaging design, colour and characters make products stand out.
  - Limited-edition flavours and peer pressure play a part.
  - Convenience matters to teenagers with busy lifestyles.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Labels help only if they are read and understood. Many teenagers glance at price and brand, not nutrition.
- Traffic lights are simple, but front-of-pack labelling is voluntary. A product can be red for salt and still be bought.
- Portion sizes on labels may be smaller than what is actually eaten.
- Health claims such as "low fat" can hide high sugar.
- Marketing budgets are far larger than the space on the label, and restrictions on advertising foods high in fat, salt or sugar aim to reduce that influence.
- Food education at school improves label use.
- Judgement: labelling is a useful tool that can support healthier choices when teenagers are taught to use it. On its own it is weaker than price, taste and marketing, so it works best alongside education and restrictions on unhealthy promotions.
