**Section A: multiple choice.** Choose one answer, A, B, C or D, for each question.

**1.1** The temperature danger zone, in which bacteria multiply fastest, is: A — 0 °C to 5 °C; B — 5 °C to 63 °C; C — 63 °C to 75 °C; D — 75 °C to 100 °C. (1)

**1.2** Which vitamin is most easily lost when green vegetables are boiled? A — vitamin A; B — vitamin C; C — vitamin D; D — vitamin K. (1)

**1.3** The golden colour that forms on bread when it is toasted is caused by: A — caramelisation; B — coagulation; C — dextrinisation; D — gelatinisation. (1)

**1.4** Which of these is a high biological value protein from a plant? A — lentils; B — soya beans; C — peanuts; D — wheat. (1)

**1.5** Which food poisoning bacterium is most commonly linked with raw chicken? A — campylobacter; B — listeria; C — staphylococcus aureus; D — bacillus cereus. (1)

(5)
---
1.1 B (1). The danger zone is 5 °C to 63 °C.

1.2 B (1). Vitamin C is water-soluble and damaged by heat, so it leaches into the cooking water.

1.3 C (1). Dextrinisation: dry heat breaks starch down into dextrins, which brown.

1.4 B (1). Soya contains all the essential amino acids; lentils, peanuts and wheat are low biological value.

1.5 A (1). Campylobacter is the commonest cause of bacterial food poisoning in the UK, and raw poultry is its main source.

**Section B.** **2.** A student is making a chicken, leek and mushroom pie with a shortcrust pastry lid, served with boiled carrots. The filling uses cooked chicken and a white sauce.

**2.1** Give two reasons why cooked chicken is classed as a high-risk food. (2)

**2.2** State the core temperature the pie filling must reach, and how the student should check it. (2)

**2.3** Explain how the shortening process gives the pastry a crumbly texture. (4)

**2.4** Explain two ways the carrots could be prepared or cooked to reduce the loss of vitamin C. (4)

**2.5** Give four personal hygiene rules the student should follow when making the pie. (4)

(16)
---
**2.1** Any two (1 each): it is high in protein; it is moist; it is ready to eat and will not be cooked again to kill bacteria (it is only reheated); it supports the growth of pathogenic bacteria such as salmonella.

**2.2** 75 °C or above (1); use a clean, disinfected temperature probe pushed into the centre of the filling (1).

**2.3** Up to 4 from: fat is rubbed into the flour (1); the fat coats the flour particles with a waterproof layer (1); this stops water reaching the gluten-forming proteins (1), so long, stretchy gluten strands cannot develop (1); the cooked pastry has short strands and breaks easily, giving a crumbly, "short" texture (1). Use a fat such as butter or lard that is plastic enough to coat the flour (1).

**2.4** Two ways, each with a reason (2 + 2):
- Steam them or cook them in a small amount of water (1), so less vitamin C dissolves (leaches) into the water (1).
- Cut them just before cooking and into larger pieces (1), so a smaller cut surface is exposed to oxygen and water (1).
- Put them into water that is already boiling and cook for the shortest time (1), because vitamin C is destroyed by heat (1).
- Use the cooking water for gravy or sauce (1), so the dissolved vitamin is eaten (1).

**2.5** Any four (1 each): wash hands with hot soapy water before starting and after handling raw food; tie long hair back or wear a hat or hairnet; wear a clean apron; remove jewellery; cover cuts with a blue waterproof plaster; do not cough or sneeze over food; do not cook when ill with sickness or diarrhoea; keep nails short and free of varnish; do not lick fingers or taste with a spoon then put it back.

**3.** A food technology class is making macaroni cheese with a roux sauce, and a whisked sponge (a Swiss roll).

**3.1** Explain what happens to the starch during gelatinisation as the roux sauce is heated. (4)

**3.2** Complete the table to give two reasons for each problem with the sauce. Do not repeat your answers.

| Problem | Reasons |
|---|---|
| The sauce is lumpy | 1. 2. |
| The sauce is too thin | 1. 2. |
| The sauce tastes burnt | 1. 2. |

(6)

**3.3** Explain how a gas-in-liquid foam forms when eggs and sugar are whisked for the sponge. (4)

**3.4** Give two reasons why the whisked sponge might sink after baking. (2)

(16)
---
**3.1** Up to 4 from: starch granules do not dissolve in cold liquid, they are suspended in it (1); at about 60 °C they begin to absorb liquid and swell (1); as heating continues the granules swell further and some burst, releasing starch molecules into the liquid (1); this thickens the sauce, increasing its viscosity (1); gelatinisation is complete at about 100 °C, when the sauce boils (1); the sauce must be stirred throughout so the granules heat evenly (1).

**3.2** Two reasons for each, 1 mark each (6):
- Lumpy: liquid added too quickly; not stirred continuously; heat too high so starch gelatinised unevenly; roux not cooked for long enough; liquid not blended in gradually off the heat.
- Too thin: too little flour for the amount of liquid; too much liquid; not brought to the boil, so gelatinisation incomplete; not simmered for long enough.
- Burnt taste: heat too high; not stirred, so it stuck to the base of the pan; thin-based pan; left unattended.

**3.3** Up to 4 from: whisking adds mechanical energy that unfolds (denatures) the egg proteins (1); the unfolded proteins trap air bubbles (1), forming a foam of gas bubbles held in liquid (1); sugar helps to stabilise the foam (1); in the oven the heat coagulates the proteins (1), so the foam sets permanently and holds the risen structure (1).

**3.4** Any two (1 each): the oven door opened too early; undercooked so the protein and starch had not set; mixture over-folded or handled roughly, knocking out air; oven too cool; mixture not whisked long enough to form a stable foam.
:::

**4.1** Calculate the cost of one portion of the home-made chilli. Show your working. (3)

**4.2** One portion provides 12 g protein, 8 g fat and 60 g carbohydrate. Calculate the energy in one portion in kcal, and the percentage of that energy that comes from fat. Protein and carbohydrate provide 4 kcal per gram; fat provides 9 kcal per gram. (3)

**4.3** Give four pieces of information that must, by law, appear on the label of the chilled ready meal. (4)

**5.** A 48-year-old office worker has been told by his doctor that he has high blood pressure and raised blood cholesterol. He is overweight and walks very little. This is a typical day's food:

- **Breakfast:** two slices of white toast with butter; coffee with whole milk and two sugars.
- **Mid-morning:** a pork pie.
- **Lunch:** a meal deal of a bacon and egg mayonnaise sandwich, salted crisps and a cola.
- **Evening meal:** a takeaway pepperoni pizza with garlic bread; two chocolate biscuits.
- **Evening:** salted peanuts while watching television.

**5.1** State the maximum amount of salt an adult is advised to eat each day. (1)

**5.2** Give three ways to reduce the amount of salt used when cooking at home. (3)

**5.3** Use the food diary and your knowledge of healthy eating guidelines and nutrition to:
- analyse the suitability of this diet for the office worker
- evaluate how his diet could be improved to meet his nutritional needs.

(12)

(16)
---
**5.1** 6 g a day (1).

**5.2** Any three (1 each): use herbs, spices, garlic, lemon or pepper to add flavour instead; use low-salt or reduced-salt stock cubes or make fresh stock; taste before seasoning and add salt at the table only if needed; use fresh rather than processed or canned ingredients (or canned in water not brine); reduce salt gradually so the taste adapts; use fewer salty ingredients such as bacon, soy sauce or cheese.

**5.3** Levels of response (12):
- **Level 4 (10–12):** a detailed, well-balanced analysis of the diary that refers to specific foods and links them to his conditions, weight and activity. The improvements are specific, realistic and justified. Technical terms such as saturated fat, LDL cholesterol, NSP/fibre and energy balance are used accurately. A clear judgement is reached.
- **Level 3 (7–9):** a good analysis of several foods with some links to health, and relevant improvements that are mostly justified. There may be some imbalance between analysis and evaluation.
- **Level 2 (4–6):** some analysis with limited links to the conditions; the improvements are generic, such as "eat healthier" or "eat less fat", and have little justification.
- **Level 1 (1–3):** a few basic points, often a list of "unhealthy foods", with little or no reference to his needs.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content (analysis):
- Salt is very high: pork pie, bacon, crisps, pizza, pepperoni, garlic bread and salted peanuts. Excess sodium raises blood pressure and strains the heart, raising the risk of stroke and CHD.
- Saturated fat is high: butter, whole milk, pork pie pastry, bacon, mayonnaise, pizza cheese, pepperoni and biscuits. This raises LDL cholesterol, which forms fatty deposits in the arteries (CHD risk).
- Free sugars are high: two sugars in the coffee, cola and biscuits. These give empty calories, adding to weight gain and the risk of type 2 diabetes.
- Energy intake is likely above his needs: the diet is energy-dense, his PAL is low and his BMR falls with age, so there is a positive energy balance and weight gain.
- Fibre is very low: white bread, no wholegrains, no pulses. Low fibre gives no help lowering cholesterol and a risk of constipation.
- There is almost no fruit and vegetables against the target of at least 5 a day, so vitamin C, potassium, antioxidant vitamins and fibre are low.
- Fluid is mainly caffeinated or sugary drinks, not water.

Indicative content (evaluation):
- Swap to wholemeal toast with a spread made from unsaturated fat, or a plant sterol cholesterol-lowering spread. Drink skimmed or semi-skimmed milk and no sugar, or a sweetener.
- Replace the pork pie with fruit or unsalted nuts in small amounts.
- Make a packed lunch on wholemeal bread with lean chicken or tuna and salad and a low-fat dressing, plus fruit and water. This cuts salt, saturated fat and cost.
- Cook the evening meal from scratch, for example grilled fish or a bean chilli with brown rice and vegetables. Use herbs instead of salt and keep takeaways as occasional treats.
- Oily fish provides omega-3 fatty acids, which support heart health.
- Add oats and pulses (soluble fibre helps lower cholesterol).
- Increase activity to raise his PAL, and cut portion sizes to create an energy deficit for gradual weight loss.
- Judgement: the biggest gains come from cutting processed and takeaway foods, which carry most of the salt and saturated fat. Changes must be realistic for a busy office worker to be sustained.

**6.** A school wants to reduce the environmental impact of the food it serves in its canteen.

**6.1** Explain the term "food miles". (2)

**6.2** Give two examples of British fruit or vegetables that are in season in the autumn. (2)

**6.3** Give two advantages and two disadvantages of the canteen buying locally produced food. (4)

**6.4** Food waste is a major environmental problem.
- Analyse the reasons why food is wasted in the home.
- Evaluate the impact that reducing food waste would have on the environment and on food security.

(8)

(16)
---
**6.1** The distance food travels from where it is grown or produced to where it is eaten (1); more miles usually means more fuel burnt and more greenhouse gas emissions (1).

**6.2** Any two (1 each): apples, pears, plums, blackberries, pumpkins or squash, parsnips, leeks, cabbage, beetroot, potatoes, carrots, kale.

**6.3** Advantages (2), any two: fewer food miles, so less transport pollution; fresher food with more flavour and nutrients; supports local farmers and the local economy; clear provenance (easier to know how it was produced); less packaging needed. Disadvantages (2), any two: may cost more; limited range, especially out of season; supply may not be enough for a large canteen; some local food grown in heated greenhouses may have a higher carbon footprint than imported food.

**6.4** Levels of response (8):
- **Level 4 (7–8):** a detailed analysis of several distinct reasons for household waste, and a well-developed evaluation of both environmental and food-security impacts, reaching a reasoned judgement. Terms such as carbon footprint, landfill, methane and food security are used accurately.
- **Level 3 (5–6):** a clear analysis of several reasons, and a sound evaluation that covers the environment and at least touches on food security.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** some reasons given with limited explanation; the evaluation is brief or one-sided.
- **Level 1 (1–2):** a list of simple points.
- **0:** nothing worthy of credit.

Indicative content:
- Reasons: buying too much through poor planning or multi-buy offers; misunderstanding date marks (throwing food away at its best-before date); poor storage, so food spoils; cooking portions that are too large; not using leftovers; fussy eating; confusion over what can be frozen.
- Environmental impact of reduction:
  - Less food rotting in landfill, which produces methane, a powerful greenhouse gas.
  - The water, energy, fertiliser, land and transport used to produce and deliver food are not wasted.
  - Less packaging thrown away.
  - A lower carbon footprint overall.
- Food security:
  - Wasted food could feed people. Reducing waste makes more food available without clearing extra land.
  - It eases pressure from a growing population and from climate-related drought and flooding.
  - Surplus can be redistributed through food banks and apps.
  - Money saved helps households on low incomes afford nutritious food.
- Judgement: cutting waste is one of the cheapest ways to reduce environmental impact. But on its own it does not solve food insecurity, which also depends on income, distribution and climate.
