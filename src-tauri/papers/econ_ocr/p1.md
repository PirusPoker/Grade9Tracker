A Workers become highly skilled at car assembly.
B Suppliers of car parts set up nearby.
C The factory gains economies of scale.
D Unemployment would rise sharply if the factory closed. (1)

B A rise in the price of petrol
C A rise in the cost of batteries

A price elastic.
B unitary.
C price inelastic.
D perfectly elastic. (1)

**7.** Which of the following would cause a decrease in the supply of strawberries?
A A late frost destroys much of the blossom.
B The price of strawberries falls.
C Consumers' incomes rise.
D A new machine picks fruit faster. (1)

**8.** Which of the following is most likely to make the supply of a product price elastic?
A The product takes several years to produce.
B Firms are already working at full capacity.
C The raw materials needed are in short supply.
D Firms hold large stocks of the product in their warehouses. (1)

**9.** At a price of £12, consumers demand 500 units of a product and producers supply 800 units. What will happen in this market?
A Excess demand will cause the price to rise.
B Excess supply will cause the price to fall.
C Excess supply will cause the price to rise.
D The market is already in equilibrium. (1)

**10.** Which of the following is a feature of an oligopoly?
A Many small firms selling identical products
B One firm that supplies the whole market
C A few large firms whose decisions affect each other
D No barriers to entering the market (1)

(10)
---
1. B (1). Capital is a man-made aid to production; money (A) and profit (C) are not factors, and skills (D) are part of labour.

2. D (1). Opportunity cost is the single next best alternative given up — the second choice — not the money earned (A) or every alternative (C).

3. A (1). Software developers sell their labour, a factor of production; B, C and D are product markets.

4. D (1). Over-dependence on one employer is the cost of regional specialisation; A, B and C are benefits.

5. B (1). Dearer petrol makes the substitute (electric bikes) more attractive at every price. A is a movement along the curve; C and D shift supply.

6. C (1). PED = −2% ÷ 8% = −0.25, which is less than 1 in size.

7. A (1). The frost cuts the quantity growers can supply at every price. B is a movement along the supply curve; C shifts demand; D increases supply.

8. D (1). Stocks can be released quickly when the price rises; A, B and C all make supply less responsive.

9. B (1). Quantity supplied (800) exceeds quantity demanded (500): a surplus of 300 units, so sellers cut the price.

10. C (1). Oligopoly is a few large, interdependent firms; A is a competitive market, B is a monopoly and D describes a competitive market.

**21. Extract 1 — Hartley's Bakery**

Mia and Tom Hartley run Hartley's Bakery, a family business in a market town. They employ six bakers and use a large electric oven bought eight years ago. Table 1 shows the bakery's figures for an average week in 2025 (figures invented).

**Table 1 — Hartley's Bakery, average week**

| Items sold | Average price per item (£) | Total costs (£) |
|---|---|---|
| 4,000 | 2.20 | 7,400 |

The old oven is slow and often breaks down, so bakers sometimes stand waiting for it. Mia wants to spend £18,000 of the business's savings on a new oven that bakes 50% more per hour, using the same six bakers. Tom would rather use the £18,000 to open a second shop in the nearby town of Felbridge. He argues that a bigger business could buy flour in bulk at a lower price per bag and share the cost of one delivery van between two shops. Mia worries that running two shops would stretch the family thin, and points out that Felbridge already has three bakeries, including a branch of a national chain.

**21(a)** Explain what is meant by capital as a factor of production. (2)

**21(b)** Using Table 1, calculate Hartley's Bakery's profit in an average week. Show your working. (2)

(4)
---
**21(a)** AO1. One mark for a basic statement, one more for development or an example. Capital is a man-made resource used to produce other goods and services (1), such as machinery, tools, buildings or vehicles — e.g. the bakery's oven (1). Do not credit "money" or "savings" as capital.

**21(b)** AO2. Total revenue = 4,000 × £2.20 = £8,800 (1); profit = £8,800 − £7,400 = £1,400 (1). Two marks for £1,400 with or without working. One mark for a correct method with an arithmetic slip (own figure rule), or for £1,400 without the £ sign.

Two firms, ZipRide and Volta, run all the rental e-scooters in the city of Brackton. Riders unlock a scooter with an app and pay per ride. The city council licenses a fixed number of scooters — 1,200 in total — and will not issue more licences until next year.

- Analysis (benefits): higher pay → care work more attractive relative to supermarkets → supply of care workers rises → shortages ease → more older people get care → better health and wellbeing (social sustainability); care workers' living standards rise; fewer elderly people stuck in hospital beds, freeing NHS capacity; higher pay may reduce staff turnover and training costs.
- Analysis (costs): opportunity cost — £1 billion that cannot be spent on schools, hospitals or roads; higher taxes reduce disposable income and spending; more borrowing adds to the deficit and debt interest; supermarkets and warehouses may have to raise their own pay.
- Evaluation: the benefit depends on whether pay is the main reason for the shortage — if night shifts and stress matter more, extra pay may draw few workers; it depends on how much of the £1 billion reaches workers' pay rather than care-home profits or other costs; with an ageing population the need will keep rising, so a one-off rise may not be enough; the cost is modest if it saves NHS spending elsewhere.
- Judgement, e.g.: the plan is likely to be worth its opportunity cost because the ageing population makes care a growing need and failing to staff it raises costs in the NHS, but only if the money is tied to pay and combined with better working conditions. Any supported judgement can reach Level 3.
