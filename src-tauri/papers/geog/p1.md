**1.** Figure 1 describes the three main types of plate margin.

| Margin | How the plates move | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Constructive | Plates move apart | North American and Eurasian plates at the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (Iceland) |
| Destructive | A denser oceanic plate moves towards a continental plate and sinks beneath it | Nazca and South American plates (Chile) |
| Conservative | Plates slide past each other | Pacific and North American plates at the San Andreas Fault (California) |

(a) What is a natural hazard? (1)

(b) Which one of the following statements is true? (1)
A. Volcanoes are common at conservative plate margins.
B. Earthquakes only happen at destructive plate margins.
C. Most earthquakes and volcanoes occur in narrow belts along plate margins.
D. Volcanoes only occur near plate margins.

(c) Using Figure 1, explain why volcanoes form at destructive plate margins. (3)

(5)
---
(a) A natural process or event (such as an earthquake, volcanic eruption, tropical storm or flood) that has the potential to cause loss of life and damage to property (1). Both the natural event and the threat to people are needed.

(b) C (1). (D is wrong: hotspot volcanoes such as Hawaii form far from plate margins.)

(c) The denser oceanic plate is forced down (subducted) beneath the continental plate (1); as it sinks into the mantle it melts because of heat and friction, forming magma (1); the magma is less dense than the surrounding rock, so it rises through cracks in the crust and erupts at the surface as a volcano (1).

**2.** Figure 2 describes the global atmospheric circulation model and where tropical storms form.

Air rises at the equator, creating a belt of low pressure. It sinks at about 30° north and south of the equator, creating belts of high pressure. Surface winds called the trade winds blow from these high-pressure belts back towards the equator. Tropical storms form over oceans between about 5° and 30° north and south of the equator, mostly in late summer and autumn.

(a) State the type of air pressure found at the equator. (1)

(b) State the minimum sea-surface temperature needed for a tropical storm to form. (1)

(c) Suggest why tropical storms do not form within about 5° of the equator. (1)

(d) Explain why a tropical storm weakens when it moves over land. (2)

(5)
---
(a) Low pressure (1).

(b) About 27 °C (accept 26–27 °C, or 26.5 °C) (1).

(c) The Coriolis effect is too weak near the equator to make the rising air spin (1).

(d) Over land the storm is cut off from its source of energy — warm, moist air rising from the ocean (1); with less condensation there is less heat released to power it, and friction with the land surface slows the winds (1).

Pondweed and algae are eaten by water snails, mayfly nymphs and tadpoles. Water snails and mayfly nymphs are eaten by sticklebacks. Tadpoles are eaten by great diving beetles. Sticklebacks and great diving beetles are eaten by herons. Dead plants and animals are broken down by bacteria and fungi.

(a) Name one producer in Figure 3. (1)

(b) Name one primary consumer in Figure 3. (1)

(c) State the role of the bacteria and fungi in the pond ecosystem. (1)

(6)
---
(a) Pondweed or algae (1).

(b) Water snail, mayfly nymph or tadpole (1).

(c) They are decomposers: they break down dead plants and animals, returning nutrients to the water and soil for plants to use (nutrient cycling) (1).

**4.** (a) Which one of the following is a process of coastal erosion? (1)
A. Longshore drift
B. Attrition
C. Slumping
D. Deposition

(b) Describe two characteristics of destructive waves. (2)

(c) Explain how longshore drift moves sediment along a coastline. (3)

(6)
---
(a) B (1).

(b) Any two of: tall/high and steep (1); frequent — about 11–15 waves a minute (1); a strong backwash that is more powerful than the swash, so they remove material from the beach (1); formed by strong winds blowing over a long fetch or during storms (1).

(c) The prevailing wind makes waves approach the beach at an angle (1); the swash carries sediment up the beach at that same angle (1); the backwash drags it straight back down the beach at right angles to the coast under gravity, so sediment moves along the coast in a zig-zag (1).

**5.** Figure 4 shows sample data from a storm hydrograph for a small river. Rainfall began at hour 0.

| Hours after rainfall began | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 | 12 | 14 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rainfall (mm) | 4 | 12 | 6 | 2 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Discharge (cumecs) | 3 | 4 | 7 | 12 | 18 | 14 | 9 | 6 |

(a) State the peak discharge. (1)

(b) Calculate the lag time. (1)

(c) Explain two physical factors that could make the lag time of this river shorter. (4)

(6)
---
(a) 18 cumecs (1).

(b) Peak rainfall at hour 2, peak discharge at hour 8, so the lag time is 6 hours (1).

(c) Two factors, each explained (2 + 2):
- Steep valley sides (relief) (1) — water runs off quickly over the surface rather than soaking in, so it reaches the river sooner (1).
- Impermeable rock such as clay or granite (1) — little water can infiltrate, so more flows over the surface to the channel (1).
- Ground already saturated or frozen after earlier weather (1) — no more water can soak in, so surface runoff increases (1).
- Little vegetation (1) — less interception, so rain reaches the ground and the river faster (1).
- A small or circular drainage basin (1) — water has a short distance to travel to the river (1).
Human factors such as urbanisation are not physical and gain no credit here.

| | Chile, 27 February 2010 | Nepal, 25 April 2015 |
|---|---|---|
| Magnitude | 8.8 | 7.8 |

(a) Using Figure 5, compare the two earthquakes. (2)

(b) Explain why the Nepal earthquake caused far more deaths than the Chile earthquake, even though it was smaller. (4)

(6)
---
(a) The Chile earthquake was stronger (magnitude 8.8 against 7.8 — about 32 times more energy) (1); but Nepal had far more deaths, about 18 times as many (about 9,000 against about 500) (1). One mark needs a comparative point; the second needs use of the data.

(b) Levels of response.
Level 2 (3–4): clear explanation of two or more developed reasons, linked to wealth and to the places.
Level 1 (1–2): basic, mostly generic reasons with little development.

**7.** (a) Explain one way in which plants are adapted to the climate of tropical rainforests. (2)

(b) For a tropical rainforest you have studied, explain the causes of deforestation. (6)

(8)
---
(a) Drip tips: leaves have pointed tips and a waxy surface (1) so heavy rain runs off quickly, stopping fungi and algae growing and the leaf being damaged (1). Or: buttress roots (1) support very tall trees whose roots are shallow because nutrients are only in the top layer of soil (1). Or: lianas/epiphytes climb or live high on trees (1) to reach sunlight above the dark forest floor (1).

(b) Levels of response.
Level 3 (5–6): detailed explanation of several causes, well developed and clearly linked to a named rainforest with accurate place-specific detail.

Indicative content (Malaysia):

- Logging: valuable hardwoods such as meranti are sold abroad; logging roads open the forest to further clearance.
- Road building: roads such as the Pan-Borneo Highway through Sarawak and Sabah cut through forest and encourage settlement along them.

**8.** Figure 6 describes the climate of a hot desert.

Annual rainfall is usually below 250 mm and very unreliable. Summer daytime temperatures often exceed 40 °C, but night-time temperatures can fall close to freezing. The skies are usually clear.

(a) Using Figure 6, explain why hot deserts have a large daily (diurnal) range of temperature. (2)

(b) Explain how strategies can reduce the risk of desertification on the fringes of hot deserts. Use examples. (6)

(8)
---
(a) With no cloud cover, strong sunshine heats the ground quickly during the day (1); at night there are no clouds to trap the heat, so it is lost quickly and temperatures fall sharply (1).

(b) Levels of response.
Level 3 (5–6): detailed explanation of how two or more strategies reduce the risk, linked to the causes of desertification, with named examples.
Level 2 (3–4): clear explanation of some strategies with some development; some examples.
Level 1 (1–2): basic list or description of strategies with little explanation.
Indicative content:
- Water and soil management: "magic stones" (stone lines laid along the contours, used in Burkina Faso) slow runoff so more rain infiltrates and soil is trapped instead of being washed away; zai planting pits concentrate water and compost around crops; drip irrigation delivers water to the roots with little waste.
- Tree planting: tree roots bind the soil and reduce wind and water erosion; trees give shade, shelter crops and supply fodder and fuelwood. The Great Green Wall, an African Union initiative launched in 2007, aims to restore a belt of land across the Sahel, although progress has been slower than planned.
- Appropriate technology: solar cookers reduce the need to cut trees for fuelwood; simple, cheap methods that local people can build and maintain.
- Grazing and cropping management: controlling herd sizes, rotating grazing and crops, and leaving land fallow reduce overgrazing and over-cultivation, letting vegetation and soil recover.

**9.** Figure 7 describes High Force, a waterfall on the River Tees in the North Pennines.

The river drops about 21 metres over a band of hard, resistant dolerite rock called the Whin Sill. Below the dolerite are softer layers of limestone, sandstone and shale. Downstream of the waterfall the river flows through a narrow, steep-sided gorge.

Using Figure 7, explain the formation of a waterfall and gorge. (4)
---
Levels of response.
Level 2 (3–4): clear, sequenced explanation of the processes, using correct terms, reaching the formation of the gorge.
Level 1 (1–2): basic description with limited explanation of processes or sequence.
Indicative content:
- The river flows over a band of hard rock (the Whin Sill) lying on top of softer rock, which erodes more quickly.
- Hydraulic action and abrasion erode the softer rock beneath, undercutting the hard rock and leaving an overhang.
- Water and rocks swirling at the base erode a deep plunge pool by abrasion.
- The unsupported overhang eventually collapses; the fallen rocks help deepen the plunge pool.
- The process repeats, so the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge downstream, as at High Force.

**10.** Figure 8 describes a change in the atmosphere. The concentration of carbon dioxide was about 280 parts per million (ppm) before the Industrial Revolution. By the 2020s it was over 420 ppm.

(a) Calculate the percentage increase from 280 ppm to 420 ppm. Show your working. (2)

(b) Explain one natural factor that can cause climate change. (2)

(4)
---
(a) Increase = 420 − 280 = 140 ppm (1); 140 ÷ 280 × 100 = 50% (1).

(b) One factor, explained (2):
- Orbital (Milankovitch) changes: the shape of the Earth's orbit and the tilt of its axis change over tens of thousands of years (1), altering how much solar energy reaches the Earth and causing glacial and interglacial periods (1).
- Volcanic activity: large eruptions release ash and sulphur dioxide high into the atmosphere (1), which reflect sunlight and cool the Earth for a year or two (1).
- Solar output: the Sun's energy varies with sunspot activity (1); a period of very few sunspots, the Maunder Minimum (1645–1715), coincided with the cold "Little Ice Age" (1).

::: q 8-9 3.1.1.3b 3.1.1.3a
**11.** Typhoon Haiyan hit the Philippines in November 2013.

"The long-term responses to a tropical storm are more important than the immediate responses."

Using a named example, to what extent do you agree with this statement? (9)
---
Levels of response. (In the real exam this 9-mark question may carry 3 extra marks for spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terms.)
Level 3 (7–9): thorough, detailed understanding of both types of response; applies knowledge coherently to weigh their importance; a well-supported judgement on "to what extent"; accurate, detailed use of a named storm.
Level 2 (4–6): clear understanding of responses; some evaluation, perhaps unbalanced; a partial judgement; some case study detail.
Level 1 (1–3): basic understanding; responses listed or described with little evaluation; no real judgement; little or no place detail.
Indicative content (Typhoon Haiyan):
- Effects that shaped the responses: more than 6,000 deaths; a storm surge of around 5 metres swept through Tacloban; around 4 million people displaced; the airport, roads, power and water supplies were badly damaged.
- Immediate responses: the government had evacuated many people before landfall; international aid (food, water, shelter, medical supplies) arrived; foreign military help, including US forces and the UK's HMS Daring and HMS Illustrious, reached isolated islands; field hospitals were set up; Tacloban airport was reopened for relief flights. These saved lives in the first days, when injuries, thirst and disease were the greatest threats.
- Long-term responses: rebuilding homes, schools and roads to stronger designs; building more storm shelters and evacuation centres; "cash for work" schemes paying people to clear debris, restoring incomes; replanting mangroves as a natural storm-surge barrier; proposed no-build zones along the coast; better warnings — many people had not understood what a "storm surge" meant.
- Evaluation: immediate responses are vital because most deaths happen quickly and a lack of clean water can cause disease; but long-term responses reduce the effects of the next storm, which in the Philippines (hit by many typhoons each year) matters greatly. Long-term responses depend on money and are slower in poorer countries; in an LIC or NEE aid is often needed for both.
- Judgement: for example, that the two are interdependent — immediate responses decide how many survive, long-term responses decide how resilient the country is next time — so the answer depends on the timescale and the country's wealth.

**12.** For a coastal management scheme in the UK that you have studied, evaluate how successful it has been. (6)
---
Levels of response.
Level 3 (5–6): detailed, balanced evaluation of benefits and costs/conflicts, with a clear judgement; accurate detail of a named scheme.
Level 2 (3–4): clear points about the scheme with some evaluation; some place detail.
Level 1 (1–2): basic description of strategies with little evaluation; generic.
Indicative content (Lyme Regis, Dorset):
- Reasons for management: the town is built on unstable cliffs of clay and limestone that are prone to landslides and erosion by the sea; homes, roads and the tourist economy were at risk; the coast is part of the Jurassic Coast World Heritage Site.
- Strategy: Phase II (completed 2007) built a new sea wall and promenade, extended rock armour and used beach nourishment with sand and shingle to widen the beaches, with cliff stabilisation (drainage and pinning); Phase IV (completed 2015) added a sea wall and slope stabilisation east of the town.
- Successes: homes and infrastructure protected; landslide movement slowed; the wider sandy beach attracts more visitors and supports tourist businesses.
- Costs and conflicts: very expensive schemes; beach nourishment needs repeating; the defences look artificial on a World Heritage coast; stopping erosion reduces the exposure of fossils that attract collectors; interrupting natural processes may increase erosion elsewhere.
- Judgement: largely successful at protecting the town for now, but at a high financial and environmental cost that needs ongoing maintenance.
Credit other UK schemes (for example Medmerry managed realignment) marked on their merits.
