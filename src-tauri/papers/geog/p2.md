intro: AQA GCSE style (8035 Paper 2): urban issues and challenges, the changing economic world and resource management (with food). Figures are described in words or tables; data labelled "sample" is made up for skills practice, everything else is real. **6.** (a) Explain why Rio de Janeiro is an important city nationally or internationally. (2)

(b) Using a named example, explain how urban planning is improving the quality of life for the urban poor. (6)

---
(a) One reason, developed (2): for example, it is Brazil's second-largest city and a major port, and the national oil company Petrobras has its headquarters there (1), so it brings jobs and investment to the whole region and country (1); or it is a world-famous tourist destination that hosted the 2014 World Cup final and the 2016 Olympic Games (1), attracting international visitors and investment (1); or it was Brazil's capital until 1960 and remains a major cultural centre (1).

Indicative content (Favela Bairro Project, Rio de Janeiro, begun in 1995):

::: q 8-9 3.2.3.2b
**11.** Pakistan's Indus Basin Irrigation System is one of the largest irrigation systems in the world. Its water is stored by dams such as Tarbela on the Indus and Mangla on the Jhelum, and carried to farmland by a huge network of canals.

"The advantages of large-scale agricultural developments outweigh their disadvantages."

Using a named example, to what extent do you agree with this statement? (9)
---
Levels of response. (In the real exam this 9-mark question may carry 3 extra marks for spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terms.)
Level 3 (7–9): thorough, detailed understanding of advantages and disadvantages; evaluates them coherently, weighing who gains and who loses; a well-supported judgement; accurate detail of a named development.
Level 2 (4–6): clear understanding; some evaluation, perhaps one-sided; a partial judgement; some place detail.
Level 1 (1–3): basic points listed with little evaluation; no real judgement; generic.
Indicative content (Indus Basin Irrigation System, Pakistan):
- Background: the 1960 Indus Waters Treaty, brokered by the World Bank, gave Pakistan the western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) and India the eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej); dams, barrages and link canals were then built, including Mangla and Tarbela.
- Advantages: irrigation allows crops such as wheat, rice, cotton and sugar cane to be grown in a dry climate, feeding a large population and supporting exports such as rice and cotton textiles; the dams generate hydroelectric power and help control floods; farming and related industries provide millions of livelihoods.
- Disadvantages: poor drainage has caused waterlogging and salinisation, reducing yields on large areas of land; silt is filling the reservoirs and reducing their storage; building the dams displaced many people; less water now reaches the Indus Delta, so mangroves have been lost and seawater has moved inland; unlined canals lose water by seepage and evaporation; larger landowners have often gained more than small farmers; the water supply depends on the treaty with India, which India said in 2025 it was holding "in abeyance".
- Judgement: for example, that the system is essential to Pakistan's food supply and economy so the advantages outweigh the disadvantages overall, but only if salinisation, silting and water waste are tackled; or that the environmental and social costs are growing and undermine long-term sustainability.

::: q 8-9 3.2.2d
**12.** Assess how successful strategies have been in reducing the north-south divide in the UK. (6)
---
Levels of response.
Level 3 (5–6): detailed assessment of several strategies with clear evidence of success and limitations, and a supported judgement.
Level 2 (3–4): clear points about some strategies with some assessment; partial judgement.
Level 1 (1–2): basic description of the divide or strategies with little assessment.
Indicative content:
- The divide: on average, incomes, house prices and life expectancy are higher in London and the south-east, and investment has concentrated there; parts of the north were hit hardest by deindustrialisation.
- Northern Powerhouse (from 2014): investment in transport, science and innovation in northern cities; some growth, especially in Manchester, but critics say the funding has been small compared with spending in London.
- HS2: meant to link London with the Midlands and the north, but the leg north of Birmingham to Manchester was cancelled in 2023, reducing the expected benefit to the north.
- Devolution: combined authorities with elected mayors (for example Greater Manchester, first elected in 2017) have more control over transport, housing and skills.
- Relocating organisations: the BBC moved major departments to MediaCityUK in Salford, bringing jobs and related media firms.
- Enterprise Zones and levelling-up funds target investment and tax incentives at poorer areas.
- Judgement: for example, some cities have benefited, but the gap in incomes, productivity and health largely remains, so the strategies have had limited success so far and need long-term funding.
