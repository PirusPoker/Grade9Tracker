**05** A student investigated how the concentration of sodium thiosulfate solution affects the rate of its reaction with dilute hydrochloric acid.

The reaction forms a precipitate of sulfur, so the mixture slowly turns cloudy. The student put a conical flask on a paper cross, added the sodium thiosulfate solution and the acid, and timed how long it took until the cross could no longer be seen.

**Table 2**

| Concentration of sodium thiosulfate (g/dm³) | Time for the cross to disappear (s) |
|---|---|
| 8 | 160 |
| 16 | 81 |
| 24 | 54 |
| 32 | 40 |
| 40 | 32 |

**05.1** Give **two** control variables in this investigation. (2)

**05.2** The student's hypothesis was: "The rate of reaction is directly proportional to the concentration of sodium thiosulfate."

Use data from Table 2 to show whether the results support the hypothesis. (2)

**05.3** Explain, in terms of particles, why increasing the concentration of sodium thiosulfate increases the rate of reaction. (2)

(6)

- volume of hydrochloric acid
- concentration of hydrochloric acid
- temperature (of the solutions)

**11** Ammonia is made by the Haber process.

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)

The forward reaction is exothermic.

**Table 5** shows the percentage yield of ammonia at equilibrium under different conditions.

| Temperature (°C) | Yield at 100 atm (%) | Yield at 200 atm (%) | Yield at 300 atm (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 350 | 36 | 49 | 57 |
| 400 | 25 | 37 | 47 |
| 450 | 16 | 27 | 36 |
| 500 | 11 | 18 | 26 |

**11.1** Give the source of the hydrogen used in the Haber process. (1)

**11.2** In industry, the Haber process is carried out at about 450 °C and 200 atmospheres, with an iron catalyst.

Use Table 5, the equation and your knowledge of rates and equilibrium to explain why these conditions are used. (6)

(7)
---
**11.1** Natural gas / methane (reacted with steam) (1). Do not accept water or air.

**11.2** Levels of response.

Level 3 (5–6 marks): a detailed, coherent explanation of the effects of **both** temperature and pressure on yield **and** rate, using data from Table 5, explaining that the chosen conditions are a compromise involving rate, yield and cost, with the role of the catalyst.

Level 2 (3–4 marks): explains the effect of temperature **or** pressure on yield or rate, with some reasons, and some idea of a compromise; may use data.

Level 1 (1–2 marks): simple statements about the conditions, for example "high pressure gives a high yield", with little explanation.

0 marks: no relevant content.

Indicative content:

Temperature

- the forward reaction is exothermic, so a lower temperature shifts the position of equilibrium to the right, giving a higher yield
- data: at 200 atm the yield is 49% at 350 °C but only 27% at 450 °C
- but at a lower temperature particles collide less often and with less energy, so fewer collisions have the activation energy and the rate is too slow
- 450 °C is a compromise: an acceptable yield produced at a fast enough rate

Pressure

- there are 4 molecules of gas on the left and 2 on the right, so a higher pressure shifts the equilibrium to the right (towards fewer molecules), increasing the yield
- data: at 450 °C the yield rises from 16% at 100 atm to 27% at 200 atm and 36% at 300 atm
- higher pressure also increases the rate, because the gas particles are closer together so collide more often
- but very high pressures are expensive: more energy is needed to compress the gases, and stronger, thicker pipes and vessels are needed; there is a greater safety risk
- 200 atm is a compromise between yield and cost / safety

Catalyst and recycling

- the iron catalyst increases the rate by providing a pathway with a lower activation energy, so a fast rate is reached at a lower temperature
- the catalyst does not change the position of equilibrium / yield
- ammonia is removed by cooling it until it liquefies, and unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled, so a low yield per pass is acceptable and little raw material is wasted
