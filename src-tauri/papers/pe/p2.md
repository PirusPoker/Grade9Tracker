**11.** A coach is teaching a beginner to swim front crawl. Identify TWO types of guidance the coach could use. Justify why each is suitable for a beginner. (4)
---
Two marks per type: identify (1) + justification for a beginner (1), max 4. For example:
- Visual — a demonstration of the arm action (1): the beginner gets a clear picture of what the correct movement looks like without having to process technical language (1).
- Manual/mechanical — a float or the coach supporting the body (1): builds confidence and safety in the water and lets the beginner feel the correct body position (1).
- Verbal — short, simple teaching points (1): only if kept brief, because a beginner is easily overloaded by detail (1).

::: q 8-9 3.2.2.2
**16.** Evaluate the impact of technology on officials in sport. (4)
---
One mark per valid point, max 3 if only positives or only negatives:
- Positive: decisions are more accurate, e.g. video review or ball-tracking, so fewer mistakes (1).
- Positive: officials are less likely to be blamed or abused for a wrong call, which reduces pressure on them (1).
- Positive: communication kit lets officials consult each other during play (1).
- Negative: reviews stop play and can take a long time, breaking the flow of the game (1).
- Negative: the official's authority can be undermined when decisions are overturned in front of the crowd (1).
- Negative: technology is expensive, so it is only available at elite level — inconsistent decisions across levels (1).
- Judgement: overall it improves fairness, provided its use is limited so the game still flows (1).
:::

::: q 8-9 3.2.1.5b
**17.** A gymnastics club gives medals and certificates to its young gymnasts. Evaluate the use of these extrinsic rewards to motivate the gymnasts. (4)
---
One mark per valid point, max 3 if one-sided:
- Tangible rewards give young gymnasts a clear target and can raise effort in the short term (1).
- Winning a medal can itself produce pride and self-satisfaction, feeding intrinsic motivation (1).
- But overuse of extrinsic rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation — they train for the medal, not the enjoyment (1).
- Gymnasts may become reliant on rewards and lose interest when they stop (1).
- Intrinsic motivation is generally more effective and more likely to keep them taking part long-term (1).
- Judgement: use rewards sparingly, alongside praise and a focus on enjoyment and personal improvement (1).
:::

::: q 8-9 3.2.1.2
**18.** Analyse how a coach could use SMART targets to improve the performance of a 15-year-old hurdler. (6)
---
AO1 = 1, AO2 = 2, AO3 = 3.

**Level 3 (5–6):** accurate knowledge of SMART; application to the hurdler is clear and appropriate; analysis is thorough, explaining how each principle leads to better performance; clear, correct terminology.
**Level 2 (3–4):** knowledge is evident; some application to the hurdler; analysis is present for some principles but not developed.
**Level 1 (1–2):** limited knowledge; little application; description rather than analysis.
**0:** nothing creditworthy.

Indicative content:
- AO1: specific, measurable, accepted, realistic, time-bound.
- AO2: specific — improve lead-leg clearance over hurdles 3 to 6; measurable — cut her 100 m hurdles time from 16.2 s to 15.8 s; accepted — agreed between hurdler and coach; realistic — within her ability given her training time; time-bound — by the county championships in eight weeks.
- AO3: a specific target focuses training on the weakness that costs her most time; a measurable target lets progress be checked, and seeing improvement raises motivation; if she has accepted the target she is committed to it and trains harder; a realistic target avoids the demotivation of failure, while still being challenging; a deadline gives urgency and a point to review and set the next target; using performance (not outcome) targets keeps the focus on factors she can control.
:::

::: q 8-9 3.2.2.3a
**19.** Evaluate the use of performance-enhancing drugs by different types of performer. Refer to the effects on the performer and on the sport. (9)
---
AO1 = 2, AO2 = 2, AO3 = 5.

**Level 3 (7–9):** accurate, detailed knowledge of several types of PED; well-chosen application to different performers; evaluation is thorough, weighing advantages against disadvantages for both the performer and the sport, with a reasoned conclusion; clear, coherent, correct terminology.
**Level 2 (4–6):** knowledge is evident but may lack detail; some appropriate application; evaluation is present but uneven or mainly one-sided.
**Level 1 (1–3):** limited knowledge; little or no application; little or no evaluation.
**0:** nothing creditworthy.

Indicative content:
- AO1: stimulants — alertness; narcotic analgesics — mask pain; anabolic agents — build muscle mass; peptide hormones (EPO) and blood doping — raise the red blood cell count and oxygen-carrying capacity; diuretics — rapid weight loss or masking other drugs; beta blockers — lower heart rate, muscle tension and the effects of adrenaline for fine control.
- AO2: endurance cyclist or marathon runner — EPO/blood doping; sprinter or weightlifter — anabolic agents; boxer or jockey — diuretics to make weight; archer, snooker player or shooter — beta blockers; rugby player — narcotic analgesics to play through injury.
- AO3 for the performer: advantages — greater chance of success, fame and wealth, and some believe it creates a level playing field if others cheat; disadvantages — it is cheating and immoral; serious health risks (anabolic agents: liver damage, aggression; EPO and blood doping: thickened blood, embolism, heart attack, infection; beta blockers: nausea, weakness, heart problems); fines, bans and damage to reputation that can end sponsorship.
- AO3 for the sport: its reputation and credibility suffer; spectators and sponsors lose trust in results; clean athletes are cheated of medals; costly testing is needed.
- Judgement: the short-term gains are outweighed by the health risks and the likelihood and consequences of being caught; the effect on the sport's credibility harms everyone in it.
:::
