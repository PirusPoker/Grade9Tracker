**10.** A hockey player recorded her resting heart rate every two weeks during a ten-week continuous training programme.

| Week | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Resting heart rate (bpm) | 72 | 70 | 68 | 66 | 64 | 62 |

(a) Describe the trend shown in the data. (1)

(b) Name the long-term effect of exercise that this data shows. (1)

(c) State whether this data is qualitative or quantitative. Justify your answer. (2)

(d) At week 10 her resting cardiac output is 4.96 litres per minute. Calculate her resting stroke volume in millilitres. Show your working. (2)

(6)

(b) Bradycardia / a lower resting heart rate (1). Accept "the heart gets bigger and stronger (hypertrophy)" only if linked to the lower resting rate.

**11.** Describe the pathway of blood through the heart and lungs, starting when deoxygenated blood enters the right atrium and ending when blood leaves the heart for the body. (4)
---
One mark for each stage, in the correct order (max 4):
- Right atrium into the right ventricle (1).
- Pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs, where gas exchange oxygenates it (1).
- Pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood back to the left atrium (1).
- Into the left ventricle, which ejects it to the body through the aorta (1).

Naming the vessels is required for the middle two marks. Valves are not required.

**13.** A basketball player jumps to shoot. At take-off her ankle plantar flexes.

(a) Identify the class of lever operating at the ankle during take-off. (1)

(b) Describe the positions of the fulcrum, the load and the effort in this lever. (2)

(c) Explain why this lever has a high mechanical advantage. (1)

(d) Later in the session she performs a cartwheel. Identify the plane and the axis of movement in a cartwheel. (2)

(6)
---
(a) Second class lever (1).

(b) The load (body weight) is in the middle (1); the fulcrum is at the ball of the foot/toes and the effort (gastrocnemius pulling on the heel) is at the other end (1).

(c) The effort arm is longer than the load (resistance) arm, so mechanical advantage (effort arm ÷ load arm) is greater than 1 and a large load can be moved with a small effort (1).

(d) Frontal plane (1); sagittal axis (1).

::: q 8-9 3.1.3.2b
**16.** Evaluate the Vertical Jump Test as a way of measuring the fitness of a volleyball player. (4)
---
One mark per valid point, max 3 for one-sided answers:
- It measures power (anaerobic power / explosive strength), which a volleyball player needs to spike and block (1).
- The movement is similar to the jumps used in a match, so it is fairly relevant to the sport (1).
- It is quick, cheap and easy to set up and repeat, so progress can be monitored (1).
- But it tests one maximal jump from standing, not the repeated jumps, run-ups and arm swing of a rally (1).
- It does not replicate competitive conditions — fatigue, timing to the ball, an opponent (1).
- Results depend on technique, marking the wall accurately and motivation, so reliability can be questionable (1).
- Conclusion: a useful but general indicator of power; best combined with sport-specific observation (1).
:::

::: q 8-9 3.1.3.4b
**17.** A marathon runner plans a four-week training camp at high altitude before a race held at sea level. Justify why she might choose altitude training, and give ONE limitation of it. (3)
---
- There is less oxygen in the air at altitude, so the body adapts by making more red blood cells (1).
- Back at sea level she can carry and use more oxygen, improving aerobic endurance and delaying fatigue over 26 miles (1).
- Limitation, any one of: altitude sickness; harder to train at normal intensity, so fitness may drop; the benefit is lost within a few weeks of returning; expensive and time away from home (1).
:::

**18.** Justify the parts of a warm-up that a netball centre should complete before a match. (6)
---

**Level 3 (5–6):** accurate knowledge of the parts of a warm-up; clear, well-chosen application to a netball centre; justification is thorough with valid reasons for each part; clear and focused, with correct terminology.
**Level 2 (3–4):** knowledge is evident but uneven; some application to netball; justification is present but not developed for every part.

**0:** nothing creditworthy.

Indicative content:
- AO1: pulse raiser; stretching; skill-based practices/familiarisation; mental preparation.
- AO2: jogging and side-steps around the court; dynamic stretches for hamstrings, quadriceps, gastrocnemius; passing drills (chest, bounce, shoulder passes), footwork and dodging drills; visualising centre passes and set plays.
- AO3: the pulse raiser gradually raises heart rate and body temperature and increases oxygen to the working muscles, so she can work at full pace from the first whistle; stretching increases range of movement and reduces the risk of strains when she lunges to intercept; skill practices rehearse the exact movements, sharpening passing accuracy and footwork so she does not give away footwork penalties; mental preparation gets her to optimal arousal and focus for a position that covers the whole centre third; the gradual build-up of intensity is itself an injury-prevention measure.

::: q 8-9 3.1.3.3b 3.1.3.4a 3.1.3.4b
**19.** Dan is a rugby prop. He is starting his pre-season training.

Evaluate the use of weight training and circuit training to prepare Dan for the competition season. (9)
---
AO1 = 2, AO2 = 2, AO3 = 5.

**Level 3 (7–9):** accurate, detailed knowledge of both methods; application to a prop in pre-season is mostly appropriate and clear; evaluation is thorough, weighing strengths and limitations of both and reaching a well-reasoned conclusion; clear, coherent, correct terminology.
**Level 2 (4–6):** knowledge of both methods is evident but more detailed for one; some appropriate application; evaluation is present but may be one-sided or unsupported in places.
**Level 1 (1–3):** limited knowledge; application absent or inappropriate; little or no evaluation.
**0:** nothing creditworthy.

Indicative content:
- AO1: weight training uses resistance; strength/power training is high weight, low reps (above 70% of one rep max, about three sets of 4–8); muscular endurance is low weight, high reps (below 70%, about three sets of 12–15); needs correct lifting technique and spotters. Circuit training is a series of stations with a set work:rest ratio; the stations, time and rest can be changed to target different components of fitness.
- AO2: a prop needs maximal and dynamic strength and power to scrummage, lift in the line-out and drive in rucks and mauls, plus muscular endurance to do this repeatedly for 80 minutes; pre-season aims are general/aerobic fitness plus specific fitness needs.
- AO3 for weight training: high-load, low-rep work at over 70% of 1RM builds exactly the strength a scrum needs — highly specific; progressive overload is easy to measure by adding weight; but it does little for cardiovascular endurance; heavy lifting carries injury risk without spotters and technique, and needs equipment/gym access; overtraining risk if rest is ignored.
- AO3 for circuit training: stations can mix scrummage-style pushes, tyre flips, burpees and shuttle runs, improving muscular endurance and aerobic fitness together, which suits the general-fitness aim of pre-season; can be done in a squad with little equipment; motivating and varied, avoiding tedium; but the loads are usually too light to build maximal strength; harder to measure overload precisely.
- Conclusion: a combination is best — heavy weight training for strength and power two or three times a week, with circuits for endurance and general fitness, adjusted as the competition season approaches when the aim shifts to maintaining fitness.
