::: q 4-5 1.1a
**1.** Read the source below. It is the opening of a short story.

**The Keeper's Daughter**

[1] The road ran out before the land did. Beyond the last farm gate there was only a track of packed shingle, and beyond that the causeway, slick and black, with the tide already licking at its edges. Nell stood at the top of it with her bag pulling at her shoulder and counted the waves. One, two, three; then a big one that came further than the rest and hissed back over the stones like something breathing.

[2] The lighthouse waited on its rock half a mile out. In the photograph her mother kept on the mantelpiece it had been white. Now it was the colour of an old tooth, streaked with rust beneath every window, and the lamp at the top was dark.

[3] She should have come earlier. She had known that on the train, and known it again in the bus that smelt of wet dog, and she had told herself both times that there was plenty of light left. There was not plenty of light left. The sky in the west had gone the dull yellow of a healing bruise, and the wind had found the gap between her collar and her neck and was working at it patiently.

[4] Nell stepped onto the causeway.

[5] The stones shifted under her boots. Halfway across, the sea was on both sides of her, close enough to touch, and the noise of it filled her head so that she could no longer hear her own breathing. Spray struck her cheek, cold as a slap. Somewhere behind her a gull was screaming, or laughing; she could not tell which. She did not look back. Her father had always said that the causeway was kind to those who kept walking and cruel to those who stopped, and she had thought it one of his stories, like the one about the bell under the water, until now.

[6] When at last her hand found the iron rail at the foot of the tower, it was so cold that it burned. She hauled herself up the steps, shouldered open the door, and stood in the dark hallway with the water running off her and her heart going like a trapped bird. Above her, faint and far up the spiral stair, a light came on.

Read again paragraphs [1] and [2]. List four things you learn about the lighthouse and the way to it. (4)
---
One mark each for any four: it stands on a rock half a mile out; it was white in the photograph; it is now the colour of an old tooth; it is streaked with rust beneath the windows; its lamp is dark; it is reached by a causeway; the causeway is slick and black; the tide is already covering the edges of the causeway; the way begins as a track of packed shingle beyond the last farm gate (4).

::: q 6-7 1.1b
**4.** Look in detail at paragraph [3].

How does the writer use language here to show Nell's growing unease? You could include the writer's choice of words and phrases, language features and techniques, and sentence forms. (8)
---
Mark against these levels. 7–8: perceptive, detailed analysis of the effects of language, judicious quotation, sophisticated and accurate terminology. 5–6: clear explanation of effects with relevant detail and clear terminology. 3–4: attempts to comment on effect with some relevant detail. 1–2: simple comment, simple references.

Indicative content: the repeated phrase "plenty of light left" is immediately contradicted by the blunt short sentence "There was not plenty of light left", exposing her self-deception and puncturing her reassurance; the parallel structure "She had known that on the train, and known it again in the bus" shows the worry recurring and being pushed away; the sensory detail "smelt of wet dog" grounds the journey in discomfort; the simile "dull yellow of a healing bruise" makes the sky sickly and suggests harm, while "healing" hints that damage has already happened; the wind is personified — it "had found the gap" and "was working at it patiently", making the weather deliberate and predatory, and "patiently" implies it has all the time it needs; the long final sentence with its accumulating clauses mirrors the pressure building on her.
:::

How has the writer structured the text to interest you as a reader? You could write about what the writer focuses your attention on at the beginning, how and why the writer changes this focus as the source develops, and any other structural features that interest you. (8)
---
Mark against these levels. 7–8: perceptive, detailed analysis of structural features and their effects, judicious examples, sophisticated terminology. 5–6: clear explanation of effects with relevant examples. 3–4: attempts to comment on effect with some examples. 1–2: simple comment.

Indicative content: the opening is a wide shot of the landscape and the "road running out", establishing a threshold before we meet Nell; the focus then narrows to Nell counting waves, so we arrive at her as she arrives at the sea; paragraph [2] shifts outward to the lighthouse and backward in time through the photograph, contrasting then (white, lit) and now (decayed, dark) and planting the dark lamp as a question; paragraph [3] delays the crossing with a flashback to the journey, building tension by withholding action; the single-sentence paragraph [4] is the pivot — the decision isolated for emphasis; paragraph [5] narrows again to immediate sensation, sea on "both sides", and the father's saying gives the crossing meaning as a test; the ending shifts the focus upward to the light coming on, answering the dark lamp of paragraph [2] with a circular structure and ending on a cliffhanger that reframes the whole passage — someone is there.

::: q 6-7 2.1b
**6.** Using paragraphs [2], [5] and [6], explain what you can infer about why Nell has come to the lighthouse and how she feels about it. Support each inference with evidence from the text. (6)
---
Award 5–6 for three or more well-supported inferences that go beyond the obvious; 3–4 for two or three inferences with evidence; 1–2 for simple statements with little support.

Indicative content: the lighthouse is connected to her family — her mother keeps its photograph on the mantelpiece and her father told stories about the causeway, suggesting he was the keeper and she has come back to a place from her past; the father is spoken of in the past tense ("had always said"), which suggests he is dead or gone, so the visit may be about loss; she has delayed ("should have come earlier"), suggesting reluctance or dread; the decayed state of the tower ("old tooth", "rust") makes it feel abandoned, so she may expect no one to be there; her heart "like a trapped bird" shows fear mixed with exhaustion, and the light coming on suggests either hope or a new fear, leaving her feelings unresolved.

::: q 8-9 1.1d
**7.** Focus this part of your answer on paragraphs [5] and [6].

A student, having read this section of the text, said: "The writer makes the crossing feel genuinely dangerous — by the end the reader shares Nell's fear."

To what extent do you agree? In your response you could consider your own impressions of the crossing, evaluate how the writer creates a sense of danger, and support your opinions with references to the text. (20)
---
Mark against these levels. 16–20: a perceptive, detailed evaluation with a critical, convincing response to the statement, perceptive analysis of methods and judicious quotation. 11–15: a clear, relevant evaluation with clear explanation of methods and relevant quotation. 6–10: some attempt at evaluation with some comment on methods and some references. 1–5: simple, limited evaluation.

Indicative content: agreement — the sea is "on both sides of her, close enough to touch", removing any escape; the noise "filled her head so that she could no longer hear her own breathing" shows the environment overwhelming her body; the simile "cold as a slap" makes the sea violent and personal; the ambiguity "screaming, or laughing" suggests a hostile, mocking world; the father's saying shifts from "one of his stories" to truth "until now", so the reader's sense of danger grows with Nell's; the short sentence "She did not look back" shows discipline that implies real threat; the rail "so cold that it burned" and her heart "like a trapped bird" carry the fear into the safety of the tower. Alternative view — the danger is largely atmospheric: nothing actually happens to her, we are never told the tide reaches her, and because the narrative stays with Nell the reader may sense she will survive; the fear is partly her own imagination, which the writer signals through "she could not tell which". The strongest answers weigh both and conclude that the writer makes the reader share Nell's fear precisely because it is psychological rather than physical.
:::

::: q 8-9 1.2b 1.2a 1.2c
**8.** A magazine is running a competition for short fiction by young writers.

Either: write the opening of a story about someone returning to a place they have not seen for many years.

Or: write a description of a coastline as a storm arrives.

Your writing will be marked for content and organisation (16 marks) and technical accuracy (8 marks). (24)
---
Content and organisation, mark against these levels. 13–16: convincing and compelling — register matched to purpose and audience, extensive and ambitious vocabulary, sustained crafting of language devices, varied and effective structural features, fluently linked paragraphs, a highly engaging and complex narrative or description. 9–12: clear and consistent — vocabulary chosen for effect, a range of devices, clear structural features, coherent paragraphs with connected ideas. 5–8: some success — some vocabulary for effect, some devices, some paragraphing. 1–4: simple, limited.

Technical accuracy. 7–8: sentence demarcation consistently secure, a wide range of punctuation used with accuracy, a full range of sentence forms for effect, Standard English with secure grammar, accurate spelling including ambitious vocabulary. 5–6: mostly secure demarcation, a range of punctuation, varied sentence forms, mostly accurate spelling. 3–4: some secure demarcation, some punctuation and sentence variety, accurate spelling of common words. 1–2: occasional accuracy.

Check for: a deliberate opening that establishes place or mood rather than starting with waking up; a structural shift (zoom in, flashback, change of pace or a one-line paragraph) used on purpose; at least one extended image or motif that returns; sentence lengths varied for pace; an ending that closes on an image or a turn rather than "and then"; and no more than one or two characters.
