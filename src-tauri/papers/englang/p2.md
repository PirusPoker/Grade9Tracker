::: q 4-5 2.1a
**1.** Read the two sources.

**Source A** — from an online newspaper column, 2023: *Let them walk*

[1] My daughter is nine and she walks to school. There: I have said it, and somewhere a WhatsApp group has just caught fire. It is eleven minutes door to door, one road to cross at a zebra, and a lollipop lady called Bev who has been standing on that corner since before I was born. I timed it. I walked it with her, twice, then stood at the gate and watched her go, and it was the hardest thing I have done as a parent, which tells you something about parenting and nothing at all about the walk.

[2] We have talked ourselves into a strange place. The streets are safer than they were when I was a child — the numbers say so, every year, in every category we are frightened of — and yet the school run has become a convoy of cars idling outside the gates, each one carrying a single child two hundred metres from a door to a door. We are not protecting them from the world. We are protecting them from the experience of being in it.

[3] Here is what my daughter has learned in a term of walking. That the man with the greyhound says good morning and expects one back. That the puddle by the church is deeper than it looks. That if you leave at 8.31 you will have to run. She has learned to look, to judge, to be a small person moving through a big place on her own account. None of this is on the curriculum. All of it is the point.

[4] I am not careless. I know the arguments; I have made most of them myself at two in the morning. But a childhood spent in the back seat is not a safe childhood, only a postponed one, and the bill comes later, at fourteen, at eighteen, when the world arrives all at once and they have never practised.

[5] So: let them walk. Start small. Stand at the gate and hate it. Bev will be there.

**Source B** — a letter to the editor of a provincial newspaper, 1882, signed *A Schoolmaster*

[6] Sir, — I have kept a school in this town for one-and-twenty years, and I write to protest at a practice which grows worse with every season: the sending of children, some of them not eight years old, upon errands through the public streets alone, and at all hours.

[7] I do not speak of the idle boys who loiter at the corners; the constable may attend to those. I speak of decent children, from decent homes, despatched to the chandler or the pawnbroker with a coin in one hand and a basket in the other, to make their way as they can among the carts and the omnibuses and the crowd. Twice this month I have seen a child pulled from beneath a horse's feet. On the second occasion the driver did not stop.

[8] It is said that the streets are the school of life. They are a school, certainly; I have had their scholars in my classroom, and I know what they learn there. They learn to be sharp, and quick, and to trust no one, and they learn it so well that they can be taught little else.

[9] A child's place is at its lessons, at its play, and under its mother's eye. I would have every parent in this town ask himself, before he sends his little one out of the door, whether the saving of his own steps is worth what may be waiting at the crossing. I am, Sir, your obedient servant, A Schoolmaster.

Read again paragraphs [1] and [2] of Source A. Choose four statements that are TRUE.

A. The daughter's walk takes eleven minutes.
B. There are two roads to cross.
C. Bev is a lollipop lady.
D. The writer walked the route with her daughter twice.
E. The writer says the streets are more dangerous than in her childhood.
F. Cars outside the gates often carry only one child.
G. The writer believes cars are the safest way to get to school.
H. The writer found watching her daughter go easy.

(4)
---
A, C, D and F (4; one mark each).

::: q 6-7 2.1b
**4.** Use details from both sources. Write a summary of the differences between the two writers' attitudes to children being out alone. (8)
---
Mark against these levels. 7–8: perceptive synthesis, showing differences with judicious, well-chosen evidence from both and perceptive inferences. 5–6: clear synthesis with relevant evidence and clear inferences. 3–4: some attempt, some evidence, some inference. 1–2: simple, limited.

Indicative content: A sees the walk as valuable education — "None of this is on the curriculum. All of it is the point" — while B sees the streets as a school that teaches the wrong lessons, "to trust no one"; A thinks fear is out of proportion to the risk ("the numbers say so") whereas B has witnessed real danger twice in a month; A blames over-protective parents for keeping children in cars, B blames neglectful parents for sending them out; A wants children to practise independence before adulthood, B believes a child's place is "under its mother's eye"; both, however, are motivated by care for the child and both criticise the convenience of parents.
:::

::: q 6-7 2.1c
**5.** Refer only to Source A, paragraphs [2] to [4]. How does the writer use language to persuade the reader that children should walk to school? (12)
---
Mark against these levels. 10–12: perceptive, detailed analysis of language with judicious quotation and sophisticated terminology. 7–9: clear, relevant explanation of effects with relevant quotation. 4–6: some attempt to comment on effect with some quotation. 1–3: simple comment.

Indicative content: the metaphor "convoy of cars" makes the school run military and absurd, and "idling" puns on wasted engines and wasted people; the contrast "a door to a door" reduces the journey to nothing; the balanced antithesis "We are not protecting them from the world. We are protecting them from the experience of being in it" turns parents' own word against them; the list of small lessons — the greyhound man, the puddle, 8.31 — uses concrete, homely detail to make independence sound ordinary and charming; the triplet "to look, to judge, to be a small person moving through a big place" builds to the key idea; the concessions "I am not careless", "I have made most of them myself at two in the morning" make the writer trustworthy; the extended metaphor of debt — "the bill comes later" — makes protection sound like borrowing; the escalation "at fourteen, at eighteen" projects the reader into the future.
:::

::: q 6-7 2.1c
**6.** Look at paragraph [8] of Source B. Explain how the schoolmaster uses the idea of a "school" to make his point. (4)
---
He takes the common phrase "the streets are the school of life" and agrees with it ironically — "They are a school, certainly" (1); as a real schoolmaster he claims authority: "I have had their scholars in my classroom" (1); the lessons he lists, "sharp, and quick, and to trust no one", are the opposite of what a school should teach (1); the final clause, "they can be taught little else", shows the damage as permanent, so the streets do not educate but ruin (1).
:::

::: q 8-9 2.1d
**7.** Refer to the whole of Source A and the whole of Source B. Compare how the two writers convey their different perspectives on children and the streets. In your answer you could compare their perspectives, the methods they use to convey them, and support your ideas with references to both texts. (16)
---
Mark against these levels. 13–16: a perceptive, detailed comparison of ideas and perspectives, analysing how methods convey them, with judicious quotation from both texts. 9–12: a clear, relevant comparison with clear explanation of methods and relevant quotation. 5–8: some comparison, some comment on method, some quotation. 1–4: simple, limited comparison.

Indicative content: perspectives — A argues for freedom against exaggerated fear; B argues for protection against real, witnessed danger; A's is a personal, confessional viewpoint ("my daughter"), B's a public, moral one ("every parent in this town"). Methods — A's tone is wry and self-mocking (the WhatsApp group "caught fire", "hate it"), using humour to disarm; B is formal and grave, with the letter's conventions ("Sir", "your obedient servant") and the authority of "one-and-twenty years"; A uses statistics and reason ("the numbers say so"), B uses eyewitness anecdote ("Twice this month"), the second, "the driver did not stop", left stark for shock; both use lists of concrete detail, A's charming, B's threatening ("carts and the omnibuses and the crowd"); both end on the parent — A with an imperative and reassurance, B with a rhetorical challenge; context — the 1880s street with horse traffic and child errands against a modern street the writer calls statistically safe, so the same instinct to protect leads to opposite advice.
:::

::: q 8-9 2.2a 2.2b 2.2c
**8.** "Children today are wrapped in cotton wool. They should be given far more freedom, far earlier."

Write an article for a broadsheet newspaper in which you argue your point of view on this statement.

Your writing will be marked for content and organisation (16 marks) and technical accuracy (8 marks). (24)
---
Content and organisation, mark against these levels. 13–16: convincing and compelling — register matched to a broadsheet audience, tone assured and consistent, extensive vocabulary, sustained rhetorical devices, a fluent structure with a strong opening and a considered ending, paragraphs seamlessly linked. 9–12: clear and consistent — an appropriate register, vocabulary chosen for effect, a range of devices, clear structure and linked paragraphs. 5–8: some success — some awareness of audience, some devices, some structure. 1–4: simple, limited.

Technical accuracy. 7–8: consistently secure sentence demarcation, a wide range of punctuation used accurately, a full range of sentence forms for effect, Standard English with secure grammar, accurate spelling including ambitious words. 5–6: mostly secure, a range of punctuation and sentence forms, mostly accurate spelling. 3–4: some accuracy, some variety. 1–2: occasional accuracy.

Check for: a headline and a hook that is not "In this article I will"; a clear line of argument that acknowledges the other side and answers it; evidence or examples (they may be invented but must be plausible); rhetorical questions, triplets, contrast and anecdote used deliberately rather than as a checklist; a range of paragraph lengths, including a short one for emphasis; an ending that returns to the opening or leaves the reader with a challenge.
