**Section A: The British sector of the Western Front, 1914–18: injuries, treatment and the trenches**

**1.** Describe two features of the chain of evacuation for wounded British soldiers on the Western Front. (4)
---
Award 1 mark for each valid feature identified, up to two features, and a second mark for supporting information about each (2 × 2).

For example:
- Stretcher-bearers carried the wounded from the front line to a Regimental Aid Post (1). The Aid Post was close to the front line, often in a dugout or ruined building, where the battalion's Medical Officer gave basic first aid such as cleaning and dressing wounds, but did no surgery (1).
- Casualty Clearing Stations (CCSs) were set up several miles behind the front line (1). They were usually out of range of enemy artillery, so they could carry out surgery such as amputations, and staff sorted (triaged) the wounded according to how urgently they needed treatment (1).
- Seriously wounded men were moved on to base hospitals near the Channel coast, such as those at Étaples, Boulogne and Rouen (1). From there, men who needed long-term treatment were sent home to Britain by hospital ship (1).
- Motor ambulances increasingly replaced horse-drawn wagons (1). They moved men between dressing stations and CCSs more quickly, and some were driven by women volunteers of the First Aid Nursing Yeomanry (FANY) (1).
- Ambulance trains and canal barges carried wounded men from CCSs to base hospitals (1). Barges were slow but gave a smooth ride, so they were used for men with serious wounds such as fractures (1).

Accept other valid features, for example the Dressing Stations run by Field Ambulances between the Aid Post and the CCS. A second mark needs genuine supporting detail, not a repeat of the feature.

**2. (a)** Study Sources A and B.

**Source A:** a summary of a diary entry written by a stretcher-bearer of the Royal Army Medical Corps (RAMC) during the Battle of the Somme, July 1916. (Summary, not a quotation.) The writer describes carrying wounded men back along narrow, zig-zag communication trenches that were crowded with troops moving up to the front. At each sharp corner the bearers had to lift the stretcher high above their heads to get it round, and in places they had to climb out of the trench, where they came under fire. One journey to the Regimental Aid Post took several hours. He writes that many wounded men were still lying in no man's land waiting to be collected, and that he and the other bearers had worked for two days without proper rest.

**Source B:** a British official photograph taken on 1 August 1917, during the Battle of Passchendaele (the Third Battle of Ypres), near Boesinghe. It shows a team of stretcher-bearers struggling to carry one wounded man through deep, waterlogged mud. The men are sunk in mud to their knees. The ground around them is churned up by shellfire and waterlogged.

How useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into the problems of moving wounded soldiers from the front line?

Explain your answer, using Sources A and B and your knowledge of the historical context. (8)

The sources are analysed to support the reasoning, and contextual knowledge is used in the process of interpreting them.

Indicative content:
- **Source A, content:** useful because it shows several specific problems: narrow, winding trenches that made stretchers hard to carry; trenches blocked by troops moving forward; bearers exposed to fire; long delays (several hours) before men reached treatment; the sheer number of wounded left in no man's land; and exhaustion among bearers.
- **Source A, provenance:** written by a stretcher-bearer at the time, so it is first-hand evidence from someone who did the job; a private diary was not meant for publication and is unlikely to have been censored, so it may be frank about the delays. But it describes one man's experience in one part of the line in one battle, so it cannot show whether these problems were typical of the whole war.
- **Source B, content:** useful because it shows that mud could make evacuation extremely slow and exhausting: a whole team of bearers was needed for one casualty, and the destroyed, flooded landscape offered no cover or firm path.
- **Source B, provenance:** an official photograph, taken by one of the small number of photographers the army allowed at the front; official images were controlled and used to show the war to people at home, so the photographer might avoid showing the dead or the worst suffering. That this image nevertheless shows great hardship suggests it is a genuine record. However, a photograph captures one moment in one place, and may have been posed.
- **Context:** on 1 July 1916, the first day of the Somme, the British suffered nearly 60,000 casualties, far more than the medical services had planned for, so many wounded waited hours or days to be collected. Communication trenches were built with traverses (zig-zags) to limit the effect of shell blasts and enfilading fire, which made carrying stretchers difficult. At Passchendaele in 1917 heavy rain and shelling destroyed the drainage of the low-lying Ypres salient, and teams of four to six bearers were often needed for each stretcher, with journeys taking hours. Evacuation was mainly done at night, when bearers were less visible to snipers.
- **Judgement:** e.g. Source A is more useful for the range of problems in moving men along the trenches; Source B is more useful for the effect of ground conditions later in the war; together they show that problems came both from the design of the trenches and from terrain and weather, although neither shows how the RAMC tried to solve them (for example with light railways and motor ambulances further back).

**Section B: Medicine in Britain, c1250–present**

**3.** Explain one way in which people's reactions to the plague were similar in the fourteenth and seventeenth centuries. (4)
---
Levels of response (AO2: 2 marks; AO1: 2 marks).
Level 2 (3–4): features of the period are analysed to explain a similarity; specific information about both periods supports the comparison, showing good knowledge and understanding.
Level 1 (1–2): a simple or generalised comment about a similarity; generalised information showing limited knowledge.
An answer with no comparison (describing only one period) cannot go beyond Level 1.

Indicative content:
- **Religious reactions:** in both periods many people believed the plague was a punishment from God, so they turned to prayer. During the Black Death of 1348–49 people prayed, fasted and went on pilgrimages, and flagellants whipped themselves in public to show God they repented. In 1665 the Church of England ordered special prayers and fast days, and people still prayed to be spared.
- **Miasma and cleansing the air:** in both periods people believed in miasma (bad air), so they tried to purify the air. In 1348–49 people carried posies of sweet-smelling herbs and flowers and lit fires. In 1665 people again carried posies and smoked tobacco or burned herbs, and the Lord Mayor of London ordered bonfires in the streets to cleanse the air.
- **Avoiding the sick:** in both periods those who could leave fled. In 1348–49 people abandoned towns and avoided the sick; in 1665 wealthy Londoners, including the king and his court, left London, and victims' houses were shut up with a red cross painted on the door.
Credit other valid similarities, for example continued use of traditional remedies and the reliance on local authorities to take action.

**4.** Explain why there were improvements in surgery in the years c1840–c1900.

You may use the following in your answer:
- chloroform
- carbolic acid

You must also use information of your own. (12)
---
Levels of response (AO2: 6 marks; AO1: 6 marks).
Level 4 (10–12): an analytical explanation directed consistently at the question, with a line of reasoning that is coherent, sustained and logically structured; accurate, relevant information precisely selected, showing wide-ranging knowledge and understanding.
Level 3 (7–9): an explanation showing some analysis, mainly directed at the question, with a generally sustained line of reasoning, although some passages may lack coherence; accurate, relevant information showing good knowledge.
Level 2 (4–6): an explanation with limited analysis and implicit or unsustained links to the question; some development and organisation; accurate information showing some knowledge.
Level 1 (1–3): a simple or generalised answer lacking development and organisation; limited knowledge.
Maximum 8 marks for answers that do not address three or more aspects of content. No access to Level 4 for answers that do not go beyond the two stimulus points. The middle mark in each level can be reached by stronger performance in either AO1 or AO2.

Indicative content:
- **Anaesthetics solved the problem of pain.** Before the 1840s operations were done on conscious patients, so surgeons had to work very fast. Ether was used in a public operation in Boston, USA, in 1846, and by Robert Liston in London that December, although it irritated the lungs and was highly flammable. In 1847 James Simpson in Edinburgh discovered that chloroform worked as an anaesthetic. Patients no longer struggled, so surgeons could take more time and attempt more complex operations.
- **Changing attitudes helped anaesthetics spread.** Some doctors distrusted chloroform after early deaths from overdoses, and some believed pain was God-given or useful. John Snow developed an inhaler to control the dose, and in 1853 he gave chloroform to Queen Victoria at the birth of Prince Leopold, which made it respectable.
- **Germ theory explained infection.** Louis Pasteur published his germ theory in 1861, showing that microbes caused decay. Joseph Lister, a surgeon in Glasgow, read Pasteur's work and realised that germs could cause infection in wounds. This shows the importance of science and the spread of ideas through publications.
- **Antiseptic surgery reduced infection.** From 1865 Lister used carbolic acid on wounds, dressings and instruments, and later a carbolic spray in the operating theatre; he published his results in The Lancet in 1867. Deaths among his amputation patients fell sharply, from about 45 per cent to about 15 per cent in his own figures.
- **Koch's work supported Lister.** In 1878 Robert Koch identified the bacterium that caused septicaemia (blood poisoning), giving scientific proof that germs caused infection, which helped persuade doubters.
- **Aseptic surgery.** By the 1890s surgeons were moving from killing germs to keeping them out altogether: instruments were sterilised with steam, and surgeons wore gowns, masks and rubber gloves, which were first used in surgery in the USA in 1890. Operating theatres were designed to be kept very clean.
- **Nursing and hospital hygiene.** Florence Nightingale's emphasis on cleanliness and fresh air, set out in Notes on Nursing (1859), and the training of nurses at the Nightingale School at St Thomas' Hospital from 1860, made hospitals cleaner and safer places for patients recovering from surgery.
- **Analysis:** strong answers show how factors linked (anaesthetics allowed longer operations, which at first raised the death rate from infection — the "black period" — and so made the need for antiseptics urgent), and note that progress was limited: blood loss remained a serious problem until blood groups were discovered in 1901.

Indicative content that supports the statement (science and technology):
- **Magic bullets:** in 1909 Paul Ehrlich's team, with Sahachiro Hata, found that Salvarsan 606, an arsenic compound, killed the bacterium that causes syphilis without harming the body — the first chemical cure aimed at a specific microbe. In 1932 Gerhard Domagk found that Prontosil cured streptococcal blood poisoning, leading to a family of sulphonamide drugs.
- **Penicillin:** Alexander Fleming noticed in 1928 that mould killed bacteria, but lacked the means to purify it. Howard Florey and Ernst Chain at Oxford purified penicillin and tested it on mice in 1940 and on their first human patient, a policeman, in 1941. Deep-tank fermentation technology allowed mass production.
- **Diagnosis and genetics:** better microscopes, blood tests, X-rays, CT and MRI scanners allow earlier, more accurate diagnosis. Crick and Watson's discovery of the structure of DNA (1953), building on Rosalind Franklin's X-ray images, and the Human Genome Project (completed 2003) opened the way to gene-based diagnosis and treatment.
- **High-tech treatment:** radiotherapy and chemotherapy for cancer; keyhole surgery; organ transplants (the first successful UK kidney transplant was in 1960); dialysis machines; targeted drugs.

Indicative content that counters the statement (other factors):
- **Government:** governments, above all the US government, paid for the mass production of penicillin during the Second World War, so enough was produced for Allied casualties by D-Day in 1944. The NHS, created in 1948 by Aneurin Bevan's Labour government, made treatment free at the point of use, so people could actually get the new drugs and hospital care. NICE decides which treatments the NHS can afford.
- **War:** the Second World War created the urgency and the money that turned Fleming's discovery into a mass-produced drug.
- **Individuals:** science did not act on its own; progress depended on the determination of individuals such as Ehrlich (who tested hundreds of compounds), Fleming, Florey and Chain.
- **Chance:** Fleming's discovery of penicillin was partly the result of a contaminated culture plate left while he was on holiday.
- **Attitudes and communication:** public acceptance of new treatments, and the sharing of research through journals, also mattered.
- **Evaluation:** strong answers set a clear criterion, for example that science and technology made new treatments possible, while government and war made them available on a large scale, so science was necessary but not sufficient. Credit any justified judgement.
