**Section A: Superpower relations and the Cold War, 1941–91**

**1.** Explain two consequences of the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962). (8)
---
Mark each consequence separately (2 × 4 marks). Levels of response for each (AO2: 2; AO1: 2).
Level 2 (3–4): features of the period are analysed to explain a consequence; specific information supports the explanation, showing good knowledge and understanding.
Level 1 (1–2): a simple or generalised comment about a consequence; generalised information showing limited knowledge.

Indicative content:
- **A hotline was set up.** The crisis showed how dangerous slow communication had been — messages between Kennedy and Khrushchev took hours to arrive and translate — so in 1963 a direct communications link between the White House and the Kremlin was agreed, allowing the leaders to talk quickly in a future crisis.
- **Arms control treaties.** Having come close to nuclear war, both sides moved to limit the arms race: the Limited Test Ban Treaty (August 1963) banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in outer space and under water; it was followed by the Outer Space Treaty (1967) and the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968).
- **Cuba remained communist and the USA promised not to invade it.** Khrushchev removed the missiles in return for a public US pledge not to invade Cuba, so Castro's communist government survived just 90 miles from Florida.
- **US missiles were withdrawn from Turkey.** As part of a secret deal, the USA removed its Jupiter missiles from Turkey in 1963, which reduced the direct threat to the USSR, although this was not made public at the time.
- **Khrushchev's position was weakened.** The withdrawal looked like a climbdown to many in the Soviet leadership and to China; this contributed to his removal from power in October 1964.
Credit other valid consequences, for example the growth of a period of improved relations that led towards détente.

**2.** Write a narrative account analysing the key events of the Berlin Crisis in the years 1948–49.

You may use the following in your answer:
- the introduction of the Deutschmark (1948)
- the Berlin Airlift

You must also use information of your own. (8)
---
Levels of response (AO2: 4 marks; AO1: 4 marks).
Level 3 (6–8): a narrative that organises material into a clear sequence of events leading to an outcome; it analyses the links between events and is coherent and logically structured; accurate, relevant information showing good knowledge of the key features of the events. No access to Level 3 for answers that do not address three or more aspects of content.
Level 2 (3–5): a narrative showing some organisation into a sequence leading to an outcome, with some analysis of the links between events, though some passages may lack coherence; accurate information showing some knowledge.
Level 1 (1–2): a simple or generalised narrative with limited analysis and organisation; limited knowledge.
The middle mark in Levels 2 and 3 can be reached by stronger performance in either AO1 or AO2.

Indicative content (the links between events are what earn the marks):
- **Background:** after 1945 Germany and Berlin were divided into four zones. The USA and Britain wanted a strong German economy to resist communism, so they merged their zones into Bizonia in January 1947; France's zone was later merged with it (Trizonia). Stalin saw this as a threat and wanted a weak Germany.
- **The Deutschmark (June 1948):** the Western powers introduced a new currency in their zones and West Berlin. Stalin feared that a prosperous West Berlin, deep inside the Soviet zone, would show up the weakness of communism and draw East Germans west.
- **The blockade (24 June 1948):** in response, Stalin cut all road, rail and canal links to West Berlin, aiming to starve the Western powers out of the city.
- **The Berlin Airlift:** rather than fight their way in or abandon the city, the USA and Britain supplied West Berlin by air from late June 1948. Planes landed around the clock; on the "Easter Parade" of April 1949 nearly 13,000 tons were flown in in a single day. The airlift showed that the West would not give up West Berlin, and Stalin could not shoot the planes down without starting a war.
- **The outcome:** Stalin lifted the blockade on 12 May 1949 — a propaganda defeat for the USSR. The crisis led directly to the formation of NATO (April 1949) and to the permanent division of Germany: the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) was created in May 1949 and the German Democratic Republic (East Germany) in October 1949.

**3.** Explain the importance of the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1979) for relations between the USA and the Soviet Union. (8)
---
Levels of response (AO2: 4 marks; AO1: 4 marks).
Level 3 (6–8): an explanation showing analysis of importance, with a coherent, logically structured line of reasoning; accurate, relevant information showing good knowledge of the period.
Level 2 (3–5): an explanation showing an attempt to analyse importance, with some reasoning, though some passages may lack coherence; accurate information showing some knowledge.
Level 1 (1–2): a simple or generalised answer with limited development; limited knowledge.
The middle mark in Levels 2 and 3 can be reached by stronger performance in either AO1 or AO2.

Indicative content:
- **It ended détente.** Soviet troops entered Afghanistan in December 1979 to prop up a communist government. The USA saw this as Soviet expansion towards the oil of the Persian Gulf, so the cooperation of the 1970s (SALT 1 in 1972, the Helsinki Agreements in 1975) collapsed.
- **SALT 2 was never ratified.** The treaty had been signed by Carter and Brezhnev in June 1979, but in January 1980 Carter asked the US Senate to delay considering it, so the next stage of arms control stalled.
- **The Carter Doctrine (January 1980):** Carter announced that the USA would use military force to defend its interests in the Persian Gulf, and the USA began to give aid, including weapons, to the Afghan mujahideen fighting the Soviets, turning Afghanistan into a proxy conflict.
- **Economic sanctions and the Olympic boycotts:** the USA restricted grain sales and trade with the USSR, and led a boycott of the 1980 Moscow Olympics that more than 60 countries joined; the USSR and most of its allies boycotted the 1984 Los Angeles Olympics in return.
- **It led to the "Second Cold War".** The collapse of détente helped Ronald Reagan win the 1980 US election; he greatly increased US military spending and in 1983 called the USSR an "evil empire", so tension rose sharply in the early 1980s.
- **Longer-term importance:** the costly war (Soviet troops did not leave until 1989) strained the Soviet economy and helped persuade Gorbachev that the USSR could not afford the arms race — which in turn helped bring the Cold War to an end.

**5.** Explain why support for the Nazi Party grew in the years 1929–32.

You may use the following in your answer:
- the Wall Street Crash (1929)
- propaganda

You must also use information of your own. (12)
---
Levels of response (AO2: 6 marks; AO1: 6 marks).
Level 4 (10–12): an analytical explanation directed consistently at the question, with a line of reasoning that is coherent, sustained and logically structured; accurate, relevant information precisely selected, showing wide-ranging knowledge and understanding.
Level 3 (7–9): an explanation showing some analysis, mainly directed at the question, with a generally sustained line of reasoning, although some passages may lack coherence; accurate, relevant information showing good knowledge.
Level 2 (4–6): an explanation with limited analysis and implicit or unsustained links to the question; some development and organisation; accurate information showing some knowledge.
Level 1 (1–3): a simple or generalised answer lacking development and organisation; limited knowledge.
Maximum 8 marks for answers that do not address three or more aspects of content. No access to Level 4 for answers that do not go beyond the two stimulus points. The middle mark in each level can be reached by stronger performance in either AO1 or AO2.

Indicative content:
- **The Wall Street Crash and the Depression.** After the Crash of October 1929 American banks recalled the loans that had propped up the German economy since the Dawes Plan of 1924. Businesses closed and unemployment rose to about 6 million by 1932. Desperate people turned to extreme parties that promised "work and bread"; Nazi seats in the Reichstag rose from 12 (1928) to 107 (September 1930) and 230 (July 1932), making them the largest party.
- **The failure of the Weimar governments.** Chancellor Brüning cut spending and unemployment benefits and raised taxes, earning the nickname "the hunger chancellor", and increasingly governed by presidential decree under Article 48. Democracy seemed unable to solve the crisis, so voters looked for strong leadership.
- **Propaganda.** Joseph Goebbels ran a modern, well-organised propaganda campaign: posters, rallies, leaflets and films, with simple messages aimed at different groups (jobs for workers, protection for farmers, order for the middle classes). Blaming the Treaty of Versailles, the "November criminals" and Jewish people offered easy scapegoats. The Nazis had earlier gained publicity through the 1929 campaign against the Young Plan alongside Alfred Hugenberg, whose newspapers reached millions.
- **Hitler's appeal.** Hitler was a powerful speaker who presented himself as a strong leader above party squabbles. In the 1932 presidential election he flew from city to city to address huge audiences ("Hitler over Germany"), winning about 37 per cent of the vote in the second round against Hindenburg.
- **Fear of communism.** Communist (KPD) support also grew with unemployment, winning 100 seats in November 1932. Businessmen, the middle classes and farmers feared a communist revolution; many industrialists gave the Nazis money, and voters saw the Nazis as the best barrier to communism.
- **The SA.** The SA, with about 400,000 members by 1932, gave an impression of discipline and strength, protected Nazi meetings and broke up opponents' meetings, and offered unemployed young men food, uniforms and a sense of purpose.
- **Analysis:** strong answers show how the factors linked — the Depression created the conditions, while propaganda, Hitler and the SA turned discontent into Nazi votes.

**6. (a)** Study Interpretations 1 and 2. They give different views about the lives of German workers under the Nazis in the years 1933–39.

**Interpretation 1:** a summary of the view of historians who argue that ordinary workers gained from Nazi rule. (Summary, not a quotation.) These historians stress that the Nazis ended mass unemployment, which had reached about 6 million in 1932: public works schemes such as the autobahns, the National Labour Service and rearmament created jobs, and workers regained security and self-respect. Organisations such as Strength Through Joy gave workers cheap holidays, cruises, theatre trips and sports, while Beauty of Labour improved canteens, washrooms and working conditions. On this view, many workers felt that life was better than it had been in the Depression.

**Interpretation 2:** a summary of the view of historians who argue that workers lost more than they gained. (Summary, not a quotation.) These historians stress that the trade unions were abolished in May 1933 and replaced by the German Labour Front, so workers could no longer bargain or strike. Wages were kept low while working hours grew longer and food prices rose. Unemployment figures were made to look better by not counting groups such as women and Jewish people who were pushed out of work. On this view, the leisure schemes were a way of controlling workers rather than rewarding them, and the promised "people's car" was never delivered to the workers who saved for it.

What is the main difference between these views?

Explain your answer, using details from both interpretations. (4)
---
Levels of response (AO4: 4 marks).
Level 2 (3–4): the interpretations are analysed and a key difference of view is identified and supported from both of them.
Level 1 (1–2): limited analysis shown by extracting or paraphrasing some content, but only differences of surface detail are given, or a difference of view is asserted without direct support.

Indicative content:
- A main difference is that Interpretation 1 argues that Nazi policies improved workers' lives, especially by giving them jobs and leisure (for example the fall from about 6 million unemployed, and Strength Through Joy holidays), whereas Interpretation 2 argues that workers were worse off because they lost their rights and freedoms (for example the abolition of trade unions, low wages, longer hours) and that the benefits were exaggerated or used to control them.
- A difference only of detail (for example "Interpretation 1 mentions holidays and Interpretation 2 mentions trade unions") stays in Level 1.

**6. (b)** Suggest one reason why Interpretations 1 and 2 (in question 6(a)) give different views about the lives of German workers under the Nazis in the years 1933–39. (4)
---
Levels of response (AO4: 4 marks).
Level 2 (3–4): an explanation of a reason for the difference is given, analysing the interpretations; the explanation is substantiated effectively.
Level 1 (1–2): a simple valid explanation with limited analysis; support is simple, undeveloped comment or selected details with only implied links to the explanation.

Indicative content (any one approach, explained and supported, can reach Level 2):
- **Different emphasis:** Interpretation 1 focuses on material benefits — jobs, holidays, better workplaces — while Interpretation 2 focuses on the loss of political and economic freedom, such as the right to join a union or strike. Historians who weigh jobs and security most heavily will reach a more positive view.
- **Different evidence:** historians may have drawn on different sources. One might rely on official statistics showing unemployment falling and on Strength Through Joy records of millions of holidays taken; the other on wage and price figures, working-hours data or reports from opponents of the regime, which show workers' dissatisfaction.
- **Different workers:** the experience of workers varied — skilled workers in armaments factories did better than many others — so historians studying different groups or different years (for example the early recovery after 1933 compared with the pressure of rearmament after 1936) may reach different conclusions.
- **Different view of Nazi aims:** Interpretation 1 takes the schemes at face value as rewards; Interpretation 2 sees them as tools to control workers and keep them loyal.

**6. (c)** How far do you agree with Interpretation 2 (in question 6(a)) about the lives of German workers under the Nazis in the years 1933–39?

Explain your answer, using both interpretations and your knowledge of the historical context.

(16 marks, plus 4 marks for spelling, punctuation, grammar and the use of specialist terminology) (20)
---
Levels of response (AO4: 16 marks), plus SPaG (4).
Level 4 (13–16): an explained evaluation reviewing the alternative views in coming to a substantiated judgement; precise analysis of the interpretations, showing how the differences of view are conveyed, used to support the evaluation; relevant contextual knowledge precisely selected; an overall judgement that is justified, with a coherent, sustained and logically structured line of reasoning.
Level 3 (9–12): an explained evaluation agreeing or disagreeing with the interpretation; good analysis of the interpretations, showing the difference of view and using it to support the evaluation; relevant contextual knowledge used directly; an overall judgement with some justification and a generally sustained line of reasoning.
Level 2 (5–8): valid evaluative comment agreeing with or countering the interpretation; some analysis of details from both interpretations; some relevant contextual knowledge linked to the evaluation; an overall judgement whose justification is insecure or undeveloped.
Level 1 (1–4): simple valid comment agreeing with or countering the interpretation; limited analysis of one interpretation by simple paraphrase or quotation; generalised contextual knowledge.
No credit for contextual knowledge that is not linked to evaluating the interpretations. An answer that analyses the interpretations but does not evaluate them gets only 1 mark in Level 1, or the bottom mark of a higher level.

SPaG (4): High (4) — consistently accurate spelling and punctuation, effective control of grammar, a wide range of specialist terms. Intermediate (2–3) — considerable accuracy, general control of meaning, a good range of specialist terms. Threshold (1) — reasonable accuracy, some control, a limited range of specialist terms. 0 — nothing written, irrelevant, or errors that severely hinder meaning.

Indicative content. The interpretation to be evaluated argues that workers lost more than they gained under the Nazis.

Points that support Interpretation 2:
- Trade unions were banned on 2 May 1933 and replaced by the German Labour Front (DAF) under Robert Ley; strikes were illegal and the DAF set wages and hours, so workers had no independent voice.
- Real wages rose little, if at all, in the 1930s, while the average working week grew longer and food prices rose; rearmament meant shortages of consumer goods.
- "Invisible unemployment": women pushed out of many jobs and Jewish people dismissed were not counted, and men in the National Labour Service (compulsory for six months for men aged 18–25 from 1935) and, from 1935, conscripts in the army were off the register — so the figures overstate the recovery.
- Strength Through Joy and Beauty of Labour were run by the DAF and were used to keep workers loyal and productive; workers were often expected to carry out Beauty of Labour improvements themselves, unpaid.
- The Volkswagen savings scheme: hundreds of thousands of workers paid in weekly instalments for a "people's car", but the factory switched to military production and no worker received a car.

Points that support Interpretation 1 and so challenge Interpretation 2:
- Unemployment fell from about 6 million in early 1933 to under half a million by 1938 — the most important change for many families after the Depression; public works (such as the autobahns), the RAD and rearmament created jobs.
- Strength Through Joy offered many workers holidays, cruises, concerts and sports they had never been able to afford; millions took part.
- Beauty of Labour did improve many workplaces, with better lighting, canteens and washrooms.
- Many workers valued security and order after the chaos of the Depression, and there was little open opposition from workers after 1933.

Judgement: strong answers use a clear criterion — for example, that jobs and security mattered most to workers after 1929, so Interpretation 2 is only partly convincing; or that the loss of rights, frozen wages and longer hours show workers paid a high price for work, so Interpretation 2 is more convincing. Credit any judgement that is justified with precise evidence from both interpretations and own knowledge.
