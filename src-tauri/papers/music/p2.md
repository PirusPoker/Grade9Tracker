intro: Eduqas GCSE Music C660QS Component 3 style: eight 12-mark questions, two on each area of study, with the set works in Q1 (Badinerie) and Q7 (Africa). The real exam is played from a recording, each extract heard three or four times; this mock drills the knowledge behind it, so every extract is described in words and short passages of music are written as note names and rhythm values. Q4 contains the rhythm dictation and Q8 the 10-mark extended answer, which you should mark honestly against the bands. The questions ramp from grade 4-5 to grade 8-9.

**5. Area of study 3 - unprepared extract.** A composer is commissioned to write music for a documentary scene showing a city waking up at dawn. The music begins with a marimba repeating a short pattern. Other instruments enter one at a time, each with its own repeating pattern. Two pianos play the same pattern, but one gradually drifts ahead of the other. A melody grows by adding one note each time it repeats. A synthesiser adds a soft, sustained background sound with a lot of reverb. Over several minutes the music becomes gradually louder and busier.

(a) Name the style of music described. (1)

(b) Name the technique of adding instruments one at a time, each with its own pattern. (1)

(c) Name the technique heard in the two pianos. (1)

(d) Give the term for a melody that grows by adding one note each time. (1)

(e) Explain how music technology can be used to enhance the sonority of film music. (2)

(f) Explain why the techniques described suit a scene of a city waking up. (2)

(g) Explain two things a composer must take into account when responding to a film commission. (2)

(h) Explain two ways in which performers recording this music could interpret it differently. (2)

(12)
---
(a) Minimalism (1).

(b) Layering (1).

(c) Phasing / phase shifting (1).

(d) Additive melody (accept additive) (1).

(e) Any two (1 each): synthesised sounds give colours an orchestra cannot, such as pads or electronic textures; effects such as reverb or echo make the sound seem larger or more distant; parts can be layered or multi-tracked to thicken the texture; samples and loops add real-world or unusual sounds; mixing and panning control balance and space.

(f) Any two (1 each): the slow, gradual change mirrors the slow build of activity as the city wakes; repetition suggests routine, machines and traffic; layers added one at a time suggest more people appearing; the gradual crescendo matches growing light and noise.

(g) Any two (1 each): timing the music exactly to the scene's length and cuts; matching the mood and pace of the images; the director's or producer's wishes; the budget and number of players available; the intended audience; fitting under dialogue or narration without covering it.

(h) Any two (1 each): tempo; dynamics and balance; articulation (for example more detached or more legato); tone colour or choice of synthesiser sound; the amount of rubato or how strictly they keep to a click track.

**8. Area of study 4 - unprepared extract.** A British bhangra track that fuses Punjabi folk music with pop and electronic dance music. Here is what you would hear, described in everyday words:

- A fast beat of four in a bar, with each beat divided into three.
- A large double-headed drum played with two sticks, repeating a lively pattern.
- A high, plucked instrument with a single string plays a short, repeating figure.
- A male singer with a small range; his phrases often fall at the end and are decorated, and some notes lie between the notes of the western scale.
- Shouts of "Hoi!" between phrases.
- A synthesised bass line recorded once and repeated throughout, and an electronic drum kick on every beat.
- In verse 2 a rapper performs in English.
- A verse-chorus structure. The harmony uses only two chords.
- Short string phrases taken from an old film soundtrack; the large drum is moved from one speaker to the other.

(a) Name the large double-headed drum. (1)

(b) Name the rhythm pattern typical of bhangra drumming. (1)

(c) Explain how this track fuses bhangra with western pop and dance music. Use musical vocabulary. In your answer refer to the musical elements and how the two styles are combined. (10)

(12)
---
(a) Dhol (1). Do not accept tabla.

(b) Chaal (1).

(c) Mark with the bands; the indicative content is not exhaustive. Credit a term only when it is linked to one of the styles or to the fusion.

Indicative content. Bhangra features: fast dance tempo; 4/4 with each beat divided into three (a swung, triplet or compound feel); the dhol playing the chaal rhythm; tumbi riff or ostinato (high, plucked, single string); narrow vocal range with falling phrases, ornamentation or melisma and microtones; shouts of "Hoi!"; drone-like, static harmony with only two chords; Punjabi lyrics. Western pop and dance features: looped, synthesised bass line; drum machine or four-to-the-floor kick; rap in English; verse-chorus structure with a repeated hook; samples of string phrases; panning and other studio effects; amplified, produced sound. Fusion: the two layers sit together rhythmically because the dhol and the kick drum share the beat; traditional acoustic timbres are combined with electronic ones; English rap alongside Punjabi singing reaches a wider British audience; the track keeps bhangra's dance function while using pop structure and technology.

- 9-10: a perceptive answer explaining many features in detail, clearly attributing them to each style and evaluating how successfully they are combined. Specialist vocabulary is used accurately throughout.
- 7-8: a secure answer explaining a good range of features from both styles with some comment on how they combine. Vocabulary mostly accurate.
- 5-6: an inconsistent answer identifying several features, with some attribution to a style. Some accurate vocabulary.
- 3-4: a limited answer naming a few features with little explanation. Limited accurate vocabulary.
- 1-2: a very limited answer, mostly everyday description.
- 0: nothing of value.

An answer that discusses only one of the two styles cannot go above 6.
